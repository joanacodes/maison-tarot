Pointing someone elsewhere is one of the kindest things a tarot reader can do, as long as it doesn't feel like a door closing. The person should leave with more than they came with: a better question, someone to call, and the sense that they were heard. Here's how I do it, in three steps.

## When a question isn't one for the cards {#when}

Some subjects stay off my table: anything medical, pregnancy and fertility included; death; a court case still running; a money decision; curses; and anyone who isn't there to speak for themselves. The [full list is public](/disclaimers/#refusals), so nobody discovers it halfway through a reading.

The reason is arithmetic more than principle: my notebook shows about one miss in every ten readings. On a question about a new job, a miss costs a shrug. On a question about a scan, a pregnancy or a trial, a miss can make someone wait when they should act, or panic when they could rest. I've written more about [what a miss costs on a medical question](/blog/health-pregnancy-death-tarot/).

Two other moments call for a boundary even when the topic is fine. Someone in acute distress, for whom a reading is the wrong tool that day. And someone who keeps coming back with the same question: I usually decline then; [what endless repeat readings do to someone](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/) explains why.

**A boundary isn't a verdict on the person; it's a fact about the tool.**

## Three steps to point someone elsewhere kindly {#three-steps}

1. Acknowledge why they're asking. It's nearly always worry, love or fear, and it deserves to be named first: "You're asking because you're frightened for her. That makes sense."
2. Say gently why the cards aren't the right tool. Not "I can't", but "here's why": "I don't read on health. About one reading in ten misses, and on this question a miss could hurt you."
3. Offer what helps instead: [a kinder question to ask](/blog/questions-to-avoid-asking-tarot/), what a reading can hold meanwhile, or the professional who can actually answer.

Say someone sits down, tells you nothing, and at the end of the reading asks whether her father's operation will go well. Step one: "Of course you want to know. You love him." Step two: "That's a question for his surgeon, and I'd hate for a card to sit where their answer should." Step three: "What I can look at with you is how you're holding up while you wait. Shall we?"

Most people breathe out at that point. Often they didn't want a forecast so much as somewhere to put the worry.

## Who to point them to {#who}

The door depends on the question. Here's the map I use.

| The question | The right door | What a reading can hold meanwhile |
|---|---|---|
| A symptom, a diagnosis, an operation | A doctor | How the person is carrying the waiting |
| Pregnancy, fertility | A doctor | What they hope for, and what they fear |
| A court case, a dispute, a contract | A lawyer | What keeps them steady while it runs |
| An investment, a debt, a big purchase | A financial adviser | What “value” means to them, beyond the sum |
| A weight carried for a long time | A therapist | Naming it, not holding it |
| “Does he love me?”, “What is she planning?” | That person, in a conversation | What the person in front of me feels |

Curses get their own line. Someone who believes they're cursed is usually worn out by a run of bad luck, and that tiredness is real. The kind answer is that nobody should charge them to lift anything, because [that offer is the scam itself](/blog/lift-the-curse-scam/), and that a run of hard months is worth talking through with someone trained to help.

## Sentences to keep ready {#sentences}

Good words rarely arrive in the moment if you haven't found them before. A few I use, or close to it:

- "That one's for a lawyer. They have the file; I'd only have a picture. Shall we look at what's helping you keep going?"
- "I can hear how much you want to know what he feels. I'd rather read what you feel. That's the part I can see, and the part you can act on."
- "We looked at this together last week. I don't think another reading will give you what you're after. What would you do if no card had ever been drawn?"

Leave out the sentences that shut a door in someone's face: "I can't help you", "that's not allowed", "you shouldn't have asked". Nobody should feel told off for asking. Keep your face soft, too: people read a reader's face more closely than any card.

## When the door is a therapist's {#therapist}

Sometimes what comes up in a reading is psychological, sometimes more, and the right door is a therapist's. [Where my role ends](/disclaimers/#therapy) is written down for exactly that reason.

You don't diagnose. You're not qualified to, and neither am I. You say once, kindly, what you noticed and where it could go: "This sounds like it's been with you a long time. It deserves more than a reading can give it. Is there someone you could talk to?" No lecture, no pressure, no extra card to soften it.

My notes say it in one line: tarot should never stand in for therapy, and never become something either of you can't do without. A reading can give someone a picture for what they feel. It can't hold a crisis, and [I've written about where that door is](/blog/tarot-and-mental-health/) at more length.

## Before your next reading {#next-step}

Pick one subject you know will come up — a sick parent, a lawsuit, an ex — and write your three steps for it. Say them out loud once, alone, before you need them. The first time you use them for real, they'll sound like you rather than like a rule.

And when a sentence comes out harder than you meant, there's a way back: [what to say when a reading lands too hard](/blog/reading-lands-too-hard/).
