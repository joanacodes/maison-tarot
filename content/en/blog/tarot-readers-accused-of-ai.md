Most accusations of AI land on a text, not on a reading: a written reading sent by email, a long caption, a pick-a-card script. Someone recognises a shape they’ve seen a machine produce and concludes that a machine produced it. The shape is real, the conclusion doesn’t always follow, and a reader can do a lot to make the question unnecessary.

## What the accusation looks like {#what-it-looks-like}

The accusation is the claim that a reading, or a post, was generated by an AI tool rather than written by the reader who signed it. It turns up in reviews, in comments and in private messages, and almost always about something written.

Picture a written reading that arrives the day after you paid for it. Three cards, three paragraphs, one per card, each opening with what the card “represents”. A fourth paragraph ties them into a story and ends on a gentle reminder to look after yourself. You read it twice and feel nothing in particular. Then you think you’ve seen it somewhere before.

That feeling is what the accusation runs on. It isn’t irrational, and it deserves to be taken seriously from both sides of the table.

## Why people suspect it {#why-people-suspect}

Because the reading in that picture has the shape AI tools produce, hedges included (“this may suggest”, “you might be feeling”). I took that shape apart when I explained [how I test an AI reading](/blog/is-ai-tarot-accurate/), and once you’ve seen a few, you notice the stitching.

The catch is that machines didn’t invent this shape. It’s the shape of a card-meanings book, and plenty of human readers learned from the same books and write in the same order. A reader who recites meanings card by card sounds like a model because the model learned from readers who recite.

Then there’s the money. Someone who pays for a written reading is paying for a person’s attention. Finding a template where they expected attention feels like being sold the wrong thing, and they’re right to mind.

**The accusation reads a style, and a style can’t tell you who wrote it.**

## What it can prove, and what it can’t {#what-it-proves}

It can’t prove that a machine wrote the reading. A person can write like a template, and a generated text can be edited until it sounds like a person. Pasting a reading into a detector gives you a score, not the name of whoever sat at the table.

What the suspicion does point at, reliably, is a generic reading. That’s usually the real complaint. If every sentence would fit anyone, it hardly matters who typed it: you weren’t read. The same test works in both cases. Strike out every sentence that could be said to a stranger and see what’s left. If nothing is, you’re looking at [the Barnum effect](/blog/barnum-effect-tarot/), whether a human or a model produced it.

An honest limit: I can’t tell you whether a given reader used AI, and from the text alone, neither can you. What you can judge is whether the reading said something about you that you didn’t supply.

## How a reader can be transparent {#being-transparent}

If you read for others, the best answer to the accusation comes before it. A few habits make it hard to raise:

1. Say what you use, in writing, before anyone pays. One sentence on your page will do: what your tools are for, and what they never touch. Where I put that line for myself is in [should tarot readers use AI?](/blog/should-tarot-readers-use-ai/)
2. Show the spread. Send a photo of the actual cards on your actual table with the reading.
3. Read live when you can. A voice or video call is hard to outsource, and the person can watch you look at the cards.
4. Ask for silence. When the person tells you nothing first, [anything that lands couldn’t have come from them](/blog/the-silence-rule/), or from a prompt they typed.
5. Keep your misses in. A model never writes “that part didn’t land”. A reader who does sounds like a person, because only a person does it.
6. Read the picture, not the book. Your own words for what’s on a card are hard to mistake for anyone else’s.

The reader’s version: a woman blindfolded, her arms bound, eight swords planted in the ground around her, none of them touching her, her legs free and the way in front of her open. She’s a prisoner of herself while every tool to get out is within reach; the blindfold can come off. The first sentence could have come from anywhere. ## If you’ve been accused {#if-accused}

Answer the question, not the tone. Someone who suspects a template usually wants to know they were looked at. Show them how: the photo of the spread, how you work, what you used and what you didn’t. Offer a short live call if you can, so they can see you read.

Then reread the reading yourself, as if a stranger had written it. If it could have gone to anyone, the accusation got the culprit wrong but the problem right, and that’s worth knowing.
