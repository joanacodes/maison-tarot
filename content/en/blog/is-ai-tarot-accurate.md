People ask me whether ChatGPT can read tarot the way they'd ask whether a calculator can do sums. It can produce a reading. Whether that reading is *accurate* is a different question, and the way to answer it is the same way I answer it for myself: not by how it feels, but by counting.

## What an AI reading actually is {#what-it-is}

You type "I drew the Three of Swords, the Hermit and the Star, what does it mean?" and you get three paragraphs: one per card, in order, each explaining the card's traditional meaning, then a fourth paragraph that stitches them into a narrative with the word "journey" in it. It's fluent. It's warm. It ends with a gentle suggestion about self-care.

Look at what it did. It explained each card in isolation — Three of Swords means heartbreak, Hermit means withdrawal, Star means hope — and then arranged the meanings into a story. That's a card-meanings book with a nicer voice. What it didn't do is the thing that makes a reading a reading: look at *you*.

## Three things it structurally can't do {#cant-do}

**It can't be told nothing.** The whole discipline of an honest reading, for me, is that the person gives me no context, so that whatever lands couldn't have come from them. With a language model, *you* type the situation. Everything it says back is your own words, reorganised. The silence rule, which is the one check that separates reading from reflection, is impossible to apply.

**It never misses.** Ask a reader ten things and a good one will say "that's a miss" at least once. A model never will. It qualifies — "this may suggest", "you might be feeling" — until nothing it says can be wrong. A reading that can't be wrong can't be right either; it's just weather.

**It reads cards, not the spread.** The most useful thing in a real reading is what happens between cards: the 1 next to the 10, the Cups crowding out everything else, the one reversed card in a row of upright ones. Models handle the units and lose the relations. Readers who've tested them say the same thing in the same words: every card explained separately, no layering, no link to what already came up.

## The test, in ten minutes {#the-test}

1. Draw three cards yourself, physically. Don't tell the model anything about your situation — not a question, not a mood. Just the card names.
2. Ask for a reading. Copy the answer into a document.
3. Strike every sentence that would fit anyone. "You may be processing a loss." "This is a time for reflection." Be ruthless; you'll strike most of it.
4. Of what's left, mark what's actually true of you today, and what isn't.
5. Do the same with a human reader who's been told nothing, and compare the two notebooks.

I've done versions of this. What survives from the model is a competent description of what's on the cards — genuinely useful if you're learning what the pictures show — and nothing that couldn't have been said to any stranger. What survives from a good human reading, one time in several, is a sentence about you that you didn't supply. That difference is the whole thing.

## Where it's actually good {#where-its-good}

I'll be fair. As a card-meanings reference, a model is better than most books: you can ask "why is there a dog on the Fool?" and get an answer instantly. As a way to practise describing a card — colour, number, element, what the figure is doing — it's a patient partner. As a tool for a reader who's stuck on a symbol, it's fine. [Where I draw the line for readers is here](/blog/should-tarot-readers-use-ai/).

What it isn't is a reader. And the danger isn't that it's bad; it's that it's confident, available at three in the morning, never says no to a question, and never says "that's a miss". Those are the four properties I'd list for a [reader to avoid](/blog/how-to-spot-a-fake-tarot-reader/), and the machine has all of them by design.

## If you want to use it anyway {#if-anyway}

Use it to learn the pictures, not to be read. Ask it what's on a card, never what a card means for you. And if you find yourself typing your situation in before you ask — which you will, because it's the natural thing to do — notice that you've just told the reader everything, and that whatever comes back was already yours.
