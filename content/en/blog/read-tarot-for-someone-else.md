Reading for someone else starts before the cards come out, with a yes. Ask permission, ask the person to tell you nothing, and choose words they can carry home. With friends, the same rules apply, plus one more: the friendship comes first and the reading second.

## Ask permission, every time {#ask-permission}

Before you read anyone, ask: “Would you like me to read for you?” Then take “no” or “not today” as a complete answer.

It sounds obvious, and it’s skipped all the time, especially with people we know well. Reading someone without their consent is a bit like going through their bedroom while they’re out. You wouldn’t want it done to you, whatever the intention.

There’s a practical side too. Someone who doesn’t know they’re being read isn’t open to it, so you catch half a sentence of them and build a whole statement on it. That’s my experience and the hypothesis I draw from it; nothing about it has been proven. It’s enough for me to always ask.

Consent also covers who the reading is about. **Read for the person in front of you, about their own life.** Their partner, their boss and their mother haven’t said yes; I explain how I handle those questions in [reading tarot about someone else](/blog/reading-tarot-about-someone-else/).

## Ask them to tell you nothing {#tell-me-nothing}

Once they’ve said yes, ask for silence: no question, no context, no “it’s about work”. You draw, you say what you see, and at the end they tell you what held and what didn’t. That’s [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/), and it’s what lets you check yourself afterwards.

With friends it matters twice as much, because you already know their story. Say your friend is planning a wedding and draws the Four of Wands.

{{< cards "wa04" >}}

A garland held up on four wands, two figures raising bouquets, a castle behind. You’ll say “wedding” before you’ve looked. Maybe it is — four is foundations, building a home. But you’d have found the wedding in almost any card.

So describe the drawing first, out loud, before you go anywhere near what you know. If the wedding is in the card, it will still be there after the description. If it isn’t, you’ve learned how much you were reading your friend rather than the cards.

## Choose words they can carry {#kind-words}

Sometimes you’ll feel you shouldn’t say something, or should say it differently: the person isn’t ready, or they’re scared, or wary of the whole thing. Adapt your attitude and your words. **Leave a thing unsaid if you need to, but never say something you don’t see.**

Every card has a plus and a minus, and a heavy card is still a drawing with more than one thing in it. Say your friend draws the Ten of Swords and the Five of Cups.

{{< cards "sw10 cu05" >}}

“Betrayal and grief” is one way to put it, and it lands like a slap. The other way reads the same drawings to the end: a figure on the ground under ten swords, and behind him the yellow of a dawn coming up; a cloaked figure staring at three spilled cups, with two still standing behind. “Something ended hard, and you’ve been looking at what was lost. More is still standing than you’re letting yourself see.” Same cards, same truth, and something to hold.

If you’re very empathic, you may start to feel the other person’s sadness as if it were yours. Listen to the emotion without letting it take over; keep your own. There’s more on difficult sentences in [how to share bad news in a reading](/blog/bad-news-tarot-reading/).

## Reading for friends without losing one {#friends}

Friends are where almost everyone starts, and it’s a good place. It’s also where I saw what makes teaching worth it: friends and family trying a card, saying something true, and being shocked by what they’d said. A few rules keep it a pleasure:

1. A friend can say “stop” at any point, and you stop without asking why.
2. Don’t use a reading to say what you haven’t dared say as a friend. If you think their partner treats them badly, that’s a conversation, not a card.
3. What’s said at the table stays at the table.
4. Don’t become their daily oracle. If the same friend asks about the same thing every week, say so kindly and suggest a pause.
5. The subjects I leave aside with strangers, I leave aside with friends: health, pregnancy, death, ongoing court cases, money decisions. The [list, with my reasons](/disclaimers/#refusals), is public.

When a friend asks about one of those, it’s almost always worry talking. Acknowledge it, say gently that the cards aren’t the right tool for that, and offer a kinder question: “What would help you get through this week?” is one the cards can hold.

## When you see nothing, or it’s too much {#nothing-or-too-much}

Some days you’ll look at the cards and get nothing. It can be your block — too much pressure to get it right, which is common with people you love — or theirs, if part of them is afraid of what they’ll hear. Put the cards down, talk, reassure them. Then try again, or don’t.

If your friend is in real distress, it isn’t a reading night. Make tea, listen, and if it’s more than a bad week, help them find someone trained for it. That isn’t the cards failing; it’s the right door.

Afterwards you may feel very tired. Interpreting takes energy. Rest.

## Your next step {#next-step}

This week, ask one friend if they’d like a reading. If they say yes, ask them to tell you nothing, draw three cards, describe before you interpret, and let them speak at the end. If something doesn’t land, [call it a miss and move on](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/). Then close the reading properly, as I describe in [how to end a tarot reading well](/blog/how-to-end-a-tarot-reading/) — by handing the decision back. I read; you decide.
