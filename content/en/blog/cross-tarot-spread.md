The cross spread is a five-card layout: four cards in a cross — what’s for you, what’s against you, the way through, where it’s heading — and a fifth in the centre that sums them up. In France it’s often the first spread people learn. In English it’s barely known, overshadowed by the Celtic Cross, which shares the shape and little else.

It’s a lovely spread for one kind of question, and too much for most others. Here’s both.

## How to lay out the cross spread {#layout}

Shuffle, then lay four cards in this order, and leave the centre empty for now.

| Card | Place | What it reads |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Left | What works for you: what you bring, what helps |
| 2 | Right | What works against you: the obstacle, the resistance |
| 3 | Top | The way through: what to do, or what will help |
| 4 | Bottom | Where it’s heading if nothing changes |
| 5 | Centre | The synthesis: the whole situation in one card |

Books disagree on cards 3 and 4. Some read the top card as the outcome and the bottom as the advice; some call the top card “the judgement”. None of them is the true version. **Pick your positions before you draw, and keep them.** Swapping meanings after you’ve seen the cards is how you end up reading what you hoped for.

The “where it’s heading” card deserves a light hand. I read the present far more than the future; card 4 describes the direction things take if nothing changes, and something usually does.

## The fifth card: adding up the four {#fifth-card}

What makes the French cross different is its centre. Traditionally you don’t draw the fifth card. You calculate it: add the numbers of the four cards, and if the total is over 22, add its digits together. The result is a major arcanum, and that card goes in the middle.

The tradition comes from the Tarot de Marseille, usually read with the 22 majors alone. Two things change if you use the Rider-Waite-Smith deck, as I do:

- Strength and Justice swap places. In the Marseille deck, Justice is 8 and Strength 11; in the Rider-Waite-Smith, Strength is 8 and Justice 11. Use the numbers printed on your own deck.
- The minor arcana join in. Aces count as 1 and the pip cards at face value. Court cards have no number, so either leave them out of the sum or draw the centre card instead.

I like this centre for one reason: it isn’t an extra card drawn to rescue a confusing spread. It comes out of the four already on the table. That’s very different from the [“one more card” habit](/blog/the-clarifier-card/), which usually muddies a reading. If the sum feels too mechanical, read the reduced number as a number instead, with the [numerology lens](/library/numerology/).

## A cross, read through as an example {#example}

Say someone sits down, tells you nothing, and the cross comes out like this: the Ace of Wands on the left, the Seven of Cups on the right, the Hermit at the top and the Six of Wands at the bottom. The sum is 1 + 7 + 9 + 6 = 23. That’s over 22, so 2 + 3 = 5: the Hierophant goes in the centre.

Lenses first. For you: an Ace, a 1, the initiator — and Fire, the spark of something new. Against you: a Seven of Cups, seven cups floating in a cloud, each holding a different prize; too many choices, Water, emotions pulling in every direction. The way through: the Hermit, grey cloak and lantern, alone on a height; step back and look by your own light. Where it’s heading: a Six, the number where the machine runs and success comes — a rider crowned with laurel.

Two Fire cards around a Water obstacle: the energy is there, and what slows it down is feeling — wanting every option at once. The Earth of the Hermit and the Hierophant brings it back to the ground.

The centre: the [Hierophant](/library/tarot-cards/the-hierophant/), a teacher before two kneeling figures, and a 5, the number of trying, learning and accepting mistakes.

Out loud, it might sound like this: “You’ve got the spark of a new project, and too many versions of it. Take some time alone to pick one. It can go well. And the centre says you’ll do it better inside a structure — a course, a mentor, a method — and that you’re allowed to get things wrong while you learn. Does that land?”

Then you let them answer, and you note what they confirm and what they don’t.

## When one card is enough {#one-card}

The cross is built for a situation with two sides: something helping, something resisting. If you can’t name what’s against you, you probably don’t need it.

For a question about today, one card does the job. For learning, one card is better: start with one a day, describe what you see, then move to three, then more. Five cards give a beginner five places to hide and five chances to tell a story they already wanted to hear. The [one-card daily pull](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/) teaches you more than a spread you only half follow.

Its for-and-against shape also makes the cross tempting for a court case or a money decision. I understand the pull — those are the questions that keep people up at night — but I don’t read on them; I’d rather lay the cross on how you’re carrying the situation than on how it ends.

## Where to go from here {#next-step}
