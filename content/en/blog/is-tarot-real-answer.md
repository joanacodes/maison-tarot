When someone asks "is this real?", the calm answer is the honest one: I don't know exactly how it works, I know roughly how often it works for me, and you're welcome to test it. Getting defensive comes from feeling you have to win the argument. You don't.

## Why the question stings {#why-it-stings}

It can feel like an attack on something you care about, or on your intelligence. Most of the time it isn't. It's curiosity, a test, a joke to break the ice, or worry for a friend who has started reading a lot.

I've been on the asking side myself. I put on an astrology video to mock it, read my own chart, and found it far too precise. So I tested it, box by box. When someone raises an eyebrow at my deck, I recognise the look. I tell that story in [can a sceptic learn tarot?](/blog/can-a-sceptic-learn-tarot/), and the short version is that doubt is a good place to start.

**Most people who ask aren't attacking; they're curious, and curiosity deserves a straight answer.**

## Know what you claim, and what you don't {#what-you-claim}

You can't be defensive about a claim you never made, so it helps to know your own list by heart. Here's mine:

- A card gives no orders; the person decides what to do with it.
- About one reading in ten misses, by my own manual count ([how I counted](/blog/does-tarot-work-300-readings/)). It's a tally, not a study.
- I don't know how it works. I know some conditions under which it works better, like the person saying nothing about themselves first.
- There's no gift. Anyone can learn to read.

Everything I [don't claim](/disclaimers/#claims) is written down where anyone can see it. And if you've read [the theories on how tarot might work](/blog/how-does-tarot-work/), you know nobody has settled the question. You don't have to settle it either, over coffee, to keep reading.

## A few calm answers {#calm-answers}

Adapt them to your own voice. None of them is a line to win with.

To a curious friend: "Honestly? I don't know how it works. It holds often enough that I keep doing it, and about one time in ten it misses. Want to try one and check?"

To a sharp sceptic: "Fair question. I'm not asking you to believe anything. Don't tell me anything about yourself, let me read, and judge it afterwards. If it misses, I'll say so."

To someone worried for a friend: "That's a kind thing to worry about. I don't read on health, court cases or money decisions, and I tell everyone not to act on a card. If your friend is drawing cards all day, that's rarely about tarot, and a break would do them good."

To someone who believes far more than you do: "It's real enough that I take it seriously, and not real enough to decide for you. You still get the last word."

To someone who was hurt by a reader before: "I'm sorry that happened. For what it's worth, a reader you can trust says when they miss, and doesn't sell you anything to fix what they saw."

To a joke: smile. "Real enough that you asked."

## When someone wants to argue {#argue}

Agree with what's fair. Cold reading exists. The Barnum effect exists. People remember the hits and forget the misses. Those are good objections, and they're the reason I ask people to say nothing first and why I count my misses. I've written about [how to tell cold reading from a tarot reading](/blog/cold-reading-vs-tarot/). "You're right, that's a real risk" ends more arguments than any comeback.

Then let it go. A calm answer won't convert anyone, and it isn't meant to. The aim is that the person leaves knowing what you actually do, rather than what they'd imagined. You don't need their agreement to keep reading, and they don't need yours to keep doubting.

## What not to say {#what-not-to-say}

The defensive moves come fast when you feel cornered. These are the ones to catch:

- "You're not open enough." It puts the miss on them.
- "Science doesn't know everything." True, and not an answer.
- "It'll make sense later." Turning a miss into a promise is how honest readers drift into dishonest ones.
- "You'll see." It sounds like a warning, not a conversation.
- Mocking back. Sneering at a sceptic is no better than a sceptic sneering at you, and the same goes for laughing at people who believe more than you do.
