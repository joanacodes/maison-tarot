A tarot live works if you keep your rules and accept that the format will try to break them. Lag, comments and nerves are what break first. My own lives were on Facebook, in tarot groups, not on Instagram; what follows are the lessons that don’t depend on the platform.

## What a tarot live is, and what I did {#what-it-is}

A tarot live is a reading, or a series of readings, broadcast in real time to whoever is watching, while viewers write in the comments. Some readers do one general reading for everyone. Others pick viewers and read for them one at a time.

A few months after I started reading, I was doing readings in Facebook tarot groups, and lives there. A Facebook review I received came from a viewer I’d read in one of those lives. If you’re choosing Instagram, check its live tools as they are today. For the wider question of tarot on that platform, I’ve written about [the reader you follow and the reader you need](/blog/tarot-on-instagram/).

Much of what changes when people watch has nothing to do with the camera, and [reading tarot in public](/blog/reading-tarot-in-public/) covers it: consent, what’s said aloud and what waits, declining a question kindly, nerves. This page is about what a live adds: a camera, a delay and a comment stream.

## What works: your rules, adapted to a camera {#what-works}

My rule doesn’t change in a live: tell me nothing. Ask people not to type their question, their age or their situation. In a comment stream that’s harder to hold than across a table, and it matters more, because every viewer can read what was typed, and so can you. Here’s [why I ask for silence](/blog/the-silence-rule/) in the first place.

Pick people at random rather than the fastest typists. In a live, speed in the comments is otherwise the only thing that decides who gets read.

Show every card to the camera. My notes from 2021 record that, with practice, messages began to come before I’d so much as shuffled, and that these days the cards are on the table largely because people want to see them. In a live that’s doubly true. If something comes before you’ve laid a card, say it, then show the card anyway so people can follow. **In a live, viewers can only follow what you put in front of the lens.** I answer [whether you still need the cards](/blog/do-you-still-need-tarot-cards/) at more length elsewhere.

And say misses out loud, as you would in any room. The replay will keep them either way, so you may as well be the one who names them.

## What breaks: lag and comments {#lag-and-comments}

Lag is the first surprise. There’s a delay between what you say and what viewers hear, and another between what they type and what you read. You ask “does that fit?”, and the answer arrives while you’re on the next card, attached to the wrong sentence. Ask one question at a time, wait, and say out loud which card you mean when the answer comes.

Comments are the second. People type reactions as you read: “yes”, “oh no”, “that’s my ex”. Each one is information, and each one steers you. Ask them to keep their reactions for the end. If they can’t, read with your eyes on the cards rather than on the comments.

The third is everyone else’s questions, arriving in the middle of someone’s reading: their health, a court case, whether their partner is cheating. They’re asking because they’re worried, and the kind way to decline is the same as in a room. The difference is that the question stays on screen: answer it once, briefly, then repeat the rules, or pin them if your platform lets you. I explain [the subjects I step back from](/disclaimers/#refusals), and why.

## Staying calm with a camera on you {#nerves}

Nerves in a live look a little different from nerves in a room. The camera is often close to your hands, so any tremor shows. There are usually no faces to read, only a stream of text, so you lose the small nods that steady you across a table. And your own face may sit in a corner of the screen, which turns reading into watching yourself read.

Staying calm doesn’t mean stopping the shaking. It means letting it shake and reading anyway. Lay the cards flat on the table instead of holding them up to the lens, and point the phone at the table rather than at your face if the preview distracts you. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in. If it helps, say it: “my hands are a bit shaky tonight”. Viewers forgive nerves far more easily than pretending.

## Before you go live: a checklist {#checklist}

1. Decide the length and the number of readings before you start, and keep both small: your attention is split between the cards, the camera and the comments.
2. Write the rules at the top: no questions in the comments, adults only, the subjects you don’t read on.
3. If the video will stay online as a replay, tell each person before they agree to be read. A reading in a room ends when the room empties; a replay doesn’t.
4. Test sound and light with a friend watching on another phone. Say a word and ask them to type it back: the gap you see is your delay.
5. Keep water within reach, and know what you’ll do if the connection drops: say where you were and carry on.
6. Read only the people who are there. “What does my boss think of me?” is one to gently set aside.

## Where to go from here {#next-step}

Before your first public live, rehearse with two friends as the only viewers: rules pinned, one reading each, and a note of every moment the delay or a comment pulled you off the card. If the comment stream still feels like too much, start with a small video call instead, where people speak rather than type; I explain [how to host a free reading night online](/blog/free-tarot-event-online/).
