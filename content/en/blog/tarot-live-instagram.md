A tarot live works if you keep your rules and accept that the format will try to break them. Lag, comments and nerves are what break first. One thing to say plainly before anything else: my own lives were on Facebook, in tarot groups, not on Instagram. What follows are the lessons that don’t depend on the platform.

## What a tarot live is, and what I did {#what-it-is}

A tarot live is a reading, or a series of readings, broadcast in real time to whoever is watching, while viewers write in the comments. Some readers do one general reading for everyone. Others pick viewers and read for them one at a time.

A few months after I started reading, I was doing readings in Facebook tarot groups, and lives there. One of the reviews I received on Facebook came from someone I’d read during a live. If you’re choosing Instagram, look at its live tools as they are today; I’ll stick to what I know from my own lives. For the wider question of tarot on that platform, I’ve written about [the reader you follow and the reader you need](/blog/tarot-on-instagram/).

## What works: the same rules as anywhere {#what-works}

My rule doesn’t change in a live: tell me nothing. Ask people not to type their question, their age or their situation. In a comment stream that’s harder to hold than across a table, and it matters more, because everyone can see what was typed. Here’s [why I ask for silence](/blog/the-silence-rule/) in the first place.

Pick people at random rather than the fastest typists. It’s fairer, and it keeps the evening from going to whoever insists most. [Reading in front of a crowd](/blog/reading-tarot-in-public/) already changes the stakes; a random draw keeps them even.

Show every card to the camera. In my 2021 notes, I wrote that the more I read, the more messages came before I’d shuffled, and that I use the cards mostly because the person wants to see them. In a live that’s doubly true: viewers want to see what you see. If something comes before you’ve laid a card, say it, then show the card anyway so people can follow. I answer [whether you still need the cards](/blog/do-you-still-need-tarot-cards/) at more length elsewhere.

Say misses out loud. **In a live, the rules you keep are the lesson people take home.** Rescue a miss in front of fifty people and you’ve taught fifty people that misses get rescued.

## What breaks: lag and comments {#lag-and-comments}

Lag is the first surprise. There’s a delay between what you say and what viewers hear, and another between what they type and what you read. You ask “does that fit?”, and the answer arrives while you’re on the next card, attached to the wrong sentence. Ask one question at a time, wait, and say out loud which card you mean when the answer comes.

Comments are the second. People type reactions as you read: “yes”, “oh no”, “that’s my ex”. Each one is information, and each one steers you. Ask them to keep their reactions for the end. If they can’t, read with your eyes on the cards rather than on the comments.

The third is everyone else’s questions, arriving in the middle of someone’s reading. Someone asks about their health, a court case, whether their partner is cheating. They’re asking because they’re worried, and in public the kindest answer is short: that’s not something I read on, and here’s what a reading can hold instead. I explain [the subjects I step back from](/disclaimers/#refusals), and why.

## Staying calm while your body shakes {#nerves}

Your body may react before your mind does: hands that shake, a dry mouth, a voice that goes up. It happens to people who read calmly at a kitchen table, the moment they know people are watching.

Staying calm doesn’t mean stopping the shaking. It means letting it shake and reading anyway. Slow down. Breathe out for longer than you breathe in. Lay the cards on the table instead of holding them up. If it helps, say it: “my hands are a bit shaky tonight”. Viewers forgive nerves far more easily than pretending.

Some of what rises in you may not be yours at all. When you read for someone, you can feel their emotions while keeping your own. Notice it, stay steady, and don’t let it take over.

## Before you go live: a checklist {#checklist}

1. Decide the length and the number of readings before you start. I sometimes stop after two hours of readings, out of caution; a live should be much shorter than that.
2. Write the rules at the top: no questions in the comments, adults only, the subjects you don’t read on.
3. Ask each person you pick whether they agree to be read in public. If the video will stay online afterwards, tell them before they say yes.
4. Test sound and light with a friend watching on another phone.
5. Keep water within reach, and know what you’ll do if the connection drops: say where you were and carry on.
6. Read only the people who are there. “What does my boss think of me?” is one to gently set aside.
