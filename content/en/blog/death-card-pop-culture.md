In films, the Death card is a countdown: someone turns it over, the music drops, and you know who won't make it to the end credits. On the 1909 card, Death is an ending that makes room for what comes next. The surprise, when you look closely, is that the best scenes know it.

## Why films love the Death card {#why-films}

Because it's the only card everyone can read without learning anything. A skeleton reads in half a second, in any language, from the back row of a cinema. For a director, it's foreshadowing that costs one shot.

No other card does that job as well. The Tower comes close, but a burning building needs explaining; the Ten of Swords is too much to take in at a glance; the Three of Swords looks like a greeting card. So when a script needs tarot to mean "danger", it reaches for number 13.

## Three scenes worth watching again {#three-scenes}

I've kept only scenes I could check. Better three real ones than seven half-remembered.

*Cléo from 5 to 7* (Agnès Varda, 1962) opens on a tarot reading filmed from above, and it's the only part of the film in colour. Cléo, a singer, is waiting for the results of a medical test. The reader lays out the cards, Death comes up, and she says what a reader would say: it means a deep transformation, not necessarily a death. Cléo doesn't hear "transformation". The rest of the film follows her through Paris carrying the word she did hear ([the film's summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cl%C3%A9o_from_5_to_7)).

*The Simpsons*, "Lisa's Wedding" (1995). At a Renaissance fair, Lisa sits down with a fortune-teller. The first card is Death, and the reader explains that it stands for change, a new leaf, not dying. The next card is the Happy Squirrel, which exists in no real deck. The joke only works because the writers knew the real meaning well enough to play with it ([an essay on the scene](https://wildhunt.org/2026/03/tarot-at-my-table-and-in-popular-culture.html)).

*Tarot* (2024) is a horror film in which a group of students use an old deck and start dying one by one, each according to their reading. A character gives the textbook line — Death can mean the end of something or the start of something new — and the film then spends its whole length ignoring the second half. As [Rue Morgue's review](https://rue-morgue.com/movie-review-tarot-isnt-playing-with-a-full-deck) summed it up: in this film, it just means death.

## What the scenes get wrong {#what-they-get-wrong}

Three things, and they matter more than the skeleton.

The first is the card as a verdict. On screen, a card announces an event and the event happens. No card announces a death — not this one, not any other. That isn't a softened version of the meaning; it is the meaning.

The second is the reader as an oracle, or as a threat. In these scenes the reader knows and the sitter is told. At a real table it goes the other way: I read; you decide.

The third is treating the ending as the whole card. Look at what Pamela Colman Smith drew: a skeleton in black armour on a white horse, carrying a black banner with a white rose. A king lies fallen in front of him, a bishop stands with his hands joined, a young woman turns her face away, a child looks up. And in the background, between two towers, a sun on the horizon. The picture already contains the after.

My own note on the card says it isn't about death itself. It's linked to the harvest and to starting again — someone without a goal who wants to make life fertile again. By number, 13 reduces to 4: foundations. You'll find the rest on [the Death card page](/library/tarot-cards/death/), and in the Marseille deck the card doesn't even carry its name — [why Death is the card with no name](/blog/death-card-no-name/).

## What they get right {#what-they-get-right}

Two of the three scenes put the correct meaning in the reader's mouth, then show that a frightened person can't hear it. That's accurate. **When Death comes up, faces change before you've said a word — the films taught them that.** So the first thing a reader does is slow down and say what's actually on the card.

Cléo's scene gets one more thing right. She's waiting for a medical result, and she goes to the cards for an answer they can't give. I understand why people do it: fear wants a date, a yes or a no, anything but the wait. I don't read on health or on death, and it isn't coldness. A wrong word there costs too much, and the cards aren't built for that question. If you're waiting for results, your doctor is the person to ask; meanwhile, a gentler question for the cards is "what helps me get through the wait?". I explain this at more length in [why careful readers step back from health, pregnancy and death](/blog/health-pregnancy-death-tarot/).
