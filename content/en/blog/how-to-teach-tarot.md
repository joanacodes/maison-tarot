Teach tarot by making people try before you explain. Hand someone a card, ask what they see, and let them talk. Every person I’ve helped to read managed it within one to three tries; what they needed from me was permission and a few tools, not a lecture.

## How I started teaching {#how-i-started}

I didn’t set out to teach. I made my friends and family try reading, and something happened that I hadn’t planned for.

Someone says something true about the person in front of them, and then they’re shocked by what they’ve just said. Their face changes before they’ve decided what to think about it. That moment is why I teach.

I can’t describe it in a way that does it justice. You can’t be told what it’s like. You can only try. That’s the first lesson for whoever is teaching, too: your explanations matter less than the moment you step back and let the other person speak.

## Card first, explanation after {#card-first}

Start with one card, not with the history of tarot or the 78 meanings. Put it face up and ask three things: what’s on it, what do you see in it, what do you receive?

Then let them talk, and don’t correct. Say they look at the Three of Cups and tell you it’s friends celebrating something that’s already over. That’s a reading. You can add context later; you can’t give back the confidence you took by correcting the first sentence.

It’s how my [workshops start: a card in the first ten minutes](/blog/first-ten-minutes-tarot-workshop/), the explanation after. People who learn the meaning first tend to recite it. People who look first tend to read.

Then build in steps: one card a day for a while, then three cards, then more. Tell them early that the same image can read differently from one person to another, and from one day to the next. It saves them a lot of worrying about whether they’ve got it “right”.

## Give them lenses, not 78 meanings {#lenses}

Memorising the deck is where most beginners stall. What I teach instead are lenses, ways of looking at any card: colours, the four elements, numbers from one to ten, feminine and masculine energies, and astrological modes.

Colour is the quickest to pass on. Show them the yellow sky behind the Fool and ask what yellow does for them; my grid says joy, optimism, the inner child, will. Then add the number, then the element. With [the colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) and one other lens, a beginner can say something about nearly any card on the first evening.

**A lens gives people something to look at; a list of meanings gives them something to forget.**

## When someone freezes {#when-they-freeze}

Sometimes a person looks at the card and nothing comes. In my notes, that’s usually a block: too much pressure, the fear of getting it wrong in front of you, or the person being read is afraid of hearing something. Talk with them, reassure them.

What helps:

1. Lower the stakes. Tell them misses are part of learning and that you’ll count them together.
2. Ask them to describe rather than interpret: the figure, the colour, the number.
3. Remind them not to take themselves too seriously. Trust what comes, say it, see.
4. Change who they’re reading for. Some people read a stranger more freely than their own sister.
5. Stop for today. The second or third try is usually the one.

Whatever happens, don’t tell them they have a gift, or that they don’t. Anyone can learn. It’s like art: some people have talent, and anyone who practises can do it. I’ve explained why [you don’t need psychic abilities](/blog/do-you-need-psychic-abilities/) to read.

## The rules to teach on day one {#rules}

Teach these before reading becomes a habit, because they’re harder to add later:

- Ask permission before reading anyone. Reading someone who hasn’t agreed is like opening the drawers in their bedroom.
- The person tells you nothing first. [The silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/) is what lets a beginner check themselves afterwards.
- Write down what you said and what was confirmed.
- Say a miss out loud, with no second card drawn to rescue it.
- Some subjects aren’t for the cards: health, death, pregnancy, court cases, money decisions. Teach a kinder question to offer instead.
- Tarot never replaces therapy, and it shouldn’t turn into a habit nobody can put down, for the reader or the person being read.

## Where to take it next {#next-step}
