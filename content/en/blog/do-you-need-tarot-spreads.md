You don’t need spreads to read tarot. A spread is a tool for holding a question in shape, and like any tool it helps with some jobs and gets in the way of others. Here’s how to tell which is which, and how to read when you leave the layout aside.

## What a tarot spread is {#what-a-spread-is}

A spread, or layout, is a set pattern for laying out cards in which each place — a position — carries a fixed question. In a past–present–future spread, the card on the left is read as the past, whichever card it is. The Sun in that place is a happy past; the Sun on the right is a happy direction.

Readers use them in two ways, and I find both legitimate. Some follow set patterns: a career reading laid out this way, a love reading that way, every time. Others follow their intuition and invent a new spread for each person, or lay the cards in a row and let the story find its own shape. Two readers can look at the same cards, see two different stories, and both explain why — one of the reasons I think of reading as [close to an art form](/blog/is-tarot-an-art-form/).

## When a layout helps {#when-it-helps}

When you’re starting. Three cards with no positions can all talk at once. A position gives your eye a place to begin and a question to answer. That’s why I start people on one card a day, then [three cards in the present](/blog/three-card-tarot-spread/) — what’s here, what’s in the way, what you can do — and only then more.

When you’ll compare over time. If you lay the same five positions every month, you can put the readings side by side in your notebook and see what moved. A different improvised layout each time makes that impossible.

When you read for someone else. Good positions keep you on the kind side of the line. In [a love spread built that way](/blog/love-tarot-spread/), every position is about the person and the relationship, never about what the other one thinks, so the questions that hurt never get a slot.

When you want to be checkable. “Card three is what’s in the way” is a claim. The person can say yes or no to it, and you can write the answer down. **A position turns a vague impression into a sentence someone can confirm or contradict.**

## When it gets in the way {#when-it-gets-in-the-way}

When the position speaks louder than the card. The Sun lands in the “obstacle” slot, and you twist yourself into explaining how joy is the problem. Sometimes there’s something in that; often you’re serving the layout instead of reading the card.

When there are more positions than the question needs. “Should I text her back tonight?” doesn’t need ten cards; it may not need tarot at all. [How many cards to pull](/blog/how-many-tarot-cards-to-pull/) gives a rule of thumb, question by question.

When you pick the spread to get the answer you want. Scrolling through layouts until one has a position called “will he come back” isn’t choosing a tool. It’s looking for permission.

And when collecting spreads replaces reading. You can own a hundred layouts and never have looked properly at the Two of Cups.

## Reading without a spread {#without-a-spread}

Decide how many cards before you shuffle — three or five — and lay them in a row with no positions. Then read the row as one sentence through its lenses: which element leads, whether the numbers rise or fall, which colour carries across. The odd one out is often the point.

Say you draw four cards: the Queen of Swords, the Seven of Swords, the Two of Swords and the Ace of Cups.

Three Swords, then one Cup. Three [Air cards](/library/four-elements/air-element/) — thinking, weighing, cutting — with the grey of the Two of Swords in the middle, neutral, undecided. Then one Water at the end: a cup overflowing from a hand in a cloud, a number 1, a new start in feeling. As a sentence: “Your head has been doing all the work, weighing and deciding, and what’s waiting at the end of the row is a feeling that’s only beginning.” No position told you that. The row did.

Reading without positions has a cost, and it’s worth saying. With nothing fixed in advance, it’s tempting to keep drawing until the story feels finished, or to let the sentence drift once you see the person’s face. Two rules keep it honest: the number of cards is decided before the shuffle, and the sentence is written down before anyone confirms it.

## How to choose, tonight {#choose}

1. Write your question down in one line.
2. If it’s about one thing, today, draw one card.
3. If it’s a situation with something stuck in it, draw three cards in the present.
4. If you’ll want to compare over months, choose a layout and keep it — or [build your own in four questions](/blog/build-your-own-tarot-spread/).
5. If it’s a real crossroads and you have an hour, try the [Celtic Cross](/blog/celtic-cross-spread/), two cards at a time.

Then test it rather than taking my word for it: one week with a layout, one week without, both in your notebook. Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.
