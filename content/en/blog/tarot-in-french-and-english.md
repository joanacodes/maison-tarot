A Rider-Waite-Smith card looks exactly the same in Paris and in London. What changes is everything we say about it: the names of a few majors, the suit of coins, and the words for the reading itself. I'm bilingual, I lived in London in 2019, and today I read and teach from Paris, in French and in English. Here's what moves when tarot speaks French.

## Two languages, two home decks {#two-decks}

In France, the traditional deck is the Tarot de Marseille, a pattern printed by French cardmakers for centuries ([its history is in the Library](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/)). In English-speaking tarot, most people meet the Rider-Waite-Smith first. So when a French reader names a card, they often use its Marseille name, and an English reader uses the name printed on the 1909 cards.

One more surprise for English speakers: in France, *le tarot* is also a card game, played around kitchen tables with a 78-card pack, twenty-one trumps and a card called l'Excuse. "On se fait un tarot ?" means "fancy a game?", not "will you read my cards?".

The decks themselves differ too: pips or scenes, old woodcuts or Pamela Colman Smith's drawings. I've compared them in [Marseille tarot vs Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/). Here, I'll stay with the words.

## Bateleur or Magician: the names that change {#names}

Most majors translate word for word: the Hanged Man is le Pendu, the Star is l'Étoile, the World is le Monde. A handful don't, and these are the ones that cause confusion in a bilingual conversation.

| Rider-Waite-Smith (English) | Rider-Waite-Smith in French, on this site | Tarot de Marseille |
|---|---|---|
| The Fool | Le Mat | Le Mat (unnumbered) |
| The Magician | Le Magicien | Le Bateleur, a street performer |
| The High Priestess | La Grande Prêtresse | La Papesse, the female pope |
| The Hierophant | Le Hiérophante | Le Pape |
| The Lovers | Les Amoureux | L'Amoureux, in the singular |
| Strength (VIII) | La Force | La Force, numbered XI |
| Justice (XI) | La Justice | La Justice, numbered VIII |
| Death (XIII) | La Mort | L'Arcane sans nom, the card with no name |
| The Tower | La Tour | La Maison Dieu |

The singular matters more than it looks. The Marseille's Amoureux is one young man standing between two women, with Cupid aiming above him: a choice. Waite's Lovers are a couple under an angel. Read the French name and you think of one person in love; read the English and you think of two.

And if someone says "l'arcane VIII", ask which deck before you answer. In the Marseille it's Justice; in the Rider-Waite-Smith it's Strength. The card that frightens people most also changes its label: [why Death is the card with no name](/blog/death-card-no-name/) has the full story.

## Lame, arcane, tirage: the vocabulary {#vocabulary}

A short glossary for the words you'll meet in French tarot books and conversations.

- *Une lame*: a tarot card, literally "a blade". It belongs to tarot; you'll rarely hear it for a playing card.
- *Un arcane*: from the Latin for a secret. English mostly uses it as a group, the major arcana. French also uses it for a single card: "l'arcane XIII".
- *Un tirage*: from *tirer*, to draw. It covers the layout, the act and often the whole reading. English splits them into a spread, a draw and a reading.
- *Tirer les cartes*: to read the cards for someone. *Se faire tirer les cartes*: to have them read.
- *Un ou une tarologue*: a tarot reader. Not the same as *une voyante*, a clairvoyant, the word you'll see on [the adverts for psychic phone lines](/blog/psychic-phone-lines-france/). English tends to blur both into "reader" or "psychic".
- *Les figures*: the court cards. Valet, Cavalier, Reine, Roi for Page, Knight, Queen, King, [explained here](/blog/court-cards-explained/).
- *À l'envers*: reversed.

## Deniers or Pentacles: the suit that changes {#suits}

Three suits translate cleanly: Wands are Bâtons, Cups are Coupes, Swords are Épées. The fourth doesn't.

| English | Tarot de Marseille | Rider-Waite-Smith in French, on this site | Element |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wands | Bâtons | Bâtons | Fire |
| Cups | Coupes | Coupes | Water |
| Swords | Épées | Épées | Air |
| Pentacles | Deniers | Pentacles | Earth |

Deniers are old coins, and the word pulls a French reader straight towards money. Pentacles are discs carrying a five-pointed star, which is what Pamela Colman Smith drew. In French you'll meet both words; on Maison Tarot, the French pages say "Pentacles", which matches the drawing.

Either way, I read the suit through its element. [Earth](/library/four-elements/earth-element/) is matter, prosperity, whatever has value for you, money or sentimental. **Whatever the word on the card, the suit is about what you value, and not all riches are gold.**

## What changes in the reading itself {#reading-across}

Less than you'd think, and more than nothing. The same image can read differently from one person to another and from one day to another; the language is one more thing that shifts. A French speaker who learnt on the Marseille may hear "the Magician" and not connect it with their Bateleur. An English speaker may hear "Deniers" and read a money card where you meant something broader.

So when you read for someone in their second language, describe the picture before you name it. "A man at a table, one hand raised, everything laid out in front of him" works in any language; a name only works if you share the same deck. And one sentence crosses over without losing anything: cards aren't magic, you are. *Les cartes ne sont pas magiques, vous l'êtes.*

## Try it with one card {#try-it}

Draw a card. Name it in English, then in French, then describe it without using its name at all. Notice which of the three sentences tells you most; it's often the third. Every card in the [Library's 78 card pages](/library/tarot-cards/) has its French twin, so you can check the names and compare what each language adds.
