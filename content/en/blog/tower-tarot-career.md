The Tower in a career reading is the moment something at work stops holding: a restructure, a project pulled overnight, a truth about your job you can’t unsee. It shows the shock, not where you land, and it asks you not to decide in the flash.

{{< cards "ar16" >}}

## The Tower through its lenses {#lenses}

A grey tower on a rock, a black sky, lightning knocking off a roof shaped like a crown, two people falling headfirst. Pamela Colman Smith stopped the picture mid-fall: we never see anyone land. At work, that’s where the fear lives — in the part of the frame the card doesn’t draw.

The number: 16 becomes 1 + 6 = [7](/library/numerology/number-7/). Seven is assessment, patience, analysis before action. In the Instagram story I use to teach the numbers, 7 is the negotiating table where Facebook changes the app’s model: neither triumph nor fall, the moment you choose what the thing becomes. **The lightning isn’t a verdict on your career; it’s the start of a review.**

The element is Fire, which spreads fast when nobody masters it. In my notes, fire destroys at once where neglected water destroys slowly, and at work the Tower is often both: the announcement is sudden, but the cracks had been there for months — the budget that kept shrinking, the meeting that kept being postponed.

The colours: a black sky, which in my grid is reality without varnish; a grey tower, a structure that stood because it always had; yellow and red-orange for the lightning and the flames — will, pride, impulse. The energy is masculine, and the astrology, if it speaks to you, is Mars: action, conflict, what moves fast. Every lens is laid out on [the Tower’s own page](/library/tarot-cards/the-tower/).

## What the Tower says about your job, a project or a team {#work}

The Tower is a structure meeting the truth. At work, that structure can be many things.

A job: a restructure, a merger, a new director who changes everything in a week. A project: the one you’ve carried for a year, cancelled in a fifteen-minute call. A team: someone leaves, and the whole arrangement turns out to have rested on them. A belief — “this company is my family” — that falls in a single meeting.

It isn’t always imposed from above. Sometimes the lightning is yours: the morning you realise you can’t do this job for one more year. That’s a Tower too, and it can feel like relief as much as fear.

Say someone draws the Tower the week a reorganisation is announced. The card doesn’t tell them whether their post survives. It tells them the old arrangement — the org chart, the unwritten deal — has already fallen, whatever happens to their name on it. That’s a different place to stand: not waiting for a verdict, but looking at what’s left.

## The Tower reversed at work {#reversed}

Reversed, the Tower is the collapse in slow motion. Everyone can see the cracks — the project nobody believes in, the department everyone knows will be merged — and the meetings carry on as if nothing were happening. Sometimes holding on a little longer is right; sometimes it only makes the fall longer.

Or it’s the Tower in excess: burning every bridge yourself so you feel in control of the fall, like the resignation email written at midnight and copied to everyone. The question for a reversed Tower is what you’re keeping up only because you’re afraid of the noise it would make coming down.

## The Tower next to other cards {#combinations}

{{< cards "ar13 ar17 pe08" >}}

Next to Death, you have the slow and the fast versions of the same change: Death harvests, the Tower strikes. Together they say an ending at work is real, and that the question is what you plant next. I’ve written about [Death in a career reading](/blog/death-tarot-career/) for that slower season.

Next to the Star, the card that follows it in the deck, the calm is already in sight: a woman kneels by a pool and pours water out freely under an open sky. Hope doesn’t come instead of the shock; it comes after it.

Next to the Eight of Pentacles — a craftsman at his bench, mastery and necessary effort in my notes — you rebuild by working rather than by worrying: one skill, one application at a time.

## “Will I lose my job?” — and a kinder question {#question}

It’s the first thing people ask when this card turns up, and I understand why: a job is rent, routine and a part of who you are.

I don’t read that question as a prediction. Whether a post survives depends on people who aren’t in the room and decisions that haven’t been made yet. A card that said “you’re safe” or “you’re out” would add a false calm, or a second shock to the first. And [tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/): whatever you decide about your work, decide it on what you know and feel.

A gentler question: “What in my work has stopped holding, and what do I want to rely on next?” Or: “What would I do in the first week if it happened?” Those have answers, and they turn fear into a plan.

## What to do next {#what-to-do}

These help in the days after a shake.

1. Don’t decide in the flash. Seven is analysis before action: resigning, accepting, confronting — none of it has to be settled today.
2. Make two lists: what fell, and what’s still standing — your skills, the people who answer your calls. The second list is usually longer than you expect.
3. Get the facts in writing: dates, what was said, what’s proposed. Fire makes memory unreliable.
4. If your contract, your rights or your money are at stake, that’s one for an employment adviser, a union rep or a lawyer. I don’t read on legal cases or money decisions ([what I don’t read, and why](/disclaimers/#refusals)).
5. Take a break from your cards now and then — especially now. Pulling a card every hour after a shock is the shock talking.
6. When the dust settles, lay three cards: what fell, what’s still standing, what I want to build on it. Read them again in a month. For a fuller layout, my [career spread](/blog/career-tarot-spread/) reads a work decision without letting the cards take it.
