I always waited for a light before I stepped into a dark room. Today I walk through my place with the lights off, and noises at night don't make me jump. Shadow work is the idea that helps me understand the distance between those two sentences.

## How I used to walk into a dark room {#before}

Light first, then me. At night I turned the lights on everywhere I went.

Looking back, I’d call that living in fear without noticing. That’s the part worth looking at. Fear you don’t notice doesn’t look like fear: it looks like a reflex, a hand reaching for a switch, repeated so often that nobody would think to call it anything else.

**A lot of what we avoid doesn't feel like fear; it feels like a habit.** Nobody organises their evenings around a scream. They organise them around a switch.

## How I walk through it now {#now}

Lights off. If there’s a noise in the night, I don’t panic. What I tell myself is more or less this: *well, if that was ever a spirit or something, let it be.*

That sentence isn't "there's nothing there", and it isn't "it's a spirit". It leaves the question open and stops needing an answer. Most night noises are a building being a building. And if a noise were ever something more, the sentence still holds: let it be.

The dark is as dark as it ever was. Whatever changed, changed on my side of the switch, and that side is exactly what shadow work looks at.

## What shadow work is {#shadow-work}

The shadow is Carl Jung's word for the parts of yourself you've pushed out of view because they didn't fit who you were supposed to be: an anger you weren't allowed, a want you were ashamed of, a memory you couldn't deal with. Shadow work is dealing with the things you keep pushing away so you can get through the day — the things that end up disabling you precisely because you won't look at them.

Say someone grew up in a house where anger wasn't welcome. As an adult, they're the calm one, always. The anger hasn't gone anywhere. It comes out sideways: a sharp tone over something small, or a joke that bites harder than they meant. Shadow work would be noticing the pattern, naming the anger, and asking where it was put away and why.

It's hard work. Can you do it with a therapist? Of course, and you should. Tarot can help you look, but it should never replace therapy; I've written about [where that door is](/blog/tarot-and-mental-health/).

## What the dark has to do with it {#the-dark}

Here's my hypothesis, and it's mine, not a rule: being afraid of the dark can be being afraid of your own shadow. A dark room is like the cell where you've put what you never wanted, or couldn't deal with. You don't know what's in there, so you don't go in without a light.

A switch is a reasonable answer to a dark room. It's a less useful answer to a room you carry around with you, because there's no wall to put it on.

Two cards sit close to this idea. [The Moon](/library/tarot-cards/the-moon/) shows a path running between two towers at night, a dog and a wolf howling, a creature crawling out of the water: what surfaces when the light is low. The [Nine of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/nine-of-swords/), as I read it, is coming out of a nightmare, a long troubled period, with protection above you.

Getting past that kind of fear takes a long road, and also a split second. It's like a veil you take off. **But first you have to see that there's a veil.** Most of the road is that part.

## If the dark is heavy for you {#if-its-heavy}

Being afraid of the dark is common, at any age, and there's nothing to be ashamed of in it. Leaving a light on is fine. Nobody owes anyone a brave walk down a black corridor.

If the fear is heavy, though — if it keeps you from sleeping, from being home alone, or comes with panic — it isn't one for a card or an article. A therapist is the right person, and going to one isn't a failure of anything; it's [the door I point to](/disclaimers/#therapy) whenever something is bigger than a reading. Be as gentle and tolerant with yourself as you are with others.

## One small thing to try {#try}

If your fear is a light one, here's an experiment. The next time your hand reaches for a switch, pause for one breath before you press it. Notice what you were expecting to find. Write it down, in one line. Then turn the light on; there's no prize for leaving it off.

Do that for a week and read the lines back. If the same thing turns up night after night, that's worth noticing: it's rarely about the room. If you'd like to bring what you find to the cards, the [three-card shadow work spread](/blog/shadow-work-beginners/) is a gentle place to start.
