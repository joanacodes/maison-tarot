You can learn tarot at any age, and starting at 40, 50 or 60 has a real advantage. The cards are scenes from ordinary life, and you've lived more of them. Reading is mostly recognising, and the more situations you've been through, the more you recognise.

## Is it too late to learn tarot? {#too-late}

No. There's no age for it, and there's no gift you needed to receive young. Every person I've helped to read has managed it within their first three tries, once they stopped trying to be right.

It's a bit like drawing or music: some people have a knack, and anyone who practises gets there. The only practice that really matters in tarot is letting go, and letting go has no age limit.

What you need hasn't changed since you were twenty: a deck, a way of looking, someone who tells you nothing, and a notebook. The [beginners' guide](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/) covers all four, and if you're wondering which deck, [my pick for anyone starting out is the Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/best-tarot-deck-for-beginners/): every card has a scene, and scenes are what experience reads best.

## Why life experience reads better {#experience}

A tarot card shows a situation. Reading it means recognising what's going on, and recognition depends on what you've seen. **The deck doesn't reward memory; it rewards recognition, and recognition grows with the years.** Take four cards.

The Ten of Pentacles shows three generations in a courtyard: an old man in a patterned cloak, a couple, a child, two dogs. My note on it: complete wealth at home, the whole family present — and possibly short-lived. If you've been the child, then the parent, then watched your own parents age, you read "short-lived" in a way a twenty-year-old can only guess at.

The Five of Pentacles puts two figures out in a snowstorm, hurrying past a church whose window is lit. Hardship and cold, and a warm place right beside them. If you've been through a hard winter of your own, you're more likely to notice the window.

The Six of Cups is childhood, taking time for yourself, flowering again. At fifty, childhood is a place you can look at from a distance, with some tenderness.

And Death isn't about dying. It's the harvest, the start of something else — someone without a goal who wants to make life fertile again. If you've changed careers, moved country or started over after a separation, you already know a chapter can end without anything ending.

## What's harder when you start later, and what helps {#harder}

Two things tend to get in the way. Neither is age.

The first is memorising. Learning 78 meanings is tedious at any age, which is why I don't teach them. A few lenses — the colour, the number, the element — give you a way into any card without learning it by heart.

The second is being used to being competent. At fifty you're good at things, and a first reading asks you to say something unproven out loud, to a person, with no safety net. That discomfort is normal. It usually fades the first time someone looks up and asks how you knew.

If you've spent decades as the rational one in the room, that's an asset. A sceptic makes a careful reader, because a sceptic checks. I came to astrology to mock it myself; [a sceptic can learn tarot](/blog/can-a-sceptic-learn-tarot/), and often learns it well.

If the small details are hard to see, a good lamp or a larger edition of the same deck solves it. The scenes are what you're reading, not the fine print.

Time matters less than you'd think. Ten minutes a day is enough to begin, with [one card each morning](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/) and three lines in a notebook.

## What you bring to a reading {#what-you-bring}

Reading isn't only seeing. It's also knowing how to say it. Sometimes you sense you shouldn't say something yet, or should say it differently, because the person isn't ready or is frightened. That's a skill life teaches better than any course: when to wait, when to soften, when to stay quiet.

The same goes for the moments when nothing comes. Often it's because the person is afraid of what they'll hear, and the answer is to put the cards down, talk and reassure them before going on. Years of conversations at work, in families and around kitchen tables have trained you for exactly that.

You also tend to know which questions belong elsewhere. Illness and death, pregnancy, lawsuits, big money choices: people ask about them out of worry or love, and a card is the wrong tool for them. If you've ever waited for a medical result, you understand why without being told. [I explain where I step back, and why](/disclaimers/#refusals).

## Where to start {#start}

Pick the pace that suits you. A [workshop](/workshops/) is three hours, twelve people and one lens, with a card in your hands in the first ten minutes. A [one-to-one session](/sessions/) is sixty minutes at your own speed, with room for every question you'd rather not ask in a group.

Either way, the first step is the same: one card, one person who tells you nothing, and the courage to say what you see.
