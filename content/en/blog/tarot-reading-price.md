There’s no standard price for a tarot reading. What you pay depends on how it’s sold — per session, per question or per minute — on how long it lasts, where it happens, and who takes a share along the way. I’d rather not quote market figures I can’t back up, so this page gives you what outlasts any price list: how readings are sold, what goes into a price, what never justifies a higher one, and how to compare two.

## How tarot readings are priced {#how-priced}

In France and in the UK, readings are sold in a handful of formats. **The format decides your final bill more than the number on the page does.**

- *Per session.* A fixed length — half an hour, an hour — for a fixed price, in person, by video or by phone. You know the total before you sit down.
- *Per question.* One question, one answer, often written or recorded and sent afterwards.
- *Per minute.* The model of phone lines and chat platforms. In France it has long meant premium-rate numbers, and now often a card payment with a counter running. The total depends on how long you stay.
- *Written or recorded readings.* You send a first name, sometimes a photo, and a reading arrives by email or video. There’s no conversation, so no moment to say “that doesn’t fit”.
- *Bundles and subscriptions.* A set of questions or a monthly plan, cheaper per unit. [How pay-per-question and subscriptions work](/blog/tarot-subscriptions/) deserves its own page.
- *Fairs and events.* A few minutes at a stand, at a wellbeing show or a psychic fair.

You won’t find a price range on this page. Published prices are each reader’s own list, so they tell you what one person asks, not what readings cost. To get a range that means something where you live, take three readers who sell the same format for the same length, note the total each would charge, and compare those three numbers. A per-minute rate next to an hourly fee tells you nothing until you’ve turned both into a total.

## What a fair price pays for {#what-goes-in}

Time, first, and more of it than you see. A one-hour reading has preparation before it, notes after it, and rest. Interpreting takes energy; I sometimes stop after two hours of readings, out of caution, and a reader who works full days has to price in the hours they can’t sell.

Experience, but not the kind measured in years. What matters is whether the reader has checked their own work. Someone who keeps a notebook of what they said and what was confirmed knows their miss rate. Someone who doesn’t is guessing about their own accuracy, and charging for the guess.

Then the costs you never meet. A platform or phone line keeps a share of what you pay. A room in central Paris or London has rent. There are payment fees, a website, sometimes insurance. And tax: social contributions for a self-employed reader in France, income tax and National Insurance in the UK.

Say two readers charge the same for an hour. One reads by video from home and keeps nearly all of it. The other works through a platform that takes its cut, then pays contributions on the rest. Same price for you, very different pay for them. A high price isn’t automatically greed, and a low one isn’t automatically a bargain.

## What never justifies a higher price {#what-doesnt}

Some things get sold as premium and shouldn’t be:

- *A “gift”.* There isn’t one; anyone can learn to read, so it can’t be a surcharge.
- *A lineage or a title*, offered as the reason for the price rather than as background.
- *Urgency.* “I see something serious, call me back today.”
- *Objects and rituals.* Candles, baths, “clearings” added after the reading.
- *Curse removal.* It’s [the oldest con in the trade](/blog/lift-the-curse-scam/), and no reading ever needs it.
- *A promise of accuracy.* Nobody reads at a hundred per cent. By my own count, about one reading in ten misses, and I’d be wary of anyone who claims none.

## How to compare two prices {#compare}

1. Ask for the total before you start, not the rate.
2. Divide it by the time you’ll actually be read — not the waiting, not the small talk.
3. Ask what the reader doesn’t read on. If the answer is “nothing”, that’s an answer.
4. Ask what happens when part of a reading doesn’t land. A fair reader says so and doesn’t sell you a second card to rescue it.
5. Before any per-minute call, set your own ceiling and hang up when you reach it. The meter rewards long calls, not good ones.
6. Read the small print. In the UK, “for entertainment purposes only” turns up on many price lists; [what that line really means](/blog/entertainment-purposes-only/) takes two minutes to understand.

## What I charge, and what I don’t {#my-prices}

I don’t sell readings, so I’m outside this market. I sell lessons — one-to-one sessions of an hour, workshops of three hours for twelve people — and readings are free at events. The details are on [what I charge for and what I give away](/blog/what-i-charge-for/), and why I chose that model, with what money does to a practice, is in [should tarot be free?](/blog/tarot-and-capitalism/).

One honest limit: no price list tells you what a reading is worth to you. That depends on what you do with it afterwards, and a reading isn’t a reason to act. Act on what you feel.
