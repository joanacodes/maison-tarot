The Rider-Waite-Smith is the 78-card tarot deck published in London in December 1909: designed under the direction of Arthur Edward Waite, drawn by Pamela Colman Smith, printed by William Rider & Son. “Rider” is the publisher, not a person who drew or wrote anything. It became the standard for three reasons — a scene on every card, a cheap American edition in the early 1970s, and drawings that are now public domain — and it's the deck I teach with.

## What the Rider-Waite-Smith deck is {#what-it-is}

It's a tarot of 78 cards: 22 major arcana, numbered 0 to 21, and 56 minor arcana in four suits — Wands, Cups, Swords and Pentacles — each running from Ace to Ten, plus a Page, a Knight, a Queen and a King.

Two people made it. [Arthur Edward Waite](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/), a writer on mysticism living in London, chose the symbolism and wrote the companion book, *The Pictorial Key to the Tarot* (1910). [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/), an illustrator and stage designer, drew all 78 cards in 1909. Both had been members of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, an occult society founded in London in 1888, and its system of correspondences runs through the deck. That's why Waite swapped two cards: Strength became VIII and Justice XI, the reverse of the older order. [The Golden Dawn and its tarot](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/) explains where that comes from.

Smith was paid a flat fee for the drawings, with no share in the sales. For most of the twentieth century her name wasn't in the deck's name either. “Rider-Waite-Smith”, or “Waite-Smith”, is how readers now put her back.

## Who was ‘Rider’? {#who-was-rider}

Not a co-author. William Rider & Son was a London publishing house. Its director from 1892 to 1925, Ralph Shirley, made it one of the leading British publishers of occult and mystical books, and in 1905 he founded a monthly magazine, the *Occult Review*. Waite was one of his authors; Rider published the deck, then the illustrated book a year later.

The publisher's name stuck to the cards. For decades, people asked a shop for “the Rider pack” or “the Rider-Waite”, the way you ask for a brand. **The name you know the deck by is, in large part, the name of the company that printed it.** The firm itself survives as Rider, an imprint now owned by Penguin Random House.

## Why it became the standard {#why-the-standard}

Three things happened, one after the other.

### A scene on every card

In the decks most people used before, the Tarot de Marseille among them, the minor cards show their suit symbols in a pattern: the Five of Cups is five cups arranged on the card. Smith drew a scene for each one. Her Three of Swords is a heart pierced by three blades in the rain; her Eight of Pentacles is a craftsman at his bench, carving coin after coin. A beginner can start reading those without a book.

She had a model. The Sola Busca, an Italian deck from the end of the fifteenth century, had already illustrated its minor cards, and she very likely saw photographs of it in London. But it was her deck that made illustrated minors the norm.

### A cheap edition in the United States

In 1968, an American businessman, Stuart Kaplan, brought a Swiss tarot deck back from the Nuremberg toy fair and sold it to bookshops. It did well enough for him to found a company, U.S. Games Systems, which at the start of the 1970s obtained the rights to publish the Rider-Waite in the United States ([the Penn Gazette tells the story](https://thepenngazette.com/the-collector/)). The deck arrived just as interest in tarot was growing. Many of the tarot books written from then on used its images; learners bought the deck the books showed; the next books used it again. That loop is still turning.

### Drawings anyone can use

Smith's 1909 drawings are now in the public domain: in the United States because the copyright wasn't renewed, and in the UK and the EU since 1 January 2022, seventy years after Smith's death in 1951. That's why hundreds of modern decks are redrawings of hers, and why a site like this one can show you the original cards. Later recoloured editions and trade names are a separate matter, and the line between the original and its reprints has even reached court ([Plagiarism Today explains the dispute](https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2024/02/28/the-copyright-battle-over-a-tarot-card-deck/)).

Smith herself was largely forgotten for decades; exhibitions such as the one [Pratt Institute's libraries devoted to her](https://hyperallergic.com/490918/pamela-colman-smith-pratt-institute-libraries/) are part of why her name is said out loud again.

## Why I teach with it {#why-i-teach-with-it}

Because the lenses I teach read straight off the drawing. The yellow sky behind the Fool and the Magician says joy and will before you know either card's name. The [Five of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/five-of-cups/) is, in my notes, the heaviest card in the deck: losing, dark emotion, alone with your problems. And yet Smith drew two cups still standing behind the cloaked figure, and a bridge leading to a house. You see the minus and the plus in one glance.

That's what a scene gives a beginner: something true to say before you've memorised anything. Cards aren't magic, you are — but a good drawing helps you get started.

The honest limit: the Rider-Waite-Smith isn't older, purer or more “authentic” than other decks. Its symbolism comes from a late-nineteenth-century system, and the Marseille pattern is a good two centuries older. If you're curious how they differ, I compare them in [Marseille or Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/), and if another deck speaks to you more, read with that one.
