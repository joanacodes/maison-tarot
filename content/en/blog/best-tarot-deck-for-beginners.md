The best tarot deck for a beginner is the Rider-Waite-Smith, the 1909 deck drawn by Pamela Colman Smith. Every one of its 78 cards has a scene you can read without a book. It's the deck I read with and the one I start everyone on; other decks come later, and I'll say when.

## Why the Rider-Waite-Smith first {#why-rws}

Every card has a scene, including the forty numbered cards from Ace to Ten. That's rarer than it sounds. In older decks like the Tarot de Marseille, the Five of Cups shows five cups among stylised flowers, and no people. In the Rider-Waite-Smith, a figure in a black cloak looks down at three spilled cups while two still stand behind them. Before opening any book, you know what the card is about: losing, a dark emotion, being alone with it — and something left standing that the figure isn't looking at.

The same goes for the cards around it.

The Five of Pentacles shows two figures in the snow passing a lit church window: poverty, cold, and the place where warmth could be found. The Eight of Swords shows a blindfolded figure surrounded by swords, the ropes loose: a prisoner of yourself while you hold every tool. Nobody needs to teach you those. You see them.

**A scene is something you can read the first evening; a symbol is something you have to learn first.** That's the whole reason I start people here.

It isn't the only deck people read well with. Plenty of readers learned on something else and read beautifully. The Rider-Waite-Smith is simply the shortest road from opening the box to saying something true about a card.

## The lenses are built in {#lenses}

The way I teach — through colours, numbers and elements instead of 78 memorised meanings — works best on this deck, because those things are visible on every card.

The colours are deliberate. Yellow fills the sky behind the Fool and the Magician: joy, will, self-expression. White dominates Temperance: peace, something unsoiled. Learn [the colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) and you're reading before you know a single card's name.

The numbers and suits are printed plainly, so the number tells you where a situation stands in its cycle and the suit tells you what it's about. And because most books, courses and videos use this deck, whatever you learn transfers. Its images are also in the public domain, which is why they appear on every card page of this site.

## Which edition to buy {#which-edition}

Look for the original Pamela Colman Smith drawings. Editions differ in colouring, card size and card stock, and any of them reads the same. Choose the one you can see clearly and that feels good when you shuffle. Size matters more than people expect: smaller cards are easier for small hands to shuffle, larger ones show the details to tired eyes.

Redrawn decks that keep the same scenes work too. Be aware that their colours often change, and with them one of your lenses; that's fine once you know what the original says.

There's no need to wait for someone to give you one. [Buying your own deck brings no bad luck](/blog/do-you-have-to-be-gifted-your-first-tarot-deck/), whatever you've heard. If you'd like a few specific decks to compare, there's [a shortlist of decks for beginners](/blog/best-tarot-decks/).

## What comes after: Marseille, Thoth, oracles {#what-comes-after}

The right moment for a second deck is when you can read a stranger from a single card without opening a book, and your notebook shows it. Before that, a second deck doubles the learning without doubling the reading. And it's a pleasure, not a required step: I still read with the Rider-Waite-Smith.

The Tarot de Marseille is the French tradition. Its numbered cards have no scenes, so you read them with the number and the element alone. It's the best test of whether the lenses have really sunk in, and [the two decks side by side](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/) show you exactly what changes.

The Thoth deck was designed by Aleister Crowley and painted by Frieda Harris. It's richly developed, realistic in its own way, and heavy with occult symbolism. I keep it on my own "to explore later" shelf. I'd keep it for the day you're curious about symbols for their own sake; [here's what changes when you switch](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/).

Oracle decks aren't tarot: any number of cards, with meanings set by their authors. Collecting decks is a bit like collecting art, each card a picture you can read a message in, and oracles can be lovely to own. They won't teach you tarot's structure, though. [The difference is explained here](/blog/tarot-vs-oracle-cards/).

## Start with one card {#start}

Open the deck, shuffle, draw one card, and describe the scene out loud before you look anything up. Who's there, what are they doing, what colour fills the card? That's a whole first evening.

Then make it a habit: [one card each morning](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/), three lines in a notebook, and the deck will teach you more than any book about it.
