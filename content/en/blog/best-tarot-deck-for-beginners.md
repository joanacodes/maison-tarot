The best tarot deck for a beginner is the Rider-Waite-Smith, the 1909 deck drawn by Pamela Colman Smith. All 78 of its cards are little scenes, and a scene speaks before any guidebook does. It's the deck I read with and the one I start everyone on; other decks come later, and I'll say when.

## Why the Rider-Waite-Smith first {#why-rws}

Every card has a scene, including the forty numbered cards from Ace to Ten. That's rarer than it sounds. In older decks like the Tarot de Marseille, the Five of Cups shows five cups among stylised flowers, and no people. In the Rider-Waite-Smith, a cloaked figure bows its head over three overturned cups, and two more stay upright behind it. Before opening any book, you know what the card is about: a loss, sorrow, and the loneliness that comes with them — and something left standing that the figure isn't looking at.

The same goes for the cards around it.

In the Five of Pentacles, a pair trudges through falling snow below the bright window of a church: want and cold outside, shelter within reach. The Eight of Swords shows a blindfolded woman ringed by swords, her arms tied but her feet free: caught by your own thinking while the means of escape are all within reach. Nobody needs to teach you those. You see them.

**A scene is something you can read the first evening; a symbol is something you have to learn first.** That's the whole reason I start people here.

It isn't the only deck people read well with. Plenty of readers learned on something else and read beautifully. The Rider-Waite-Smith is the shortest road I know from opening the box to saying something true about a card.

## The lenses are built in {#lenses}

The way I teach — through colours, numbers and elements instead of 78 memorised meanings — works best on this deck, because those things are visible on every card.

The colours are deliberate. Yellow fills the sky behind the Fool and the Magician: joy, will, self-expression. White dominates Temperance: peace, something unsoiled. Learn [the colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) and you're reading before you know a single card's name.

The numbers and suits are printed plainly, so the number tells you where a situation stands in its cycle and the suit tells you what it's about. And because most books, courses and videos use this deck, whatever you learn transfers. Its images are also in the public domain, which is why they appear on every card page of this site.

## Which edition to buy {#which-edition}

Any edition with Pamela Colman Smith's original drawings. The scenes are what you're buying; the box and the price don't change the reading. I've compared the main editions, their colouring and their card sizes in [a shortlist of decks for beginners](/blog/best-tarot-decks/), so here is only the rule that matters while you learn.

A redrawn deck that changes the scenes is no longer the deck you're learning. One that keeps the scenes but changes the colours costs you part of a lens until you know what the original says. Start with the original, and let the redraws wait.

There's no need to wait for someone to give you one, either. [Buying your own deck brings no bad luck](/blog/do-you-have-to-be-gifted-your-first-tarot-deck/), whatever you've heard.

## What comes after: Marseille, Thoth, oracles {#what-comes-after}

The right moment for a second deck is when you can read a stranger from a single card without opening a book, and your notebook shows it. Before that, a second deck doubles the learning without doubling the reading. And it's a pleasure, not a required step: I still read with the Rider-Waite-Smith.

Each of the others belongs to a different moment.

The Tarot de Marseille, the French tradition, is for the moment the scenes start doing too much of the work. Its numbered cards, like the Five of Cups above, have nobody in them, so the number and the element have to carry the reading alone. If they do, the lenses are yours. [The two decks side by side](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/) show exactly what you lose and what you keep.

The Thoth, which Frieda Harris painted to Aleister Crowley's design, is for the moment you want to know why a card is built the way it is. It's richly developed, realistic in its own way, and heavy with occult symbolism; in my own notes it sits under "to explore later". [Here's what changes when you switch](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/).

Oracle decks have no moment, because they aren't a step on the same road. They have any number of cards, with meanings set by each author. I see a shelf of oracle decks the way I'd see a small gallery, every card an artwork you can find a message in, so enjoy them whenever you like. What they can't give you is tarot's own frame of suits and numbers. [The difference is explained here](/blog/tarot-vs-oracle-cards/).

## Start with one card {#start}

Open the deck, shuffle, draw one card, and describe the scene out loud before you look anything up. Who's there, what are they doing, what colour fills the card? That's a whole first evening.

Then make it a habit: [one card each morning](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/), three lines in a notebook, and the deck will teach you more than any book about it.
