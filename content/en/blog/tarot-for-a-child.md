You can play tarot with a child. I wouldn't give one a reading. The pictures of the Rider-Waite-Smith deck are made for telling stories, and children are often better at describing them than adults are; what a child doesn't need is someone telling them what is about to happen in their life.

## Playing with the cards or reading for a child? {#play-or-reading}

A reading, the way I use the word, means looking at someone's cards and telling them something about their life: what's going on, what might come. Playing with the cards means using the pictures as pictures: describing them, making up stories, noticing colours. The first carries weight. The second carries none, and that's what makes it good for children.

Show a child the Sun. A small child rides a white horse, arms wide open, sunflowers behind a wall, a red banner flying. Ask what's happening and you'll get a story in ten seconds. Then the Six of Cups: one child offers another a cup full of white flowers in an old courtyard. Who's giving? Who's receiving? Are they friends?

That's the same skill I teach adults — look before you think — and children are often quicker at it, because nobody has told them yet that there's a correct meaning to recite. [The colour lens](/library/tarot-colours/) works beautifully at that age: "lots of yellow, it feels happy" is a real reading of a colour.

**Describing a picture together is play; telling a child what will happen to them is not.**

## Consent, for a child too {#consent}

Ask first. That's my rule with adults, and it doesn't change with age. You wouldn't want someone snooping in your bedroom; reading someone who hasn't agreed is the same thing. With a child, consent has two halves: the child says yes, and a parent knows. And the child can stop whenever they like, without giving a reason.

The same goes for reading a child from a distance — drawing cards about your son while he's at school. He isn't in the room and he hasn't agreed, and a question about someone who isn't there is one I don't read on for anyone, [your mother or your ex included](/blog/reading-tarot-about-someone-else/). If it's your child who worries you, there's a gentler question, and it's about you: what do I need to understand as a parent this week?

## Is there a right age? {#age}

I don't have a number, and I'd be wary of anyone who gives one as a rule. Instead I'd ask three things:

- Can they say no, and be heard?
- Do they understand that a picture is a story, not an announcement?
- Will they forget about it by dinner, or take it to bed with them?

A six-year-old making up a story about the Sun card is playing. A thirteen-year-old asking whether a classmate likes them is asking a real question, and it's about someone who isn't in the room. With teenagers, I'd keep the same lines I keep with adults, gently: health, death and other people's feelings stay off the table, and nothing is read as a verdict. Teenagers are often drawn to tarot for good reasons, and [why young people are turning to it](/blog/young-people-and-tarot/) is worth understanding before saying no.

## The cards that might frighten them {#scary-cards}

Death, the Tower, the Ten of Swords: some pictures are hard, and a child will ask about them. Answer plainly. In my notes, Death isn't about dying at all; it's tied to the harvest, to starting again. The Tower is a building struck by lightning, something that looked solid being shaken. You can explain that the way you'd explain a frightening page in a picture book: something ends, something new begins.

If a card upsets them, put it away and don't make it mean anything. [The scariest cards are kinder than they look](/blog/scariest-tarot-cards/), but a child shouldn't have to take that on trust at bedtime.

## What to teach instead of the future {#what-to-teach}

If a child takes one thing away, I'd want it to be this: the cards don't decide anything. They're pictures, and the stories come from the person looking. Teach that first, and you've given them good protection against anyone who later tries to use a card to tell them what to do. [Tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/), at eight or at eighty.

And please don't use a reading to steer a child — "the cards say you should be nicer to your sister." It works once, and it teaches the wrong lesson for years.

For a first game, start where I'd start anyone, at any age: one card, describe it, make up the story, and laugh when it goes somewhere strange. There's more on [what I learned making friends and family try](/blog/how-to-teach-tarot/), and most of it applies to a curious child.
