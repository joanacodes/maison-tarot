You can share tarot with a child; a reading is another matter. One approach that works well is to keep to play: the pictures of the Rider-Waite-Smith deck are made for telling stories, and describing them is a real skill at any age. What a child doesn’t need is someone telling them what is about to happen in their life.

At Maison Tarot, readings are for adults only. So this page is for the parent, aunt or friend holding the deck at home.

## Playing with the cards or reading for a child? {#play-or-reading}

A reading, the way I use the word, means looking at someone’s cards and telling them something about their life: what’s going on, what might come. Playing with the cards means using the pictures as pictures: describing them, making up stories, noticing colours. The first carries weight. The second carries almost none, which is what makes it a good fit for children.

Show a child the Sun. A small child rides a white horse, arms wide open, sunflowers behind a wall, a red banner flying. Ask what’s happening and you may well get a story in ten seconds. Then the Six of Cups: one child offers another a cup full of white flowers in an old courtyard. Who’s giving? Who’s receiving? Are they friends?

It’s the same first exercise I give adults: one card, then describe what’s on it, what you see, what you receive. A child who hasn’t yet heard that there’s a correct meaning to recite may find it more natural than many grown-ups do. [The colour lens](/library/tarot-colours/) suits that age well: “lots of yellow, it feels happy” is a real reading of a colour.

**Describing a picture together is play; telling a child what will happen to them is something else.**

## Consent, for a child too {#consent}

I always ask before reading anyone. You wouldn’t want someone snooping in your bedroom; reading someone who hasn’t agreed feels the same to me. Age doesn’t change that principle, even for a game. A sensible way to apply it with a child: the child says yes, a parent knows, and the child can stop whenever they like without giving a reason.

The same goes for drawing cards about a child from a distance, about your son while he’s at school. He isn’t in the room and he hasn’t agreed. I don’t read on people who aren’t in the room, [your mother or your ex included](/blog/reading-tarot-about-someone-else/), and the Maison Tarot rules say [no readings for minors, and none about them](/disclaimers/#adults). If it’s your child who worries you, there’s a gentler question, and it’s about you: what do I need to understand as a parent this week?

## Is there a right age? {#age}

For readings at Maison Tarot, the age is eighteen. For play at home, a single number helps less than you’d think, because children differ so much. Rather than an age, it can help to ask three things:

- Can they say no, and be heard?
- Do they understand that a picture is a story, not an announcement?
- Will they forget about it by dinner, or take it to bed with them?

A six-year-old making up a story about the Sun card is playing. A thirteen-year-old asking whether a classmate likes them is asking a real question, and it’s about someone who isn’t in the room. If a teenager at home wants to learn, one way to handle it is to pass on the lines I keep with adults, gently: health, death and other people’s feelings stay off the table, and nothing is treated as a verdict. Teenagers can be drawn to tarot for good reasons, and [why young people are turning to it](/blog/young-people-and-tarot/) is worth understanding before saying no.

## The cards that might frighten them {#scary-cards}

Death, the Tower, the Ten of Swords: some pictures are hard, and a child will ask about them. Answer plainly. In my notes, Death isn’t about dying at all; it’s tied to the harvest, to starting again. The Tower is a building struck by lightning, something that looked solid being shaken. You can explain that the way you’d explain a frightening page in a picture book: something ends, something new begins.

If a card upsets them, put it away and don’t make it mean anything. [The scariest cards are kinder than they look](/blog/scariest-tarot-cards/), but a child shouldn’t have to take that on trust at bedtime.

## What to teach instead of the future {#what-to-teach}

If a child takes one thing away, I’d want it to be this: the cards don’t decide anything. They’re pictures, and the stories come from the person looking. Teach that first, and you’ve given them good protection against anyone who later tries to use a card to tell them what to do. [Tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/), at eight or at eighty.

It’s also worth resisting the urge to use a card to steer a child: “the cards say you should be nicer to your sister.” It may work once, and it teaches the wrong lesson for years.

For a first game: one card, describe it, make up the story, and laugh when it goes somewhere strange. There’s more on [what I learned making friends and family try](/blog/how-to-teach-tarot/), and most of it applies to a curious child.
