In the moment, you can't, and you're not meant to. The feeling of making it up is what reading feels like from the inside. You find out afterwards, on paper: what you said in one column, what was confirmed in the other.

## Why reading feels like making it up {#why-it-feels-made-up}

Impressions arrive in your own voice. There's no special tone, no bell that rings when something is real. A word comes, an image, a sense that this is about a sister, and it feels exactly like something you thought up. It is something you thought; the question is where the thought came from.

The cards help in a modest way. They give your eyes somewhere to go, so your own thoughts and assumptions step aside long enough for something else to come through. Some days it works. Some days your own story stays in front.

**Feeling as if you're making it up is not evidence that you are.** Everyone who reads asks this question. The readers I'd worry about are the ones who never do.

## The sentence that helps: “I'm about to talk nonsense” {#the-sentence}

Before every card, I tell myself: *I'm about to talk nonsense.* It's the heart of [my method for beginners](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/), and it belongs here too, because it answers this question from the other side.

Real invention, the kind worth worrying about, comes from wanting to be right. You see the person's face, you guess what they'd like to hear, you round off a detail so it fits. That isn't reading; it's steering. The sentence removes the need to be right. Once you've accepted in advance that you may talk nonsense, there's nothing left to steer towards. You say what comes, odd bits included.

**Inventing is what you do to be right; reading is what you say before you know whether you are.**

## Four checks: reading or making it up? {#four-checks}

You can't feel the difference, but you can check four things.

1. Did you know anything beforehand? If the person told you their question, everything you said may be a rewording of it. That's why I ask people to tell me nothing: [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/) exists to answer exactly this.
2. Could it be true of anyone? "You've been through changes lately" fits everybody alive. The Barnum effect is our tendency to accept a vague, general statement as a precise description of ourselves, and it's [the most common way of making things up without noticing](/blog/barnum-effect-tarot/).
3. Did you adjust as you went? If you watched their face and bent the reading towards every nod, you were reading their face.
4. Did it surprise you? The details worth noticing are often the ones you'd never have chosen: too specific, too odd or too unflattering to invent to please someone.

Say you draw the Eight of Swords: a figure bound and blindfolded, swords all around, the way out open behind her. "You feel a bit stuck" is true of most people on most days. "You're waiting for permission you already have" could be wrong — and that's what makes it worth saying, and worth checking.

My first reading for a stranger had one of those. It came out as a story about a relationship, with a detail neither of us liked: she hadn't been especially kind to the other person. She told me it didn't resonate; she wasn't looking for a relationship. Three or four days later she texted me, shocked. Everything had happened, that part included. I'd never have made up that detail to please a stranger. [The whole story](/blog/first-tarot-reading-story/) has its own page.

## The notebook: the only real answer {#the-notebook}

The honest answer to "am I making it up?" is: let's count.

1. Rule a line down the middle of a page.
2. On the left, write what you said, in your words, before the person responds.
3. On the right, write what they confirmed, in their words.
4. Mark each reading: held, or missed. No third box.
5. Every ten readings, count, and look at what the misses have in common.

I kept that notebook through all my free blind readings for strangers. By my own count, about nine in ten held and about one in ten missed. It's a tally kept by the person doing the readings, not a study, and it says nothing about how tarot works. What it tells me is that I wasn't only making it up — or not nine times out of ten. [How to keep a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) is explained step by step elsewhere.

A miss is a miss. You don't rescue it with a second card, and you don't reframe it into "you'll understand later". If the person comes back on their own days afterwards, add their message with the date. Don't go asking. There's more on [what to do when a reading is wrong](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/), and it's mostly about saying so out loud.

## What if the notebook says you are? {#if-you-miss}

Then you've learned something, and it isn't a verdict on you. Look at the misses. Are they all about timing? All on long evenings? All with people who had told you something first? Change one thing — say less, stop watching faces, draw fewer cards, rest — and count again.

The notebook also catches the opposite habit: the reader who never writes a miss down. If your right-hand column is full of "sort of" and "maybe", you're protecting yourself, not checking. Be as gentle with yourself as you'd be with a friend, and as honest.

And if, after an honest stretch, most of it still misses, you're free to stop, or to keep reading for the pleasure of the pictures. Neither is a failure.

The quickest way to fill the notebook is to read for people who'll tell you nothing. That's what the [free events](/events/) are for: cards drawn at random, nobody explains anything first, and you compare afterwards.
