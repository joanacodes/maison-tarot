Reading in front of twenty people doesn't change the cards. It changes the room: who has agreed to what, what can be said out loud, and how calm you need to stay while your body, sometimes, isn't. I've read in public in Facebook lives and at Maison Tarot's free events, and here's what I keep in mind.

## What an audience changes {#what-changes}

Everyone hears everything. In a one-to-one reading, a sentence lands on one person, who can take it home and think it over. In public, it lands in front of friends or strangers, and the person can't un-hear it with everyone looking.

The pressure goes both ways. My notes say that sometimes you can't see anything in the cards, for one of two reasons: your own block, from too much pressure, or the person's, because they're afraid of what they might hear. An audience adds to both. Expect a slower start, and don't fill the gap with guesses.

The silence rule helps more in public than anywhere else. The person gives no question and no context, so the room knows exactly as much as I do. When something lands, everyone can see it wasn't fed to me first. [Why I ask people to say nothing](/blog/the-silence-rule/) is its own article.

## Consent, twice {#consent}

I always ask permission before reading someone, wherever we are. In public, consent comes twice: to being read at all, and to being read in front of others. [The note on events](/disclaimers/#events) says it plainly: a reading in front of others is a reading in front of others, so come only if that suits you.

At Maison Tarot's events, people register and are drawn at random, so they've opted in. I still check at the table, in a sentence that makes saying no painless: "You're happy to be read here, with everyone listening? It's completely fine if not."

What I don't do is read someone in the audience who hasn't volunteered, even as a joke. Reading someone without their consent is like snooping in their bedroom. And someone who doesn't know they're being read isn't open, so you catch half a sentence and build a statement on it.

## What's said aloud, and what waits {#aloud-or-private}

Not everything a card shows belongs to the room. **The shape of the reading is public; the details belong to the person.**

Say the Three of Swords comes up for someone at an evening event. Out loud, you describe the card and its general direction: "There's a hurt here, something close to the heart. I have more detail. Would you like it now, or afterwards, between the two of us?" Let them choose. Most people know at once.

Online, set up the private channel before you start: a direct message after the live, or a few minutes at the end of the call. If someone becomes emotional, offer to finish away from the others; [what to do when someone cries](/blog/someone-cries-during-reading/) covers the rest.

The subjects I don't read on stay off the table in public too: health, death, pregnancy, court cases, money decisions. A free reading in front of a crowd is the worst place to get one of those wrong.

## How to decline a question kindly in front of everyone {#declining}

Questions from the room are part of the fun, and sometimes one lands on a subject you don't read on. The steps are the same as in private — acknowledge why they ask, explain why the cards aren't the tool, offer something else — but shorter and warmer, because twenty people are watching how you treat the person who asked.

- "Will my mum get better?" — "I can hear how much you love her. That one's for her doctors, not for a card. If you like, we can look at how you're holding up."
- "Can you read my friend over there?" — "Only if she'd like to be read. Ask her, and if she's up for it, she can put her name in."
- "Is my ex coming back?" — "I'd rather read you than him; he isn't here to say yes. Shall we look at what you want?"

Thank them for asking, and move on without a lecture. Nobody should feel told off in public for a question that came from worry. I've gathered [more ways to point someone elsewhere kindly](/blog/client-boundaries-tarot/) for readers.

## A shaking body and a calm voice {#staying-calm}

When the audience is behind a screen rather than in the room, [reading live](/blog/tarot-live-instagram/) adds problems of its own, from lag to a comment stream, and I've written about them separately. In a room, it's mostly your body you'll notice.

Your hands may shake, your heart may race, your voice may speed up. That's normal, and it doesn't mean the reading will go wrong. What helps when it's you at the table:

- Slow your voice down. Nerves speed you up, and the room hears calm before it hears content.
- Start with what's on the card, not what it means. A description is always true, and it gives your nerves time to settle.
- Say "nothing's coming yet" if nothing is. It's honest, and it lowers the pressure for both of you.
- Say a miss out loud. Misses will happen, and twenty people learn more from an honest "that one's a miss" than from a rescued reading.
- Stop before you're drained. Interpreting takes energy, and you'll feel it afterwards.

## Before your first public reading {#before}

Say the rules out loud at the start: the person says nothing about themselves first, these subjects stay off the table, misses happen and you'll say so, and anyone can stop at any moment. Then the room knows what to expect, and so do you.
