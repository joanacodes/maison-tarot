A one-card pull, the card of the day, is the best daily practice I know for learning tarot. It makes you look at one image long enough to read it instead of reciting it. It stays a practice while you're the one deciding, and it becomes a crutch the day you draw to be told what to do. Here's how to tell the difference.

## How to draw your card of the day {#how-to-draw}

1. Pick a moment that's the same every day. The first coffee works well.
2. Shuffle in whatever way your hands already know.
3. Draw one card. No question, or a light one: "What's worth noticing today?"
4. Look for a full minute before you think. Then put it into words: what's drawn, what catches your eye, what it stirs in you.
5. Write three lines, then put the deck away.

That's the whole ritual. Nothing to cleanse, nothing to recite, no special cloth. If you've never read a single card before, [your first pull, step by step](/blog/how-to-do-a-tarot-reading/) takes about ten minutes.

Some mornings the card will look heavy: the Tower, the Ten of Swords. It isn't a forecast for your day. Every card has a plus and a minus, and a heavy one is read like any other — what's on it, what you see. The Ten of Swords, for instance, has a dawn breaking behind the figure on the ground.

## What to write down {#what-to-note}

In the morning, the card, then what you notice through the lenses: the colour that dominates, the number, the element. In the evening, what the day held and whether the morning note fits. Write the evening line before rereading the morning one, or the day will politely rearrange itself to match.

Say Tuesday's card is the Four of Wands. Morning: "Yellow everywhere, a garland, two figures raising bouquets, a castle behind. Four, Fire: something built and steady, a home, a celebration." The evening line might be "Housewarming at a friend's new flat", or it might be "Nothing like that today". Both are useful. The second one is the line that teaches you something.

The four in that note comes from the [number grid](/library/numerology/): four is two doubled, the moment after an agreement when things are stable and structured. Those two columns, morning and evening, are the same ones I use for readings in my [tarot journal](/blog/tarot-journal/).

## Why one card teaches more than a spread {#why-one-card}

**One card read properly teaches more than ten cards recited.** In a spread, half your attention goes to "what does position three stand for". With one card, all of it goes to the image.

You also meet the whole deck. A year of daily pulls is 365 draws across 78 cards, so on average you see each card four or five times, on different days and in different moods. That's how you learn a card's range: the same image reads differently from one day to the next.

Spreads come later. Once a single card reads comfortably, three is the natural next step, and the [three-card spread](/blog/three-card-tarot-spread/) is where I'd go.

## Practice or crutch: a test {#practice-or-crutch}

Same deck, same gesture; the difference is what you do with the card. Answer honestly:

- Do you draw more than once a day?
- Do you draw again when you don't like the card?
- Do you wait for a card before ordinary decisions, like whether to send a message?
- Do you feel uneasy on a day without a card?
- Is your morning question the same worry, every day?

If two or more answers are yes, the card has drifted from practice to crutch. That isn't a weakness. It's usually doubt looking for something to lean on, and doubt deserves kindness. But [tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/), and a card can't carry a decision for you.

The fix is a pause. Put the deck in a drawer for a week, or a month, and notice what you reach for instead. I've written about [why that pause helps](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/).

## What a year of daily cards gives you {#a-year}

Nobody can promise what your year will show. What it gives you, mechanically, is this:

- 365 small readings, each with a written check.
- Four or five meetings with each card on average, in different seasons of your life.
- A log that shows patterns: the card that keeps coming back, the ones you'd rather not look at, the weeks you reached for the deck more often.
- A few pauses, ideally, written in too.

What it doesn't give you is practice with another person. Your own day is a generous judge, and you know your own story too well. At some point, find someone who'll tell you nothing and read for them.

## Start tomorrow morning {#start}

Leave the deck next to the kettle tonight. Tomorrow: one card, one minute, three lines. In the evening, the fourth line.

After seven days, reread the week in one sitting. Seven cards side by side, with seven evenings next to them, show you more about how you read than any single morning can.
