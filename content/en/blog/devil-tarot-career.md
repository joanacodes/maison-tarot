The Devil in a career reading is a job that holds you more than you hold it: the salary you can’t imagine giving up, the title that became your identity, the hours that crept up until they were your whole week. It doesn’t tell you to quit. It shows you the chains — and on the card, they’re loose enough to lift off.

## The Devil through its lenses {#lenses}

A horned creature with bat’s wings perches on a black block. Chains run from a ring on the block to the necks of a naked man and woman, and they hang loose over their shoulders. Nobody holds the other end; there’s no lock and no guard. The background is black from edge to edge.

The number: 15 becomes 1 + 5 = [6](/library/numerology/number-6/), success running fast. Its advice is to stay calm and humble while things go well; the Devil is the 6 that forgot. In the Instagram story I use for the numbers, 6 is the moment Facebook invests: everything speeds up, and nobody reads the conditions any more. That’s the Devil at work in one line — a deal that pays, and small print you stopped reading.

The element is Earth: matter, money, whatever has value for you, and in my notes tenacity, endurance — and stubbornness. Earth holds you by what you value, which is why it’s so hard to see it as a chain. Not all riches are gold: status, security and other people’s approval hold as tightly as a salary.

The astrology, if it speaks to you, is [Capricorn](/library/astrology/capricorn/): “I use”, the goat whose horns this creature wears. Among my notes on Capricorn is Miranda Priestly, the boss in *The Devil Wears Prada*: ambition, structure, and a job that can swallow the person doing it. Well placed, Capricorn is responsibility taken; badly placed, the tool that became the master. The colours: black for reality and heavy thoughts, the orange and red of the torch for a desire that keeps burning. Every lens is on [the Devil’s own page](/library/tarot-cards/the-devil/).

## What the Devil says about work {#work}

Attachment at work takes many shapes. The golden handcuffs: a salary or a bonus that makes leaving feel impossible. The identity: the job title that became the answer to “who are you?”. The habit: answering emails at eleven at night, not because anyone asked, but because stopping feels dangerous. The approval: a boss whose praise you’ve been working for since your first week.

Every card has a plus and a minus, and the Devil’s plus is real at work: desire, appetite, the drive that builds careers. Its minus is when the wanting runs you instead of the other way round.

Say someone has stayed eight years in a job they describe as “fine” and draws the Devil. The card doesn’t say the job is bad. It asks what, exactly, keeps them there — and whether that could be found elsewhere, or loosened from inside. **The question isn’t “what’s wrong with you?” but “what are you holding that you could put down?”**

If the hours or the pressure have started to cost you your sleep or your health, that’s a door for a doctor before it’s one for a card.

## The Devil reversed at work {#reversed}

Reversed, the Devil is often the chain starting to slip. You notice the loop and lift a hand to it: the first evening you leave at six, and the building is still standing the next morning.

Or it’s the same attachment in excess: working more so you don’t have to look at it, hiding it better, calling the overtime a choice.

## The Devil next to other cards {#combinations}

{{< cards "sw08 pe04 cu08" >}}

Next to the Eight of Swords — a woman bound and blindfolded among swords — my note is a prisoner of yourself while you hold every tool. With the Devil, it says twice that the way out is already in your hands.

Next to the Four of Pentacles, a man hugging one pentacle to his chest with two more under his feet, it’s the job kept out of fear of losing it rather than for what it gives you.

Next to the Eight of Cups, which in my notes is moving on from what you’re already doing, it doesn’t say leave. It shows that leaving is possible, and that the decision is yours. If the real question is between two paths, I’ve written about [the Lovers in a career reading](/blog/lovers-tarot-career/), the Devil’s other 6.

## “Should I quit?” — and a kinder question {#question}

People often bring this card with that question, and they’re usually tired when they do. I understand: when something holds you, you want someone to give you permission to go, or a good reason to stay.

I don’t tell anyone to quit or to stay. Whether you can afford a change is a money decision, and I don’t read on those ([what I don’t read, and why](/disclaimers/#refusals)). The people involved aren’t in the room. And a card that told you “leave” would become one more chain: a voice outside you, deciding. Cards aren’t magic, you are — and the Devil shows it more clearly than any other card, since nobody holds the other end.

A kinder question: “What is this job giving me that I’m afraid of losing, and where else could I find it?” Or: “Which link could I loosen this month without risking anything?”

## How to loosen it {#loosen}

1. Name the chain in one word: money, status, identity, fear, approval.
2. Ask what it gives you. Every chain gives something — safety, a sense of worth. You’re not weak for wanting it.
3. Loosen one link, not all of them: one boundary on your hours, one evening without email, one honest conversation, an updated CV that stays in a drawer. Lifting the chain doesn’t have to mean walking out.
4. Check the practical side with the right people: a budget, an adviser, a friend who’ll be honest with you.
5. Write down what changes in a [reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/), and look again in a month. It’s how you see a chain move.
