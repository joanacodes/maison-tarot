People to read for are easier to find than you’d think: online groups where people ask for readings, strangers who want a message, friends willing to play by the rules, free events. Wherever you find them, two conditions don’t change. They say yes before you read, and they tell you nothing until you’ve finished.

## Online groups where people ask for readings {#online-groups}

My first reading for a stranger happened on a Discord group I’d found through Tumblr. I asked who wanted a reading, someone said yes, and I had nothing but a username. The reading came out about a relationship; she said it didn’t resonate, because she wasn’t looking for one. Three or four days later she texted me, shocked: it had all happened. I tell the whole story in [my first reading was “wrong”](/blog/first-tarot-reading-story/).

A few months after I started, I was reading in Facebook tarot groups too, and doing lives there. Groups like these exist because people want readings and readers want practice, and both sides know it.

Before you offer, read the group’s rules about readings, and say that you’re learning. Being honest about where you are is the surest way to be welcome, and it takes the pressure off you too.

## Letters to strangers {#letters}

The most unusual place I practised was a pen-pal app. I wrote “letters” to strangers who had asked for messages, and the message came from the cards.

Writing is a good school. You have no face to watch and no reaction to adjust to, so you can’t steer by the person’s expression. It’s the silence rule in its purest form. If you try it, keep each letter short — the cards you drew, what you see, what you receive — and ask for a reply, even two lines. Without one, your notebook has no second column.

One man wrote back asking whether I could read minds, or whether I was a magician. It made me laugh. It’s also a fair question from someone who has received a letter about his life from a person he’s never met — which is one more reason to say clearly, from the start, what you’re doing and why.

## Friends and family, with the rules {#friends}

At first I read for around a hundred people, before the long run of blind readings for strangers. I also made my own friends and family try reading, which is how I found out I wanted to teach. Friends are willing, patient and honest. Their one problem is that you know their lives: you can’t always tell whether you’re reading the card or remembering last Sunday’s conversation.

So ask them for two things. First, tell you nothing — not the question, not even the area of life. Second, be honest afterwards, including “no, that doesn’t fit at all”. A friend who says yes to everything to be nice is a kind friend and a useless check. Why I hold to the first rule so firmly is in [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/).

## How to ask, and what to agree on {#how-to-ask}

Reading someone without asking is like walking into their bedroom and opening the drawers. It’s also less accurate: someone who doesn’t know they’re being read isn’t open, and you end up building a whole statement on half a sentence. Consent comes first, every time.

You can keep it to one sentence: “I’m learning tarot and practising with volunteers; it’s free, don’t tell me your question, and tell me afterwards what landed and what didn’t.” Behind that sentence, here is what I’d agree on before you start:

1. Say you’re practising, and that it’s free.
2. Ask for no question and no context; they keep it to themselves.
3. Agree that they’ll tell you, afterwards, what landed and what didn’t.
4. Say what you don’t read on: health, death, pregnancy, court cases, money decisions, people who aren’t there. These are [the subjects I step back from](/disclaimers/#refusals), and why.
5. Write it down: what you said, what they confirmed.

**Consent and silence are the two things that turn a favour into practice.** Without them, you’re having a pleasant conversation; with them, you’re learning something about how you read.

## Free events where readers meet {#events}

If you’d rather practise somewhere set up for it, Maison Tarot runs [free events](/events/), online and in person. The in-person meetups are where we read, compare and talk. The online evenings are for watching first: a few people drawn at random each get a reading in front of the others, which is a good way to see the silence rule at work before you try it yourself.

## Before the first person sits down {#before-you-start}

You don’t need to be ready. Every person I’ve helped to read managed it on the first to third try, and a handful of real readings will teach you things that practice alone can’t. Until you find your first person, the drills in [practising tarot with nobody to read for](/blog/practise-tarot-alone/) will keep your eye sharp.

When someone says yes, the basics of reading for another person with care are in [how to read tarot for someone else](/blog/read-tarot-for-someone-else/).
