“For entertainment purposes only” means the reader promises no outcome and gives no advice you should follow in place of a professional’s. People often read it as a wink, or as a legal shield. I read it as the most honest sentence on a reader’s page, and it’s on mine.

## What the phrase means {#what-it-means}

It’s a disclaimer: a statement that the service guarantees no result and doesn’t replace medical, legal, financial or psychological advice. You’ll find it at the bottom of readers’ websites, on price lists and booking pages, usually in a smaller font than everything else.

At Maison Tarot it sits at the top of [the disclaimers page](/disclaimers/#entertainment): sessions, workshops and readings are for entertainment purposes only, no outcome is guaranteed, and none of it stands in for a doctor, a lawyer, a financial adviser or a therapist.

What it doesn’t mean is “this is a joke”. A film is entertainment, and a good one can change how you see your week. A reading can do the same: it holds your attention for a while, shows you something, and then it stops. The English word comes from the French *entretenir*, to hold together, and I like that meaning better than the one with a laugh track.

## Where the habit comes from {#where-it-comes-from}

In the UK, readers often explain the habit with an old law aimed at fake mediums, the Fraudulent Mediums Act 1951. What I can say for certain is that the Act [was repealed in 2008](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo6/14-15/33/introduction) and replaced by the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, general rules for every trader rather than a law about mediums. The phrase outlived the law it’s remembered for.

The rest of that story, France included, and what has changed since, is in [is tarot reading legal?](/blog/is-tarot-reading-legal/) I read cards; I’m not a lawyer, and this isn’t legal advice.

## What it doesn’t do {#what-it-doesnt-do}

It doesn’t make a misleading sale fair. A reader who prints “for entertainment only” at the bottom of the page and then finds a curse at the end of your reading is still selling you [a curse removal](/blog/lift-the-curse-scam/). The footer and the pitch can’t both be true.

It isn’t a quality label either. A careful reader and a careless one can carry the same sentence, word for word. And it doesn’t make the subject trivial: people bring real worries to a reading, and the phrase is no excuse for treating them lightly.

**The phrase is a promise about what the reader won’t claim, not a licence for what they can sell.**

## Why I like it {#why-i-like-it}

Because it’s true. Nobody knows how tarot works, me included. I count my readings, and about one in ten misses, by my own tally: a notebook, not a study. Claiming more than “a reading may show you something” would be pretending, and “entertainment” is a promise not to pretend.

Because it hands the decision back. A reading isn’t an instruction; you act on what you feel, not on what I said. I read; you decide. The phrase puts that in writing, where both of us can see it.

I can tell them what I see: a figure walking away at night from eight cups that are still standing, moving on from something that no longer fills them. Whether that’s a job, a flat or nothing at all, and whether they should leave anything, is theirs to decide. The long version of that principle is in [tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/).

And because it protects the person more than the reader. Someone who hears “the cards say leave him” may leave him. Someone who hears “here’s what the cards show, and the choice is yours” goes home with their choice intact. That’s also why some subjects stay out of my readings altogether: health, death, pregnancy, ongoing court cases, money decisions. A wrong word costs too much there, entertainment or not. What a reading can hold instead is how you’re living through it.

## How to read it on someone else’s page {#reading-it}

Check whether the rest of the page agrees with the small print. A page that says “entertainment only” and also promises “he’ll be back by spring” contradicts itself, and the footer is the part to believe. A few things to look for:

- No guaranteed outcomes, and no dates for events in your life.
- A list of subjects the reader leaves out.
- A stated price, with nothing added at the end.
- No urgency: nothing that has to be done tonight.

If those match the sentence, the reader means it. If they don’t, [the nine red flags of a fake reader](/blog/how-to-spot-a-fake-tarot-reader/) will tell you what you’re looking at.
