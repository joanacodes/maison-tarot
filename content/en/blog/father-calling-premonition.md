One night in 2019, while I was living in London, I heard my father call my name, as if he were in the next room. He was in Paris, at his Buddhist temple, praying and thinking of me. I can tell you what happened. I can’t tell you what it was, and I’d rather not pretend I can.

## What happened {#what-happened}

I was living in London. One night, I heard my father call my name. Not a voice from nowhere: his voice, and it seemed to come from the next room.

He wasn’t in the next room. He was in Paris, about 340 km away, with the Channel in between. He was at his Buddhist temple, praying, and thinking of me.

“Another country” sounds far, and in stories like this the distance tends to grow with each retelling. So here is the real number: about 340 km. Close enough for a train journey of a few hours, far enough that no voice carries.

That’s the whole of it, and I want to keep it at that size. No sentence, no warning, nothing to decode: only my name, and a father thinking of his daughter in another country.

## What people call an experience like this {#what-to-call-it}

There are a few words for it, and each one is worth defining plainly.

A premonition is a sense of something before it happens. Say you feel uneasy about a trip for no reason, and the trip is cancelled the next day. Strictly speaking, mine wasn’t one: nothing was announced in advance. It happened at the moment he was thinking of me.

Telepathy is the idea that one mind can reach another directly, without words and without any of the five senses. Two people far apart think of each other at the same instant, and one of them knows it.

Some people call it a sign, or a connection: a moment where love seems to cross a distance it shouldn’t be able to cross.

I don’t use any of these words for what happened. Not because they’re wrong, but because each one already carries an explanation, and I don’t have one.

## Three honest readings of it {#three-readings}

When I look at it calmly, I can see at least three ways to read it. None of them is complete.

*Memory.* A voice you’ve heard all your life lives in you. When you’re far from someone, your mind can bring their voice forward — the way you can hear exactly how a parent says your name when you read a message from them. Hearing your name called when nobody is there isn’t rare; plenty of people describe it, often when they’re tired or drifting off. This reading needs nothing extraordinary. Its weak point: it explains the voice, not the timing.

*Timing.* A parent with a child abroad thinks of them often. Most of those moments pass without anyone noticing. Now and then one lines up with something on the other side, and that’s the one we remember; nobody tells the story of the day they heard nothing. Its weak point: it can explain almost anything, which means it can’t really be tested.

*Something more.* That two people who love each other can reach each other across a distance in ways we don’t understand. My working definition of [magic](/blog/what-is-magic/) is what we’re sure is there without being able to say how it works, and this sits close to that. Its weak point: I can neither prove it nor rule it out.

**I don’t have to choose one of these readings to keep the experience.**

## Why I leave it open {#leave-it-open}

This story doesn’t have a spirit in it. There’s no ghost here: only my father, in Paris, at his temple, thinking of me. When people hear a story like this, they often reach for the most dramatic explanation available. I’d rather keep it at its real size: strange, and small.

Forcing a conclusion costs something either way. Turn it into proof, and you start building beliefs on a single moment, and waiting for the next one. Dismiss it, and you throw away something that mattered. Leaving it open isn’t indecision. It’s the most accurate thing I can say.

Stories like this also grow every time they’re told: the voice gets clearer, the timing more exact, the meaning bigger. I’ve tried to tell it here as it is, with the gaps left visible. If one day I understand more, I’ll add it. Until then, the gaps are part of the story too.

For warnings I can’t check, I keep a holding shelf: doubted, not discarded, and if one ever comes true, I’ll know I was told. [Why a reading can be wrong](/blog/why-a-reading-can-be-wrong/) tells where that habit comes from. This wasn’t a warning, so that shelf doesn’t quite fit. If you have an experience like mine, I’d suggest a different one: things that happened, that you can’t explain, and that you don’t need to.

There’s a lesson for reading here too. What arrived was tiny: a name. Most of what comes in a reading is small like that, and the discipline is the same. Say what happened, keep it apart from what you think it means, and let the meaning wait.

## If something like this happens to you {#if-it-happens}

1. Write it down straight away, with the date and the time, before you check anything. A [reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) works for this as well as for cards.
2. If you want to know, ask the person what they were doing. Ask kindly, and before you tell them what you heard, so their answer isn’t shaped by yours.
3. Keep the two apart in your notes: what you experienced, and what you learned afterwards.
4. Don’t make decisions on it. One moment is a moment, not a rule.
5. If it happens often, frightens you, or comes with other things that worry you, talk to a doctor. Hearing it wasn’t a mistake; it’s that some experiences like this have causes a doctor can help with, and checking is a kindness to yourself. Sometimes [a therapist’s door is the right one](/disclaimers/#therapy).

Read them the way I’d like you to read this one: as things that happened, told at their real size.
