Yes, you can read tarot for yourself, and most readers start there. The difficulty isn’t luck or energy; it’s honesty. When you read your own cards you know the question, the context and the answer you’re hoping for, so the one rule that keeps my readings for other people honest can’t apply. What follows are the habits that make up for it.

## Can you read tarot for yourself? {#can-you}

Yes. Reading for yourself means shuffling, drawing and interpreting cards about your own life, with nobody else at the table. It’s allowed, it’s common, and it’s how I tell beginners to start.

You may have heard otherwise: that it’s bad luck, that someone else has to shuffle, that you can’t read for yourself because you’re too close. The first two are habits some readers keep, not rules. The third has some truth in it, and the rest of this article is about that truth.

The way in is small. Start with a single card each morning and put it into plain words: the drawing, the detail that catches you, the feeling it leaves. Spreads can wait until single cards feel familiar. Say you draw the Page of Pentacles on a Tuesday morning: a young man standing in a green field, holding up a single pentacle and looking at it closely. You don’t need a book to say “today, I’m looking carefully at one thing”. That’s a reading. A small one, and yours.

What reading for yourself gives you: practice every day without needing anyone, a way to learn the deck card by card, and — used lightly — a check-up on your own mood and mindset while you keep your feet on the ground. What it doesn’t give you is anyone to tell you that you’re wrong.

## The honest problem: you already know the question {#the-problem}

When I read for someone, I ask them to tell me nothing: no question, no context. That’s how I know afterwards whether I read them or my assumptions about them, which is the whole point of [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/).

With yourself, there’s no silence. You know the question, you know the story so far, and you know what you want to hear. Your mind builds the story faster than your eyes reach the card. It usually goes wrong in one of two directions: you read your hope, or you read your fear.

Say you’ve been hoping for a sign to leave the city you live in, and you draw the Eight of Cups: someone with their back to you, leaving a stack of cups behind and climbing into the hills by moonlight. You’ll read “leave” in half a second. Maybe that’s right. But you’d have read “leave” in almost any card.

Now say you’re dreading a conversation, and you draw the Tower: lightning, the crown knocked off the top of a tower, two figures falling. You’ll read “disaster”. Yet the Tower can be the relief of something finally said — a structure that had been cracked for months coming down, so you can stop holding it up.

## How to stay honest: seven habits {#habits}

None of these is complicated. Together they stand in for the person across the table.

1. *Write the question before you shuffle.* Exact words, and the date. Once it’s on paper, you can’t quietly move it after you’ve seen the card.
2. *Tell yourself you’re about to talk nonsense.* Strange advice, but it works: it switches off the part of you that’s trying to be right, and leaves the part that looks.
3. *Describe before you interpret.* What’s on the card, the dominant colour, the number, the [element](/library/four-elements/) — out loud or on paper, before you decide what it means for you. The lenses don’t care what you hope.
4. *Use two columns.* “What I see” on the left, “what I’d like it to say” on the right. If the two lines are identical, be suspicious of yourself, kindly.
5. *One draw per question.* No reshuffle, and no extra card to rescue a card you don’t like; [the clarifier card](/blog/the-clarifier-card/) has my reasons.
6. *Read in the present tense.* “What’s going on” rather than “what will happen”. You can check the present; the future you can only wait for.
7. *Reread it a week later.* Note what held and what didn’t. This is the most important of the seven, and it’s the habit I describe in [keeping a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/).

Here’s what that looks like on one page. Say that on a Monday you write: “What do I need before I talk to my sister on Saturday?” You shuffle, draw once, and get the Queen of Swords.

A queen seated in profile, one sword held straight up, her other hand raised and open, clouds gathering below her and a clear sky above. You describe before you interpret: Swords, so Air, the element of decisions and of words, the one that cuts cleanly. A queen, so someone who holds that element rather than charging with it. One sword, upright, not waving about.

Left column: “Say it clearly, once, and keep my hand open.” Right column, honestly: “I’d like it to say she’ll apologise.” The card doesn’t say that, and now you can see it doesn’t.

The following Monday, you reread. Held: you said what you had to say in two sentences, without raising your voice. And the apology you’d hoped the card would promise? It wasn’t on the card, and it didn’t come. One week, one card, and a record of what you saw set against what you wanted.

The notebook gives you back the person who says, afterwards, “yes, that was right” or “no, that missed”. That person is you, a week later, calmer.

And if you look at a card and see nothing, stop. Sometimes the block is yours: too much pressure on the question. Come back another day. Interpreting takes energy, and you’re allowed to be tired.

## Questions that work when you read for yourself {#good-questions}

The questions that work best are about you, now, and something you can act on:

- “What am I not looking at this week?”
- “What do I need before this conversation?”
- “What is taking up most of my energy at the moment?”

Notice what these have in common: none of them can be answered with a yes, and all of them leave the decision with you. If a question in your head starts with “will he” or “will it”, turn it round before you shuffle. “Will he call?” becomes “What do I want to say if he does?” — and that one, you can check.

Questions about outcomes you can’t control, or about what someone else thinks, work less well for yourself than for anyone: the wish is sitting in the reader’s chair. And the ones I’d leave aside entirely — health, death, pregnancy, court cases, money decisions — aren’t easier when you read your own cards. They’re harder, because the fear is holding the deck. For each of them there’s [a kinder question that a card can hold](/blog/questions-to-avoid-asking-tarot/).

## When to put the deck down {#put-it-down}

Reading for yourself is good practice. It can also turn into a habit that does the opposite of what you wanted. A few signs:

- You’re upset and want the cards to calm you down. You’ll read the upset, not the card.
- You’ve put one question to the cards three times or more this month. That’s no longer reading; it’s waiting for permission. I go into it in [what repeat readings do to you](/blog/ask-tarot-the-same-question-twice/).
- You’re waiting for a card to make a decision you could make today. That decision was yours before you shuffled, and it still is.
- The cards are standing in for a person: a friend you haven’t called, a doctor you haven’t seen, a therapist. Sometimes what’s going on is psychological, and the right door isn’t a deck. There’s no failure in walking through it.

In each case, a few weeks without the cards does more than another draw. You need to trust yourself, make decisions, make mistakes and live with a bit of imperfection — and those muscles only grow when the deck isn’t doing the lifting.

## Then read for someone who tells you nothing {#read-for-others}

Reading for yourself teaches you the deck. Reading for someone else teaches you whether you can read. Only another person can sit across the table, keep quiet, and then tell you what landed and what didn’t.

So after a few weeks of one card a morning, find one person: a friend who can keep a straight face, a stranger in a tarot group, a pen pal. Draw one card, say what you see, and let them speak only at the end. The first time you say something true about a person who told you nothing, and you’re as surprised as they are, you’ll understand why I teach this.
