Runes, a pendulum and the I Ching are all divination tools, and each one does something tarot doesn't. Runes give you a sign. The pendulum gives you a yes or a no. The I Ching gives you a text someone wrote a very long time ago. Tarot gives you a picture — and pictures are what I read.

Here's what each one is, with an example, and what changes when you use it.

## What are runes? {#runes}

Runes are the letters of the old Germanic alphabets. The one most rune sets use, the Elder Futhark, has 24 letters, and takes its name from the first six: F, U, Th, A, R, K. Each rune has a name and a meaning. Fehu is cattle, and so wealth, what you own. Raidho is the ride, the journey. Isa is ice: things standing still.

A rune set is usually 24 small stones or wooden tiles in a cloth bag. You draw one, or three, or cast a handful onto a cloth and read the ones that land face up. The letters are old; most of the meanings people read today come from modern books.

Say you draw Fehu. You get a word: wealth, possessions. Now set it beside the Ace of Pentacles.

A hand from a cloud offers a coin over a garden, with an arch cut through the hedge beyond. That's a number (one: a start), an element (Earth: matter, what has value for you) and a scene — something is being offered, and you still have to walk through the arch. The rune is a word; the card is a sentence.

## What is a pendulum? {#pendulum}

A pendulum is a small weight — a stone, a metal drop, sometimes a ring — hanging from a chain or a thread. You hold the chain between your fingers, keep your hand as still as you can, and ask a question. Before you start, you decide what yes and no look like: for example, a clockwise circle for yes and a swing back and forth for no.

Say you can't decide between two paint colours for your bedroom. You hold the pendulum over each sample, ask “this one?”, and it circles over the second.

What the pendulum does differently is that it only answers yes or no. It doesn't tell you why, or what else is going on. The usual explanation for its movement is the ideomotor effect: tiny movements of the hand that you make without noticing. That doesn't make it useless — it may be exactly what makes it useful, because it shows you which way your body already leans. It's the same principle as the cards: the tool helps you focus, or unfocus, so your own thoughts step aside.

Keep it for small choices: a swinging weight shouldn't decide anything big for you, any more than a reader should. That's also why I answer [yes-or-no questions](/blog/yes-or-no-tarot/) differently with tarot, and why health, pregnancy or money decisions are [subjects I don't read on](/disclaimers/#refusals) with any tool.

## What is the I Ching? {#i-ching}

The I Ching, or Book of Changes, is a classical Chinese text whose oldest layer is close to three thousand years old. It is built on 64 hexagrams: figures of six stacked lines, each line either solid (yang) or broken (yin). You build one by tossing three coins six times, from the bottom line up, then read the text for that hexagram — and for any “changing lines”, which turn it into a second hexagram.

Six solid lines make hexagram 1, the Creative; six broken lines make hexagram 2, the Receptive. And the book doesn't end on hexagram 63, After Completion. It ends on 64, Before Completion: a book about change that refuses to finish on “done”.

Yin and yang are close to how I teach [feminine and masculine energies](/library/feminine-masculine-energies/): two dynamics, each holding a part of the other, equal in value. What the I Ching does differently is that the meaning is already written. Your work is to relate an old text to your life. With tarot, the words are yours.

## Coffee cups, paintings: you can read in anything {#read-in-anything}

Reading starts with the senses everyone already has. To sharpen them: meditate, read, paint — any meditative art. Then read tarot, coffee cups, even a painting on a museum wall. You can read in anything.

Coffee cup reading is a good example. After a Turkish coffee, you turn the cup upside down onto its saucer, let it cool, then read the shapes the grounds have left on the sides: a bird, a road, a face. A painting works the same way as a card: colours first, then the figures, then what comes to you. **The tool focuses you; the reading comes from you.** That's why the [colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) I use on cards works on a canvas too.

## Which tool does what? {#which-tool}

| Tool | What you get | Good for | Its limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Runes | One sign, one word | A short prompt for the day | Terse; little nuance |
| Pendulum | Yes or no | Sorting a small choice | No why; steered without noticing |
| I Ching | A written text | Slow reflection on a change | You read someone else's words |
| Tarot | A picture, a number, an element, colours | A story with several angles | Takes practice to read |

An honest limit: I've counted my tarot readings, and I've never counted runes, pendulums or hexagrams. I can tell you what each tool does; I can't tell you how often it's right. If you want to compare tarot with other card decks, there's [tarot vs oracle cards](/blog/tarot-vs-oracle-cards/).
