Giving feedback to another reader kindly means being precise: say what landed, say plainly what didn’t, and keep your opinions on their style for when they ask. A polite yes to everything feels kind. It also fills their notebook with hits that weren’t, and a learning reader can’t afford that.

## Start with what landed, word for word {#what-landed}

Quote the sentence back. Not “that was great”, but “when you said there was a door I keep not opening — that was exact.” Then, if you can, say why: which part of your life it touched, without telling the whole story.

This tells the reader which part of what they did actually worked. Was it the colour they noticed? An image that came out of nowhere? A number? Vague praise teaches nothing. A quoted sentence teaches them where their reading came from.

## Say the misses plainly {#the-miss}

“That part didn’t land.” Say it calmly, and leave it there. Don’t add “but maybe it will make sense later”, and don’t bend your life to make a sentence fit. A miss is a miss.

It can feel harsh. It isn’t. By my count about one reading in ten misses, and the readers who improve are the ones who can write that word in their notebook without flinching. **The kindest feedback is the accurate one.** If it helps the reader on the receiving end, I’ve written about [what to do when a reading is wrong](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/).

And if something does land later, write to them. That’s feedback too, and often the most useful kind. Tell them what changed, so they can move the note from one column to the other honestly. It happened to me early on: I read for a stranger on a Discord group about a relationship, and she said it didn’t resonate, because she wasn’t looking for one. Three or four days later she texted me, shocked: everything had happened.

## Separate what they saw from how they said it {#content-and-delivery}

There are two things you can comment on, and they’re worth keeping apart.

What they saw: the content, the part you’re confirming or not.

How they said it: the pace, the tone, whether they went somewhere heavy without checking first, whether they said a hard thing gently. Delivery matters as much as accuracy — a true sentence said brutally can do more harm than a miss. [How to say hard things kindly](/blog/bad-news-tarot-reading/) goes further on that side.

Ask before you give notes on delivery. “Do you want a word on how it came across, or only on what landed?” Some days a reader wants the full picture. Some days they want the count and a cup of tea.

## Don’t correct their reading with yours {#not-yours}

If you read too, it’s tempting to say “I’d have read that card differently”. Resist it, unless they ask.

Two readers can look at the same spread, see two different stories, and both explain why. That’s part of what makes reading closer to an art than a code; I go into it in [why two readers get different answers](/blog/two-readers-different-answers/). Your feedback is about what landed for you, the person being read. It’s not a correction of the meaning of the Five of Cups.

## Two compliments to leave out {#leave-out}

“You have a gift.” It’s meant warmly, and I understand why people say it. But it takes the reading out of the reader’s hands: if it’s a gift, there’s nothing to practise and nothing to check. There are no Chosen Ones, only someone who let go at the right moment, and that can be done again. Name what they did instead — “you trusted the first image and said it straight away” — so they know what to repeat. I explain why I hand that compliment back in [‘you have a gift’](/blog/you-have-a-gift/).

“You’re better than the last reader I saw.” Comparisons sound generous and teach nothing; there’s nothing in them the reader can use. Keep the feedback on this reading, this sentence, this card.

## When to say it, and a script to keep {#timing}

After, not during. While the reading is happening, keep a quiet face and let them speak; reacting mid-way hands them information, and then neither of you knows what they really read. That’s the logic of [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/).

Then soon after, while they can still write it down. In a group — in a [workshop](/workshops/), for example — talk about the reading, never about the person. Whatever the setting, this order works:

1. “This landed:” and their sentence, word for word.
2. “This didn’t:” and the part, plainly.
3. “This I don’t know yet:” and a promise to tell them if it changes.
4. “Do you want a note on delivery?” — and only then, if they say yes.

When it’s your turn to receive feedback, the same rules apply the other way: say thank you, write it down, and don’t argue a miss into a hit. Two columns, what you said and what was confirmed, kept honestly — that’s [the notebook that makes you better](/blog/tarot-journal/).
