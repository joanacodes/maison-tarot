Cold reading is the craft of seeming to know a stranger by watching them, saying what’s true of most people and following their reactions. A tarot reading, the way I practise it, is what’s left once you take all of that away. The quickest way to tell which one you just had: count how much you said before the reader got it right.

## What cold reading is {#definition}

Cold reading is a set of techniques that lets someone appear to know things about a person they’ve never met. It works with what’s in front of the reader: age, clothes, accent, a ring or the mark of one, the way someone sits down, how tired they look.

Picture a woman in her thirties who arrives a little late, phone in hand, with a pale band of skin where a ring used to be. A reader says, “There’s been a change in a close relationship, and you’re still deciding how you feel about it.” She nods. The reader goes on, a little more precise each time, guided by every nod.

The usual tools are:

- *Observation*: reading the person before the cards.
- *High-probability statements*: sentences true of most adults. On their own, that’s the [Barnum effect](/blog/barnum-effect-tarot/), which works even from a printed page; cold reading adds someone watching how you take them.
- *Fishing*: “I’m getting an older man, a father figure perhaps?” — and waiting.
- *Following reactions*: a nod means go further, a frown means change direction.
- *Recasting misses*: “it hasn’t happened yet” or “you’ll understand later”.
- *Hot reading*, its cousin: finding things out beforehand, from a booking form or a social media profile.

Mentalists perform all of this openly on stage, and it’s a real skill. It’s also worth saying plainly: a lot of cold reading isn’t deliberate. Attentive, empathic people pick up cues without noticing, and a reader can do it with complete sincerity. It’s one of the [four common explanations for how tarot works](/blog/how-does-tarot-work/).

## Why it works so well {#why-it-works}

Because it’s built on real qualities: attention, listening, warmth. The same ones that make a good friend, a good teacher or a good salesperson.

The person helps, too. They fill the gaps, connect a vague sentence to a precise memory, and afterwards remember the three hits rather than the ten near-misses. Nobody is being stupid here. That’s how all of us listen when someone seems to be talking about us, and it’s much of [why readings feel so accurate](/blog/why-tarot-readings-feel-accurate/).

The trouble starts only when the result is presented as coming from the cards.

## “They’re always right about the past” {#the-past}

This is the argument I hear most often for a reader being genuine: they couldn’t have known all that about my past.

It’s a fair thing to wonder about. But the past is exactly where cold reading is strongest. Your past is written on you: in your age, your face, the way you hold yourself, the first thing you said when you sat down. And many statements about the past are true of nearly everyone — a loss you never quite talked about, someone who didn’t see your worth, a childhood that asked you to grow up a bit early.

Say a reader draws the Six of Cups and tells you, “This is your childhood. Someone back then didn’t understand you.”

The card does speak of childhood. The sentence, though, fits almost anyone who has ever been a child. It feels precise because you supply the memory.

**Accuracy about your past only counts if you gave the reader nothing to work with.** That’s what [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/) is for: you tell the reader nothing, and whatever they get right, they can’t have got from you. It’s also why I focus on the present more than the past or the future.

## How to tell which one you just had {#how-to-tell}

Six questions to ask yourself on the way home:

1. Who spoke first, and how much did you say before the cards came out?
2. How many questions did the reader ask? Count them.
3. Would the statements fit your neighbour? If yes, they don’t count.
4. Did anything specific come up that you hadn’t hinted at by word or face?
5. What happened to the misses: named as misses, or reshaped until they fitted?
6. Did it end with a sales hook — a curse to lift, an extended reading, a “come back next week”? That last one has its own list in [how to spot a fake tarot reader](/blog/how-to-spot-a-fake-tarot-reader/).

If the reader asked a lot, guessed broadly, and turned every miss into a hit, you probably had a cold reading, sincere or not. That doesn’t mean you wasted your time; a good listener can still help. It means the cards weren’t what did the work.

## What I do about it in my own readings {#what-i-do}

Nobody stops noticing things on command, and I’m no exception. What I can do is take the tools on that list away, one by one.

- *Observation*: online, I often have only a pseudonym and a voice: no face, no ring, no clothes to read.
- *Fishing*: I ask nothing before or during the reading. The person tells me nothing, and only speaks once I’ve finished.
- *Following reactions*: I ask people to keep a poker face, and we compare at the end.
- *Recasting misses*: about one reading in ten misses, by my own count. When one does, I say so, I write it down, and I don’t draw a card to rescue it.
- *Hot reading*: at my events, the people read are drawn at random on the day, so there’s no one to look up beforehand.

The honest limit: silence doesn’t switch off every cue. A voice, a face on a video call, a hesitation still carry information, and I can’t promise I never use them. What the silence does is make the rest checkable. I explain the full set-up on the [method page](/method/).
