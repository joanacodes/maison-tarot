Yes, and doubt is a good starting kit. My own doubt was aimed at astrology first, and the habit it left me with — checking everything — is the one I teach.

## Can a sceptic learn tarot? {#short-answer}

Yes. Learning to read doesn’t ask you to believe anything. It asks you to look at a card, say what you see, and check afterwards whether it landed. Everything in that sequence can be done with your eyebrows raised.

By my own count, every single person I’ve helped read managed it on the first, second or third try. That isn’t a study; it’s my notebook. But it’s why I say anyone can learn, and why I don’t believe in Chosen Ones. Reading is like art: some people have more talent, and anyone who practises can do it. There’s [no gift you need to have first](/blog/do-you-need-psychic-abilities/).

## What I did with my own doubt {#my-story}

During the pandemic I played an astrology video purely to laugh at it. Then I read my own birth chart, and it was too precise to wave away.

So I did what a sceptic does. I gave every placement in my chart a mark, right or wrong, and then marked my family’s, my friends’ and strangers’. Across all of it, 30 to 40% held, which didn’t impress me much. But three placements stood out at 75 to 90%: Saturn, Lilith and Chiron. I wrote up [the whole experiment, numbers included](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).

I didn’t stop being sceptical. I started counting. When my friends gave me my first tarot deck one Christmas — I’d expected an astrology book — I did the same thing with the cards, and the very first reading held. I’d assumed I would need training first.

## Why sceptics often make good readers {#good-readers}

Because the habits that make a reader trustworthy are sceptical habits.

A sceptic keeps an honest notebook: what I said on the left, what was confirmed on the right. A sceptic says “that’s a miss” without trying to rescue it. A sceptic doesn’t accept “it’ll make sense one day” as an answer. **Doubt isn’t the opposite of reading; it’s what keeps a reading honest.**

There’s also the sentence I teach first, said to yourself before you read: a warning that what comes next could be nonsense. It switches off the part of you that tries to be right. Sceptics tend to find it the most natural sentence in the world.

## The hard part for a sceptic {#hard-part}

It isn’t believing. It’s letting go.

A sceptical mind wants to approve each sentence before it comes out: is that reasonable, is that likely, could I defend it? Reading asks you to say what comes first and check afterwards. The checking is your strength; the order is what takes practice. The only real practice in tarot is letting go.

Say you draw the Five of Cups.

The sceptical voice starts at once: a gloomy card, it would fit anyone. Fine; keep that for later. First say what you see. Someone wrapped in black, head bowed over three fallen cups, two upright ones behind. Black for absence, five for conflict, cups for emotions: something lost, and someone alone with it. Then ask whether it landed, and only then let the sceptic speak.

That’s why I teach the way I do. I got friends and family to try reading, and the moment I teach for is when a beginner says something accurate and stares, startled by their own words.

## How a sceptic can start {#how-to-start}

1. Get one deck: the 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith. Even its numbered cards show people doing something you can describe.
2. Learn one lens, not 78 meanings. Colours are the fastest way in.
3. Find someone who will tell you nothing: no question, no context.
4. Say the sentence, then say what you see, out loud.
5. Note it all in two columns, said and confirmed. Count the misses too.
6. Do it ten times before you decide anything.

The full first session is in [how to read tarot as a beginner](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/), and the counting is in [my notebook method, misses included](/blog/tarot-journal/).

## What you don’t have to sign up to {#what-you-dont-need}

No belief, no gift, no spiritual identity, no shopping list beyond a deck and a notebook. You don’t need to decide how tarot works, either; I don’t know myself. And you don’t need to believe in it for a reading to work, as I explain in [do you have to believe in tarot](/blog/do-you-have-to-believe-in-tarot/).

The honest limit: if you try it fairly, ten readings or more with people who told you nothing, and your notebook says it doesn’t work for you, that’s a result. I’d rather hear about it than argue with it.

If you’d like to try with someone beside you, a [workshop](/workshops/) has you reading a card within ten minutes, theory afterwards, and ends with everyone reading a card they’ve never seen.
