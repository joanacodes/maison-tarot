Yes, and doubt is a good starting kit. I came to astrology to laugh at it, and the habit that came with the laughing — checking everything — is the one I teach. Every person I’ve helped read managed it within one to three tries.

## Can a sceptic learn tarot? {#short-answer}

Yes. Learning to read doesn’t ask you to believe anything. It asks you to look at a card, say what you see, and check afterwards whether it landed. Everything in that sequence can be done with your eyebrows raised.

By my own count, every single person I’ve helped read managed it on the first, second or third try. That isn’t a study; it’s my notebook. But it’s why I say anyone can learn, and why I don’t believe in Chosen Ones. Reading is like art: some people have more talent, and anyone who practises can do it. There’s [no gift you need to have first](/blog/do-you-need-psychic-abilities/).

## I came to astrology to laugh at it {#my-story}

During the pandemic I put on an astrology video to mock it. Then I read my own birth chart, and it was too precise to wave away.

So I did what a sceptic does. I went through my chart box by box, a tick or a cross next to each placement. Then my family’s, my friends’, strangers’. Overall, 30 to 40% held, which didn’t impress me much. But three placements — Saturn, Lilith and Chiron — held 75 to 90% of the time. I wrote up [how I tested my birth chart](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/), numbers included.

I didn’t stop being sceptical. I started counting. When my friends gave me my first tarot deck one Christmas — I’d expected an astrology book — I did the same thing with the cards. My first reading was accurate. I’d assumed I would need training first.

## Why sceptics often make good readers {#good-readers}

Because the habits that make a reader trustworthy are sceptical habits.

A sceptic keeps an honest notebook: what I said on the left, what was confirmed on the right. A sceptic says “that’s a miss” without trying to rescue it. A sceptic doesn’t accept “the cards mean something you haven’t realised yet” as an answer. **Doubt isn’t the opposite of reading; it’s what keeps a reading honest.**

There’s also the sentence I teach first. Before you read, you tell yourself: *I’m about to talk nonsense.* It switches off the part of you that tries to be right. Sceptics tend to find it the most natural sentence in the world.

## The hard part for a sceptic {#hard-part}

It isn’t believing. It’s letting go.

A sceptical mind wants to approve each sentence before it comes out: is that reasonable, is that likely, could I defend it? Reading asks you to say what comes first and check afterwards. The checking is your strength; the order is what takes practice. The only real practice in tarot is letting go.

The sceptical voice starts at once: a gloomy card, it would fit anyone. Fine; keep that for later. First say what you see. A figure in a black cloak, three cups spilled, two still standing behind. Black for absence, five for conflict, cups for emotions: something lost, and someone alone with it. Then ask whether it landed, and only then let the sceptic speak.

That’s why I teach the way I do. I made friends and family try reading, and the moment I teach for is the one where someone says something true and is shocked by what they’ve just said. You can’t be told what it’s like. You can only try.

## How a sceptic can start {#how-to-start}

1. Get one deck, the 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith. Every card has a scene you can read without a book.
2. Learn one lens, not 78 meanings. Colours are the fastest way in.
3. Find someone who will tell you nothing: no question, no context.
4. Say the sentence, then say what you see, out loud.
5. Write down what you said and what they confirmed. Count the misses too.
6. Do it ten times before you decide anything.

The full first session is in [how to read tarot as a beginner](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/), and the counting is in [how I keep a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/).

## What you don’t have to sign up to {#what-you-dont-need}

No belief, no gift, no spiritual identity, no shopping list beyond a deck and a notebook. You don’t need to decide how tarot works, either; I don’t know myself. And you don’t need to believe in it for a reading to work, as I explain in [do you have to believe in tarot](/blog/do-you-have-to-believe-in-tarot/).

The honest limit: if you try it fairly, ten readings or more with people who told you nothing, and your notebook says it doesn’t work for you, that’s a result. I’d rather hear about it than argue with it.

If you’d like to try with someone beside you, a [workshop](/workshops/) starts with a card in the first ten minutes and the explanation after, and ends with everyone reading a card they’ve never seen.
