Tarot can’t bring anyone back, and it can’t tell you whether they’re at peace. What it can hold is you, while you grieve: a picture for a feeling, a place to keep a memory, an idea for the week ahead. And when grief is heavier than a card can carry, the right companion is a person.

## What tarot can hold in grief {#what-it-can-hold}

Grief isn’t one feeling. It’s sadness, and also anger, guilt, numbness, sometimes relief, often all of them in the same afternoon. When my closest friend died of cancer, I was angry, not only sad. Anger belongs to grief too, and it deserves a place on the table.

A card can give that place. Say you draw the Five of Swords on an evening when you feel furious at everyone, including the person who died. You don’t need a book. A figure gathering swords while two others walk away tells you: there’s a fight in me, and nobody to have it with. Seeing it drawn makes it easier to say out loud.

The cards can also hold a memory for a moment. A warm card can bring back a dinner, a joke, a walk, and you can write it down before it fades. And on evenings that are too long, shuffling gives your hands something gentle to do.

**In grief, a card is a place to set something down for a moment, not an answer.**

## The questions the cards can’t answer {#what-they-cant-answer}

Are they at peace? Can they hear me? Did they forgive me? Could I have done something? When will this stop hurting?

These are the questions people bring, and they come from love, and often from guilt that has nowhere to go. I understand why you’d want a card to answer them. I don’t read on them, and here’s why: a card can’t know, and if it seems to answer, you’re the one filling in the answer. On something this tender, a wrong answer can stay with you for years. By my own count, about one reading in ten misses; that’s fine for a question about your week, and too much for this one. I explain more in [why I step back from health and death questions](/blog/health-pregnancy-death-tarot/).

What helps instead: write them a letter with the question in it, and keep the letter. Tell someone who knew them the story you remember. And ask the cards about you, which is the part they can hold.

One more thing, because it frightens people. If [the Death card](/library/tarot-cards/death/) turns up while you’re grieving, it isn’t announcing anything. In my notes it’s the harvest: something ends so that the ground can be sown again, one day, not now.

## A gentle grief spread: four cards {#spread}

Lay four cards in a row.

1. *What I’m carrying today.* Not forever. Today.
2. *A memory I want to keep.* Let the card bring one back, then write it down.
3. *What would help me this week.* Something small and doable.
4. *Who I could lean on.* A living person you can reach: a friend, a cousin, a neighbour, a professional. This position is the reason for this article’s title.

Say someone, a few months after a loss, draws the Five of Cups, the Three of Cups, the Hermit and the Queen of Cups.

The Five of Cups is the card I call the heaviest in the deck: losing, a dark emotion, being alone with it. A cloaked figure looks down at three spilled cups. Two still stand behind them, and a bridge crosses the river, though the figure hasn’t turned round yet. The Three of Cups, three women dancing with their cups raised, might bring back a celebration: a birthday, a wedding, a good evening with friends. Three is the number of a bond that works.

The Hermit holds a lantern on a snowy peak: withdraw a little this week, with just enough light for the next step. And the Queen of Cups sits at the water’s edge, looking into an ornate cup. [Water](/library/four-elements/water-element/) is feeling; she’s someone warm who listens without hurrying you. Who in your life is like her? That’s who to call.

## When you need a person {#when-you-need-a-person}

Grief takes the time it takes, and there’s no schedule to keep. But some signs say the cards aren’t enough company:

- months go by and nothing has softened at all;
- you can’t sleep, eat or work, or you’ve stopped seeing anyone;
- you draw cards every night to feel close to them, or to ask the same question again;
- you have thoughts of not wanting to be here.

Then the door is a person’s: a friend first, your doctor, a bereavement counsellor or a therapist. [Not everything is a matter for the cards](/disclaimers/#therapy), and knocking on that door isn’t giving up on anyone. If you’re having thoughts of ending your life, please call now: Samaritans on 116 123 in the UK and Ireland, 988 in the US and Canada, Lifeline on 13 11 14 in Australia, or your local emergency number.

If you’re drawing every night, take a break from your cards now and then. It isn’t about forgetting them. It’s about making room for the people who are still here; I wrote more about [putting the deck down for a while](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/).

## A first gentle step {#first-step}

Tonight, if you’d like to: one card, one line about them, one line about you. Then put the deck away and send a message to someone who knew them.

At this time of year, with All Saints and the evenings drawing in, some people prefer a quieter, older ritual of remembering. If that’s you, [the ancestor spread](/blog/ancestor-tarot-spread/) is built for it, and it’s gentle too.
