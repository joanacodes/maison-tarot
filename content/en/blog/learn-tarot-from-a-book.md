You can learn *about* tarot from a book. You can't learn to *read* from one, any more than you can learn to swim from a manual. A book gives you the map; practice gives you the moment you say something true about someone and surprise yourself.

Both matter. They come in a particular order, though, and most people take them the wrong way round.

## What a tarot book gives you {#what-books-give}

A good tarot book gives you what you'd never work out alone at a kitchen table. The structure: 22 major arcana, four suits of fourteen, numbers one to ten, four court ranks. The history: who drew the deck you're holding, when, and why it looks the way it does. The vocabulary, so you can follow other readers. And the correspondences — which sign sits with which card, which element with which suit — tables that took people generations to build.

A book also gives you a second opinion. When a card stays silent for weeks, reading how someone else sees it can unlock it. That's worth a lot.

What it gives you, in short, is knowledge. Reading is something else.

## What only practice can teach {#what-practice-teaches}

When I first made my friends and family try reading, I watched the same thing happen again and again: they'd say something true about the person in front of them, and then be shocked by what they'd said. That moment is why I teach. No page can produce it, because it isn't information. You can't be told what it's like. You can only try.

My own start went the same way. My friends gave me my first deck for Christmas 2020 — from my wishlist; I'd been expecting an astrology book. I started reading on 26 December, and my first reading was accurate. I'd assumed I'd need training first.

Practice teaches the things a book can only name. Letting go, which is the only real practice there is. Which channel your impressions come through — images, words, sounds, a feeling in the body. When to say something gently, or differently, because the person isn't ready. And, most of all, **when you're wrong — which no book can tell you, because the book isn't in the room.**

## The trap: reciting the book {#the-trap}

The risk with learning from a book first is that you start reciting. You draw a card, you reach for the sentence you memorised, and you stop looking.

Say someone draws the Five of Cups.

The book says grief, loss, the most negative card in the suit — and my own notes say much the same. But as you look, your eye goes to the two cups still standing behind the figure, and that's what comes out of your mouth. It's what the person needed to hear. The book wasn't wrong. It wasn't the reading, that's all.

The same image can read differently from one person to another, and from one day to the next. And two books will often disagree about the same card — which tells you something about [what tarot readers actually disagree on](/blog/what-tarot-readers-disagree-about/), and why no single author can be your last word.

## How to use a tarot book well {#use-a-book-well}

Put the book after the card, not before it.

1. Look first. Draw one card and describe it out loud: what's on it, what you see, what you receive. Only then open the book and compare.
2. One card a day, then three, then more. That's how I tell people to get familiar with a deck, and it's how the [daily one-card pull](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/) works.
3. One lens at a time. Read the chapter on numbers and spend a week on numbers; then elements. Don't swallow the whole book in one sitting.
4. Keep a notebook. What you said, what was confirmed. It becomes the book only you could write — here's [how I keep mine](/blog/tarot-journal/).
5. Read for someone who tells you nothing. That's where a book's meanings get tested against a real person.

## The books I actually recommend {#books-i-recommend}

My list is short. For numbers, I recommend Corrine Kenner's tarot book — it's the one I point people to when they want to go deeper into the [numerology lens](/library/numerology/).

For astrology, *The Only Astrology Book You'll Ever Need* by Joanna Martine Woolfolk. It covers the signs and the planets, and it's what I'd hand to someone who wants the astrology lens without drowning in it. For drawing up charts I use Astro-Seek, which is a website rather than a book.

And then there's the free [Readers' Guide](/guide/), which holds the five lenses I teach in a few pages. It's the closest thing I've written to a book, and it's built to be read with a card in your other hand.

That's all. Two books, a guide, and a lot of cards on a lot of tables.

## Close the book, open the deck {#next-step}

Tonight, draw one card and say what you see before you check a single meaning. Then read what the book says and notice where you agree.
