A clarifier card is an extra card drawn when part of a reading is unclear, to explain the card that confused you. I don’t draw them. One more card rarely clears a reading up; it gives you a second story to stitch onto the first, and it hides the one thing you need to know — whether the first reading landed.

## What a clarifier card is {#what-it-is}

After a spread is laid out, the reader draws one more card from the deck and places it beside, or on top of, a card that’s hard to read. The new card is meant to “clarify” the old one. Some readers draw one per confusing card; some keep going until the picture feels clear.

Say a three-card reading about a new project has the Four of Cups in the middle, and neither reader nor sitter knows what to do with it. The reader draws a clarifier and gets the Knight of Wands.

Now there’s a story: “You’re ignoring an offer, but a burst of energy is on its way.” It sounds good. It flows. And it isn’t the Four of Cups made clearer; it’s a different reading.

Plenty of readers use clarifiers, and some use them carefully. This is why I don’t.

## Why one more card muddies a reading {#why-it-muddies}

*It’s a new card, not a clearer one.* The Knight of Wands didn’t explain the Four of Cups. It added a rider, a horse and a direction the first card never had. You haven’t looked harder at what was confusing you; you’ve looked away from it.

*With enough cards, anything fits.* Every extra card adds meanings, and the combined story gets wider and vaguer until it can match almost whatever the person says next. That’s how statements that fit everyone come to feel personal — the mechanism I check myself against in [the Barnum effect and tarot](/blog/barnum-effect-tarot/).

*It moves the goalposts.* Notice when the urge to draw one arrives. It’s rarely while the person is nodding along. It tends to come when the reading hasn’t landed.

**A clarifier is often drawn not because a card is unclear, but because the reading didn’t land.**

## What I do when a reading doesn’t land {#when-it-misses}

Misses happen to me about ten per cent of the time. When the person says nothing in it fits, I call it a miss out loud, note it in the book, and keep my hands off the deck.

If I drew a clarifier at that point, I wouldn’t be reading any more; I’d be negotiating. And I’d lose the only honest number I have. My count of what held and what didn’t only means something because the misses stay misses. Counting is the backbone of [my method](/method/), and a single rescued reading would quietly spoil it.

It’s also kinder than it sounds. The person in front of me doesn’t have to pretend a third story fits to make me feel better. We both know where we stand.

## What to do with a card you can’t read {#instead}

A confusing card is usually a card you haven’t looked at long enough. Go back to it instead of away from it.

1. *Describe the drawing out loud.* The Four of Cups: a young man sitting under a tree, arms crossed, three cups on the grass in front of him, and a hand coming out of a cloud offering a fourth. He isn’t looking at it.
2. *Read the lenses.* Four is stability, structure, the fruit of your work; [its advice](/library/numerology/number-4/) is to stay open to what you hadn’t planned, and its flip side is carrying on when you should stop. Cups are Water: emotions.
3. *Say what you see, even if it’s small.* “You’re comfortable — maybe too comfortable — and something is being offered that you haven’t looked at yet.”
4. *Let the person answer, after.* If it lands, good. If it doesn’t, that’s an answer too.
5. *If it still makes no sense, say so.* “I don’t know what this one is doing.” An unread card is more honest than a patched one.

That’s more work than turning over another card. It’s also how you get better: the [Four of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/four-of-cups/) you struggled with today is the one you’ll read fluently next month.

## Clarifiers, spreads and “one more card, please” {#one-more-card}

A spread with positions decided in advance is a different thing. You chose how many cards to draw before you saw any of them, so nothing is being rescued. If you’re unsure how many to choose, I give a rule of thumb in [how many tarot cards to pull](/blog/how-many-tarot-cards-to-pull/).

Sometimes it’s the person who asks for one more card. I understand: when something matters, you want more. I’d rather go back to the cards already on the table with them and look again, together. Usually there’s more there than we first said.

And when you read alone, the pull towards one more card is stronger, because nobody sees you draw it. That’s one of the habits I suggest in [reading tarot for yourself](/blog/reading-tarot-for-yourself/): one draw per question. Next time you’re tempted, put your hand flat on the deck and describe the card you already have, out loud, for one full minute. Then decide whether it was a miss — and if it was, [here’s what to do next](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/).
