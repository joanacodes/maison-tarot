The sentence I said out loud wasn’t about a card. It was about a friend’s birth chart: “Your life was hard, but it feels like you like it somehow.” He said it was right, in a way, and its shape is the one I’d hand anyone who has to say something hard to someone they look up to.

## It was his chart, not the cards {#his-chart}

I should be precise, because the title says “reading”: this one wasn’t tarot. It was astrology.

I first watched an astrology video to make fun of it, and my own chart came out uncomfortably precise. So I tested it, box by box: every placement, a tick or a cross. Then I did the same for my family, my friends, strangers — and for a friend I admired. The whole method and its numbers are in [how I tested my birth chart](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/): overall, 30 to 40% held; Saturn, Lilith and Chiron did far better, between 75 and 90%.

His chart was on point. It was also harsh.

That’s all I’ll tell you about him. The story isn’t his life; it’s one sentence, and what it did.

## Why it’s harder with someone you admire {#harder}

Reading for someone you admire raises the stakes in a way that has nothing to do with the chart or the cards.

In my notes, one of the two usual reasons nothing comes in a reading is the reader’s own block: too much pressure. Admiration is pressure. You want to be right in front of this person. You want them to think well of you. You don’t want to be the one who hurts them.

With him, the pressure had a precise shape: I didn’t want someone I looked up to that much deciding I’d lost my mind. I read his chart all the same.

That pressure pushes in two directions, and both are traps. The first is to soften the hard part until it says nothing: “You’ve had a few challenges, like everyone.” The second is to prove yourself by being blunt, because precision feels like competence: “Your chart is brutal.” One protects you from their disappointment; the other tries to impress them. Neither is about them.

Say a friend you look up to draws the Ten of Wands: a figure bent under ten heavy wands, walking towards a town. The flattering version is “you’re very hardworking”. The blunt one is “you’re crushed”. Both come quickly, and neither is quite true. In my notes the card asks you to keep your creative energy without overloading yourself, and the kind, true version sits somewhere between those two sentences.

## The sentence, piece by piece {#the-sentence}

Here it is again: *Your life was hard, but it feels like you like it somehow.*

“Your life was hard.” No euphemism, no “challenges”, no rounding off. The chart was harsh, and pretending otherwise would have been a polite lie. It also speaks about what he’d lived so far. It describes; it doesn’t forecast.

“But it feels like.” This is an impression, offered as one. Not “your chart says”, not “clearly”. It hands him the right to say no.

“You like it somehow.” This is the turn. It doesn’t deny the hardness; it looks at how he carries it. It gives him back his place in his own story: not someone things happened to, but someone who, in some way, took to them. It’s close to what Saturn stands for in astrology: the toughest lesson, the place where limits hem you in and you have to earn your way — and where, given time, hardship turns out to be what toughens you. You can read more on [Saturn as a lens](/library/astrology/saturn/), whatever his chart did or didn’t show.

“Somehow.” It admits I don’t fully understand it. The explanation is his to give, not mine.

**Say the hard part plainly, then say what the person does with it — and leave them the last word.**

## “Right, in a way” {#right-in-a-way}

He said it was right, in a way.

Not a full yes, and I think that’s the most honest answer he could have given. A sentence about someone’s life can only ever be right in a way. If he had said “exactly” to every word, I’d have trusted it less.

It’s also a reminder of how to count. If you keep a notebook of what you said and what was confirmed, “in a way” stays “in a way”. You don’t upgrade it to a hit because the moment felt important, and you don’t throw it away because it wasn’t complete.

That’s what the shape of the sentence protects: the hard part gets said, and nobody is made smaller. He decided how much of that sentence was his, and that was the right person to decide it.

## How to say a hard thing kindly {#how-to}

The sentence follows a shape you can borrow, with a chart, a card, or none at all.

1. Find the plain word for the hard part. Don’t round it off into something that could be said to anyone.
2. Look for what the person does with it: how they carry it, what it gave them. Say it only if you see it. Invented comfort is worse than none.
3. Offer it as an impression: “it feels like”, “I get the sense”.
4. Leave the door open: “Does that sound right?” Then accept whatever comes back, including “in a way”.
5. Stop there. One hard sentence, well said, is enough. Don’t pile on details to prove you were right.

There’s more on this, including the subjects I don’t read on at all, in [how to share hard news in a reading](/blog/bad-news-tarot-reading/).

And an honest limit. A harsh chart isn’t a verdict on a person. Astrology held 30 to 40% of the time in my test, and the placements that held best were the ones that describe difficulty, which may be part of why they ring so true. I keep astrology as a lens, never as an explanation of someone, and never as [an excuse](/disclaimers/#astrology).

## Before you read for someone you admire {#before-you-read}

Ask first, and mean it: they can say no, and they can stop you at any point. Decide in advance that you’re not there to impress them. And before you say the hard thing, find its other half — the way they carry it — so that what you hand them is the whole picture, not only the weight.
