You don’t need a ritual to start with a new tarot deck. You need a week of looking: fifteen minutes a day, a notebook, and one person to read for at the end. By then the deck has stopped being a new object and become something you can read with.

## Do you need a ritual for a new deck? {#ritual}

No. A new-deck ritual is any small ceremony a reader performs before using a deck for the first time: going through it card by card, sleeping with it under the pillow, cleansing it with smoke or moonlight, knocking on it, or laying a deck interview spread. People do them to mark the start, and to feel that the deck is theirs.

Some of that is pleasant, and I wouldn’t talk anyone out of a habit they enjoy. But what makes a deck yours isn’t the ceremony. It’s learning how its artist draws. I sold sage in 2021 and stopped, because nothing in it improved a reading; I tell that story in [do you need to cleanse your deck](/blog/cleanse-tarot-deck/).

My first deck was a Christmas present from friends, and I started reading with it on 26 December 2020. **What taught me that deck was reading with it.**

## What a deck interview spread is {#deck-interview}

A deck interview spread is a short spread you lay with a new deck as if you were interviewing it. Each position is a question addressed to the deck, as though it were a person. A common version has six:

1. What is your most important quality?
2. What are your strengths?
3. What are your limits?
4. What are you here to teach me?
5. How can I best work with you?
6. Where is our relationship going?

You draw a card for each position and read the answers as the deck describing itself. Say position three, “your limits”, brings up the Seven of Cups: a figure facing seven cups floating in a cloud, each holding something different — a castle, jewels, a wreath, a dragon, a snake, a veiled figure, a head.

In my notes, the Seven of Cups is indecision, too many choices. So you might conclude the deck could overwhelm you.

It’s a gentle way to spend twenty minutes with new cards, and I can see why people like it. My hesitation is practical. Six cards out of seventy-eight tell you very little about the other seventy-two, and the questions invite you to read your hopes and nerves about the deck rather than its drawings. Here’s the lighter version I’d suggest: keep the curiosity and turn it towards the pictures. Instead of asking the deck what its limits are, look at how this artist drew the Seven of Cups, and what they changed.

## A first week with a new deck, day by day {#first-week}

Fifteen minutes a day, with a notebook open beside you.

1. *Day one: look at every card.* Face up, one by one, no reading. Notice which cards you like and which you’d rather skip. Write down three of each.
2. *Day two: one card, described.* Draw one card. Describe what’s on it, what you see, what you receive. Write that down before you interpret anything.
3. *Day three: one card, through its colours.* Which colour dominates? This is where a new deck differs most, and a change of palette changes the reading; the [colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) shows how.
4. *Day four: compare.* Put the same card from two decks side by side — the new one and one you know. What did the artist keep, add, leave out?
5. *Day five: three cards.* One row, read as one sentence. Note the elements and the numbers.
6. *Day six: someone else.* Read for a friend who tells you nothing. Three cards, describe first, let them speak at the end.
7. *Day seven: reread the week.* Which cards came easily? Which did you avoid? Is this a deck you want to read with?

It’s the same order I teach for any deck: one card a day, then three, then more. Afterwards, [the card of the day](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/) is the habit to keep.

## When the artist isn’t Pamela Colman Smith {#other-decks}

Many decks sold today follow the 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith: same scenes, new style. Day four’s question is the fastest way in, card after card: what changed? A Three of Cups painted in blues and greys instead of warm yellows reads differently through the colour lens, even with the same three figures.

Some decks aren’t redrawings at all. In a Marseille deck, the minor cards show arranged suit symbols rather than scenes, so you lean on number and suit instead of a drawing; I compare the two in [Marseille vs Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/). The Thoth deck, painted by Frieda Harris for Aleister Crowley, is very developed and much more occult — a deck to explore later. If this is your first deck, I’d start with the Rider-Waite-Smith, for reasons I give in [the best tarot deck for beginners](/blog/best-tarot-deck-for-beginners/).

## If the deck doesn’t click {#doesnt-click}

Sometimes, at the end of the week, a deck still feels foreign. That isn’t a sign about you or about the deck; it’s taste. Collecting decks is a bit like collecting art: each one is a set of pictures you can read messages through, and not every painter speaks to everyone.

Put it away for a month and try again, or pass it on to someone it suits. Whether you let others handle it in the meantime is up to you; I look at that in [letting other people touch your cards](/blog/let-others-touch-tarot-cards/).
