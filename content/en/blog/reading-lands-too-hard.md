When a card lands harder than you meant, stop, say so, and slow everything down. Then hand the meaning back: you read the card, the person decides what it means for their life. Most hard landings can be repaired in a sentence or two, as long as you notice them.

## How to tell a reading landed too hard {#signs}

The signs are usually small. The person repeats your words back as a verdict: "So it's over, then?" Or they go quiet and stop asking questions, as if the reading had become something to get through. Online, the signs are smaller still: a camera switched off, replies getting shorter, a long pause before "ok".

The way I read gives me every reason to look for them. The person tells me nothing before I start, so I don't know where the sore spots are, and a card can land on one I had no way of seeing. That isn't a reason to read more timidly. It's a reason to watch the person as closely as the cards; [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/) comes with that responsibility.

Often it isn't the card so much as your sentence. My own note on the Five of Cups calls it the most negative card of the lot: losing, dark emotion, being alone with your problems. That's a fair note for a notebook. Said out loud to someone, on its own, it can sound like a sentence passed on their life.

Tears are their own situation, and [what to do when someone cries](/blog/someone-cries-during-reading/) covers them, along with the things not to do that apply here too, such as reaching for another card to soften the blow. This article is about the moment before, or instead: when your words were heavier than the person could hold.

## The first ten seconds {#first-seconds}

Stop talking. Then name what happened, plainly: "That came out heavier than I meant." It costs you nothing, and it tells the person their reaction was seen.

Then check rather than guess: "How did that land?" or "What did you hear when I said that?" What people hear is often worse than what you said. Someone hears "an ending" and thinks of a death; someone hears "loss" and thinks of their marriage. **Name it, then ask what they heard: you can only repair what you know about.**

One thing not to do belongs to this moment in particular: don't take back what was true. Naming the weight isn't the same as withdrawing the card. You're adjusting how heavy it was, not what it said.

## Slow down, and go back to the picture {#slow-down}

Lower your voice. Put the cards down for a moment. Then go back to the card, slower, and read it through a lens instead of a keyword. A hard landing usually means you gave one word and only half of the card.

Take the Five of Cups again. The keyword was loss. Through its number, the card gets wider: five, in my [number table](/library/numerology/number-5/), is conflict and trying, not being afraid to fail, accepting mistakes, making do with what's left. Then the picture: a figure in a dark cloak looking down at three spilled cups, and behind them two still standing, and a bridge over the river. The slower version: "You're looking at what spilled. Behind you, two are still standing, and there's a way across. Which would you like to look at first?" Same card, same truth, with a door in it.

Or the Nine of Swords: a figure sitting up in bed, face in hands, nine swords lined up in the dark behind. Heard alone, it's a nightmare. My note reads it as coming out of a nightmare, the end of a long, troubled stretch, with protection above you. That isn't sugar. It's the half of the card you hadn't said yet.

Describing before interpreting is also how to say a hard thing well the first time, and [how to say hard things kindly](/blog/bad-news-tarot-reading/) starts there. After a hard landing you're doing the same thing a second time, slower, with the person watching you do it.

## Hand the meaning back {#hand-it-back}

Once the weight is right, give the card back to the person it was read for. A card is a picture; the person in front of you knows their life, and only they can say what the picture means in it.

In practice: "That's what I see on the card. Which part of it feels true to you, and which doesn't?" Then listen, and let their answer lead. Say they reply: "The loss part is true, but it isn't my marriage, it's my job." Then that's the reading. Follow them there and leave your first interpretation behind. If none of it fits, say so and drop it: a miss is a miss. About one reading in ten misses, by my count, and a hard reading is no more likely to be right than a gentle one.

If they look ready to carry your sentence home as an instruction, say out loud that it isn't one.

## When it's bigger than the card {#bigger-than-the-card}

Sometimes a card lands hard because it touched something the person has carried for a long time. No better sentence repairs that, and it isn't a reader's to repair. Say once, kindly, that it deserves more time than a reading can give, and leave it there: it's the same door as for tears that go deeper than the card. The words I use and who to suggest are in [how to point someone elsewhere kindly](/blog/client-boundaries-tarot/), and [where my role ends](/disclaimers/#therapy) is written down for the same reason.

## After a hard landing {#after}

Write it down the same evening: the card, what you said, how it landed, and what you'd say next time. After a few entries you may spot a pattern, such as a word you reach for that's heavier than you think. Then close the reading on purpose instead of letting it trail off on the hard card; [how to end a tarot reading well](/blog/how-to-end-a-tarot-reading/) has the steps.
