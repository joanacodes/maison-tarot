A new-job tarot spread is five cards for your first weeks: what this place asks of you, what you bring, where to look for help, what to let settle, and one thing to do before the month is out. It won’t tell you whether you’ll pass your probation. It gives you something to hold while you find your feet.

## Why a tarot spread for your first month in a new job {#why-first-month}

The first month in a job is a flood: new names, new tools, unwritten rules about who speaks in meetings, and a strong urge to prove you were the right hire. Everything feels important, so nothing gets sorted. A spread doesn’t add information; it gives your impressions five boxes to land in.

Say you’ve joined a team where everyone seems busier than you. Without a frame, that becomes “they don’t need me”. With position four — what to let settle — it becomes “it’s week one; I don’t know their rhythm yet”.

## The five positions {#positions}

Lay five cards in a row, left to right.

1. *What this job asks of me.* Not the job description — the real demand, the one nobody wrote down.
2. *What I bring that they haven’t seen yet.* A strength you haven’t had the chance to show.
3. *Where to look for help.* A kind of help or a role, not a named colleague. You’re reading for yourself, not about them.
4. *What to let settle.* What not to judge in the first month: a first impression, a tension, your own doubts.
5. *One thing to do before the month is out.* Concrete, small, doable by Friday.

**Every position is about you; none of them is about what other people think of you.** That’s deliberate, and [reading about someone who isn’t in the room](/blog/reading-tarot-about-someone-else/) explains why.

## A new-job spread, card by card {#example}

Say someone at the end of their first week draws the Page of Pentacles, the Queen of Swords, the King of Cups, the Five of Wands and the Three of Pentacles.

What the job asks: the Page of Pentacles, a young man in a field, studying one coin as if it were the only thing in the world. [The Page](/library/numerology/page/) is the student of the deck, the one who learns before he acts, and Earth makes it practical: learn the tools, the processes, the dull parts nobody explains.

What you bring: the Queen of Swords, upright on her throne, sword raised, one hand held out. [Air](/library/four-elements/air-element/) is decision and communication. You see clearly and you say it kindly — they’ll notice once you’ve earned the room.

Where to look for help: the King of Cups, calm on a throne that floats on a choppy sea. Look for the person who stays steady when a meeting goes sideways, whatever their title.

What to let settle: the [Five of Wands](/library/tarot-cards/five-of-wands/), five young men waving sticks, seeming to fight without any coordination. The team’s noise isn’t a war, and it isn’t yours to sort out in week one.

One thing to do: the Three of Pentacles, a craftsman on a bench in a church, showing his work to two people holding the plans. Three is the machine running well. Before the month ends, show someone a piece of work in progress and ask what they’d change.

Read as one sentence, the row says: “Learn before you prove anything; your clarity will be welcome later; find the calm one; don’t take the noise personally; show your work before the month is out.” Then hold that sentence up against your actual week. If it doesn’t fit, it doesn’t fit.

## What this spread leaves aside {#limits}

Some questions come with every new job: will I pass my probation, does my manager like me, should I ask for more money, should I have taken the other offer. They come from the vulnerability of starting again, and they’re very human.

The cards aren’t the right tool for them. Your manager isn’t in the room; a salary is a money decision with real numbers attached; and whether you keep the job depends on things a card can’t see. What a reading can hold is the question underneath: “what helps me feel settled here?” For a real decision later — staying, leaving, asking — [my career spread](/blog/career-tarot-spread/) reads a choice without letting the cards make it.

## When to lay it, and when to look again {#when}

Lay it at the end of your first week, when you have faces to put to the positions. Write the five cards down, with one sentence for each. Then put the deck away.

Take a break from your cards now and then — and in a new job, that means not drawing a card every morning before you leave for work. Your first month needs your attention on the job, not on the table.

On the last day of the month, reread your five sentences and mark what held. If you’d like to learn the lenses I used here — rank, element, number — a [one-to-one session](/sessions/) goes through them in an hour, with your own cards.
