Yes, for some things, and I’m clear about which. I use AI as a dictionary: something to look things up in. I draw the line where the reading starts, at the point where what’s on a card becomes what it means for the person in front of you.

## Where AI helps a reader {#where-it-helps}

As a reference, it’s patient and awake at any hour. Ask why the Magician has a cup, a sword, a wand and a pentacle on his table, and you’ll get a clear answer without anyone sighing at you. Then hold the answer up against your own notes. In mine, he’s the card of the four elements plus the ether, relaying messages through the wand he raises.

Three uses I think are fair:

- Looking up symbols and history: what an object in a picture has meant over time, why a figure holds what it holds. [The symbols in the Library](/library/tarot-symbols/) cover the same ground, and it’s worth checking one against the other.
- Practising description. Describe a card out loud, its colours, number, element and what the figure is doing, then ask what’s in the picture you didn’t mention. Say you describe the High Priestess: a blue robe falling to the floor like water, a crescent moon at her feet, a scroll half hidden in her lap. Ask what you missed, and you may be pointed to the letters B and J on the two pillars beside her. It’s a good way to learn to look, as long as you describe first and ask second.
- Quizzing yourself. Which element are the Swords? What does a 7 do in a cycle? Repetition, with a partner who never gets bored.

One habit to keep: check what it says against the card in your hand. A fluent answer isn’t always a correct one, and the picture is the authority, not the paragraph.

## Where I draw the line {#the-line}

At the reading. Whatever reaches a person as their reading should have come from you, looking at their cards.

The reasons are set out in [AI tarot vs a human reader](/blog/ai-tarot-vs-human/), and they come down to three. A model has to be told something, while my method rests on [the person telling me nothing](/blog/the-silence-rule/). It hedges until it can’t miss, while I count my misses, about one reading in ten. And it explains cards one at a time, when the relations between them carry most of a reading. If you’d rather see that than take my word for it, [test it the way I test myself](/blog/is-ai-tarot-accurate/).

There’s one more reason, and it’s a reader’s reason. In my teaching notes, the same image can read differently from one person to another, and from one day to another. A dictionary gives the same entry every time; that’s its job. It’s also exactly why it can’t be the reading.

Some readers let it write a draft, then correct the draft. I understand the appeal; a blank page is hard at the end of a long day. But once you’ve read its version, you’re reading its version. Your first impression, the one that came before you thought, is gone, and that impression is most of what a reader brings. What I teach is the reverse: say what comes up, before you try to be right.

Underneath all this, there’s a plainer reason: the person came for you. **If someone believes you read their cards, you should be the one who read them.**

## The grey zone: posts, admin, someone’s story {#grey-zone}

Then there’s everything else: captions, newsletters, booking pages, translations. That’s admin rather than reading, and it’s your call. I’d only keep the cards themselves out of it. Your way of describing a card is your method. If a tool writes your card meanings, your page starts to sound like everyone else’s, and people notice. That’s how readers end up [accused of using AI](/blog/tarot-readers-accused-of-ai/), sometimes unfairly.

One thing isn’t grey for me: a person’s private story. Before I read anyone I ask their permission, because reading someone without it is like going through their bedroom. Pasting what they told you in confidence into a tool run by someone else is the same move. They didn’t agree to it, and you can’t take it back.

## Four questions to ask yourself {#four-questions}

Before you use AI for anything near a reading, try these:

1. Could I tell the person exactly how I used it, without wincing?
2. Will anything they receive have come from something that never saw their cards?
3. Did I type anything they told me in confidence?
4. Am I using it to avoid saying “I don’t know”, or “that one missed”?

If an answer makes you uncomfortable, that’s your line, and it may sit somewhere slightly different from mine. Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.

## What to use instead {#what-to-use-instead}

If you’re reaching for a machine because you can’t remember 78 meanings, you don’t need to remember them. Five ways of looking, colours, numbers, elements, energies and modes, let you read a card you’ve never studied, and they’re [how I teach](/learn-tarot/). If you’re reaching for it because you want a record of what the cards said, a [reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) does more: it shows you your own misses, in your own handwriting.
