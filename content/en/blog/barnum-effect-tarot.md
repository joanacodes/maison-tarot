The Barnum effect is our tendency to read a vague description as if it had been written for us alone. Every tarot reading is exposed to it, mine included. I don’t think that makes tarot worthless; I think it makes checking compulsory, and I check mine three ways: the silence rule, the notebook and the neighbour test.

## What the Barnum effect is {#definition}

The Barnum effect, also called the Forer effect, is the habit we all have of accepting general statements about personality as an accurate, personal portrait. The name comes from P. T. Barnum, the American showman whose circus was said to have a little something for everybody.

The classic demonstration is simple. In 1948 the psychologist Bertram Forer gave his students a personality test, then handed each of them “their” personal analysis. Every student received the same text, assembled largely from a newsstand astrology book. Asked to rate how well it described them, from zero to five, they gave it on average just over four. The experiment has been repeated many times since, with similar results.

Barnum statements share a few features:

- *They’re true of almost every adult.* “You give more than you receive.” “There’s someone you haven’t fully forgiven.”
- *They’re two-sided.* “You can be very sociable, but you need time alone to recharge.” Whichever side you are today, it fits.
- *They’re mostly flattering.* “You have potential you haven’t fully used yet.” Nobody argues with that.
- *They point at a feeling, not a fact.* “Part of you is still waiting for something to change.”

Later research found the effect gets stronger when you believe the description was made for you, when it comes from someone who seems to know, and when it’s pleasant to hear. **In other words, a reading is close to the ideal setting for the Barnum effect.**

## Why tarot is such a good home for it {#why-tarot}

Three things in a reading make it fertile ground.

The cards are broad. Every card has a plus and a minus — that’s how I teach them — which means any card can be read in two directions. That breadth is what makes the deck useful, and it’s also what lets a vague sentence slide in.

Say someone draws the Two of Swords, the Moon and the Five of Cups.

The easy reading writes itself: “You’re avoiding a decision. Something isn’t clear to you yet. And there’s a disappointment you’re still carrying.” Every one of those sentences is true of most people, most weeks. Said warmly, in a quiet room, they’ll land.

The second thing is the setting. People usually come to a reading with something on their mind. They’re primed to hear it, and they’ll connect almost any sentence to it within a second — without being foolish, because that’s how attention works.

The third is the reader. A reader who sounds sure of themselves lends weight to every sentence. The person nods; the nod feels like confirmation; the reader gains confidence; the next vague sentence lands even better. Nobody is lying, and the reading still drifts towards Barnum.

## Check one: the silence rule {#silence-rule}

The first check happens before I draw a single card. The person tells me nothing — no question, no context, no situation. Online, I often have nothing more than a pseudonym and a voice.

This matters for the Barnum effect in a specific way. Vague sentences become convincing when they meet a known story. If you tell me “it’s about my relationship” and I say “there’s something you haven’t said to them yet”, you’ll find the thing — everyone has one. If you’ve told me nothing, I have nothing to tailor the vague sentence to, and the temptation to use one drops.

It also changes how I speak. Without a story to lean on, the only thing I can be specific about is what’s in the cards and what comes up when I look at them. That’s riskier, and it’s the point. I explain the whole rule in [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/), and how it also guards against [cold reading](/blog/cold-reading-vs-tarot/), its close relative.

## Check two: the notebook {#notebook}

The second check happens on paper. I keep a notebook with two columns: what I said, and what was confirmed. I write the sentence as I say it, before the person reacts, and mark it afterwards.

There’s a third mark I use more than I’d like: *too vague to score*. A sentence like “you’ve been carrying a lot lately” goes there even if the person says yes, because a yes to a vague sentence isn’t a hit. It’s a Barnum statement doing its job.

Counting this way across the [blind readings I logged](/blog/does-tarot-work-300-readings/), about nine in ten held and about one in ten missed. That’s my own count, made by hand, not a study, and I say so every time I give it.

The notebook does two things the memory can’t. It keeps the misses, which memory quietly drops, and it keeps the exact wording, which memory quietly improves. If you want to set one up, [how I keep a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) walks through it.

## Check three: the neighbour test {#neighbour-test}

The third check is a single question I ask of every sentence afterwards: would this fit my neighbour? If yes, it doesn’t count.

I grade sentences on three levels:

| Level | Example | What I do |
|---|---|---|
| Fits anyone | “You feel stuck, but you have more freedom than you think.” | Strike it |
| Fits a group | “You’re hesitating over a commitment.” | Half a point at best |
| Fits one person, and could be wrong | “A plan for a home or a wedding is on hold, and you’re the one holding it.” | Counts, if confirmed |

That last sentence is a hypothetical, built from two cards: the Eight of Swords and the Four of Wands.

In my notes, the Eight of Swords is someone prisoner of themselves while holding every tool they need — the blindfold can come off. The Four of Wands is foundations: marriage, building a family. The Barnum version stays at “you feel stuck”. The specific version names a situation, a role and a cause.

**A sentence that can be wrong is the only kind worth counting.** If the person says no, it’s a miss and I write it down. If they say yes, it means something, because it could easily have been no.

I ran the same test on my own birth chart when I was testing astrology: a tick if a placement described me in a way I couldn’t have written about anyone else, a cross if it would fit my neighbour. Overall, about 30 to 40% held. That story is in [I tested my birth chart box by box](/blog/i-tested-my-birth-chart/).

## What the Barnum effect doesn’t explain {#limits}

The Barnum effect explains very well why vague readings feel accurate. It’s worth being just as clear about what it doesn’t cover.

It predicts instant agreement. The very first reading I gave a stranger went the other way. I said it was about a relationship, with an unflattering detail: she hadn’t been very kind to the other person. She said it didn’t resonate; she wasn’t looking for a relationship. Three or four days later she texted me, shocked, because it had all happened, the unkind part included. A Barnum statement gets a yes on the spot. This got a no, and then a fact.

That’s one story, and one story proves nothing on its own. I don’t know why the specific hits happen, and I don’t claim to. What I can say is that once the vague sentences are struck, something is left over, at a rate I can state, with misses I name. I wrote about the mechanics in [why tarot readings feel so accurate](/blog/why-tarot-readings-feel-accurate/).

The other honest limit is me. My checks reduce the Barnum effect; they don’t remove it. I still catch vague sentences in my own notebook. Readers who say they never do probably aren’t looking.

## Try it on your next reading {#try-it}

You don’t need to trust my numbers. Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.

1. Before the reading, agree that the person tells you nothing.
2. Write down every sentence you say, as you say it.
3. Afterwards, strike every sentence that would fit your neighbour.
4. Of what’s left, mark what was confirmed and what wasn’t.
5. Do this ten times before you draw any conclusion about yourself.

The gap between the accuracy you felt and the accuracy you scored is the most useful thing a reader can see. If you’d rather do it with someone watching, come to one of the [free events](/events/), where readings are drawn at random and compared at the end.
