Samhain is the old Gaelic festival that closes the harvest and opens winter, and for many people it’s the night when the veil between the living and the dead is thinnest. I can’t tell you whether the veil thins. I can tell you that October is the month people finally ask — and here is a three-card spread to give that question somewhere to land.

## What is Samhain? {#what-is-samhain}

Samhain (say it “SOW-in”) is a Gaelic seasonal festival that marks the end of the harvest and the start of winter, the darker half of the year. It runs from sunset on 31 October to sunset on 1 November (the day used to begin at nightfall), roughly halfway between the autumn equinox and the winter solstice. It’s one of four Gaelic seasonal festivals, with Imbolc, Bealtaine and Lughnasa.

It was kept in Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man, with cousins elsewhere: Calan Gaeaf in Wales, Kalan Goañv in Brittany. Bonfires, feasting, dressing up and fortune-telling games all belonged to the night, and many Halloween customs come from it. Today, many modern Pagans and witches celebrate it as one of the eight festivals of the Wheel of the Year, often by remembering their ancestors.

Picture a farm family on the last evening of October: the crop is in, the cattle are down from the summer hills, the fire is lit, and everyone is counting what they have to last the winter. **Samhain is, first of all, a stocktake.**

## Does the veil really thin at Samhain? {#the-veil}

“The veil thins” means this: at Samhain, the boundary between our world and the other one — the dead, the fairy folk, the Otherworld of the old Irish stories — is believed to be at its most open. Spirits can cross, and the living can sense them more easily. For a lot of people it’s the heart of the festival.

My own position is narrower. I don’t use tarot to call anyone, at Samhain or on any other night, because with spirits you don’t know who you’re calling. And when a reading in October feels heavier, I look first at the person in front of me: the dark coming at five, the anniversary they haven’t mentioned, the year catching up with them. Not everything is about spirits. Sometimes it’s grief, and sometimes it’s [a door that belongs to a therapist rather than to me](/disclaimers/#therapy).

There is a veil in the deck, by the way: behind the High Priestess, between her two pillars, embroidered with pomegranates. She doesn’t lift it; she sits in front of it, calm, knowing more than she says. A better model for October than any summoning — more on [what the veil means in tarot](/library/tarot-symbols/veil/).

## Why October is when people finally ask {#why-october}

Because for a few weeks, tarot is allowed. It’s in shop windows, on party tables, in every feed. Someone who wouldn’t think of asking for a reading in March will pull a card at a Halloween party, and the question they’ve carried since spring comes out with it.

Three other things push the same way. The end of the year comes into view, and people count what worked. The evenings close in, and we turn inward. And this is the season of the dead — All Saints, All Souls, the Day of the Dead — so a grief that sat quietly all year rises to the surface. If that’s where you are, [what tarot can hold in grief, and when you need a person](/blog/tarot-for-grief/) is worth reading before any spread.

Whatever brings you to the table, the silence rule doesn’t change with the calendar. Keep your question to yourself; you’ll know at the end whether it was answered.

## A three-card Samhain spread {#samhain-spread}

Lay three cards in a row, left to right. Each position is a question you ask yourself, not a prediction.

1. *What I’m harvesting.* What this year actually gave you — something finished, earned or learned. Not what you hoped for: what came in.
2. *What I’m leaving with the old season.* A habit, a worry, a way of doing things that belonged to the summer and doesn’t need to come into winter with you.
3. *What I’m keeping warm.* The one thing — a project, a friendship, a small pleasure — you want to look after through the dark months.

Read the three as one sentence before you read them one by one, then write that sentence down.

## A Samhain spread, as an example {#example}

Say someone draws the Nine of Pentacles, the Eight of Cups and the Four of Wands.

The harvest is the [Nine of Pentacles](/library/tarot-cards/nine-of-pentacles/). Nine is the finish line of a cycle; Pentacles are Earth — matter, money, whatever has value for you. A woman stands in her own garden among the grapes, a bird on her glove. Something was built, and it paid off.

What stays behind is the Eight of Cups: a figure walking away from eight cups stacked with care. Cups are emotion; this eight is moving on from what you’re already doing. Probably not a person — a way of caring that has run its course.

What stays warm is the Four of Wands: four is the stable ground after the effort, Fire is the energy that keeps you going, and the drawing is a garland, two people raising flowers, a house behind them. Foundations. Home.

Out loud: “This year paid you back for something you built. There’s an emotional habit you’re ready to put down — you’ve already turned your back on it. For winter, look after home: the people you celebrate with, the place you come back to. Does that land?” If it doesn’t, note it as a miss.
