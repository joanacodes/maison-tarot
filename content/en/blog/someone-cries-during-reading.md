Stop, stay, and let them cry. Tears during a reading rarely mean something went wrong; often they mean something was named for the first time. Your job in that moment isn't to fix anything. It's to be warm, calm and in no hurry.

## Why people cry during a reading {#why-people-cry}

I know this from the other chair. A pick-a-card video by Kloee Taylor once made me sob, not tear up. It was the first time anyone had put words on my situation, and partway through, the reader cried too. What made me cry was hearing my situation said out loud by someone else. I've told [the story of that video](/blog/do-pick-a-card-readings-work/) in full.

People cry in readings for all sorts of reasons: relief, recognition, a grief they've been holding for months, tiredness, the strangeness of hearing a stranger say something true. Very few of those are sadness about a card.

Say you draw the Six of Cups for someone: two children, a cup full of white flowers, an old courtyard. My notes read it as taking time for yourself, childhood, flowering again. You say something about rest, about being allowed to slow down. Nothing sad. And the person across from you starts crying, because nobody has told them that in a long time.

**Tears usually mean something landed, not that something went wrong.**

## What to do in the moment {#in-the-moment}

1. Stop talking. Finish your sentence, then leave space.
2. Put the cards down, or slide them aside. The reading can wait.
3. Offer water and tissues. Keep both within reach before you start; it quietly says that crying is allowed here.
4. Say something short and warm: "Take your time. There's no rush." That's enough.
5. Don't touch without asking. Some people want a hand on the arm; others need room.
6. When they're ready, ask what they'd like: carry on, pause, or stop. All three are fine answers.

Silence feels long to the reader and short to the person crying. You don't need to fill it. If it helps, breathe slowly yourself; calm is catching.

Online, the same steps apply with one more. On a video call, offer them a moment off camera. In a group call or a live, offer to finish privately, away from the others.

## What not to do {#what-not-to-do}

- Don't draw another card to make it better. A hurried "let's see what comes next" turns their moment into your discomfort, and [one more card muddies the reading](/blog/the-clarifier-card/) anyway.
- Don't rush to the positive. "But it's a good card really" tells them their feeling was a mistake.
- Don't read the tears as a sign. They belong to the person, not to the card.
- Don't apologise as if you'd done harm. "I'm sorry this is hard" is kind; "I'm sorry, I shouldn't have said that" leaves them comforting you.
- Don't carry on as if nothing happened. The reading changed the moment they started crying; follow it.

If what made them cry was a hard card, the way you put it into words matters even more from here on. I've written about [how to say hard things kindly](/blog/bad-news-tarot-reading/).

## If you cry too {#if-you-cry-too}

It happens, and it isn't a failure. In the video that made me sob, the reader cried too, and that video is where it all started for me.

What matters is keeping your own feelings while you feel theirs. That's what empathy means to me: feeling and understanding the other person's emotions while keeping your own. Very empathic readers can end up feeling, even expressing, the other person's emotion. Let a tear come if it comes, but stay the steady one, so the person doesn't end up looking after you.

Afterwards, rest. Interpreting takes energy, and a reading with tears in it takes more. Don't go straight into the next one.

## When it's a door for someone else {#the-therapists-door}

Some tears are about something that's been there a long time: a loss, a fear, a weight carried alone. A reading can name it. It can't hold it.

Not everything is about spirits. Sometimes it's psychological, sometimes more, and the right door is a therapist's, a doctor's, or a trusted friend's. Say it once, kindly: "I think this deserves more time than a reading can give it. Is there someone you can talk to about it?" No pressure, no lecture. If the tears are about a loss, [what the cards can hold in grief, and when you need a person](/blog/tarot-for-grief/) may help. The [disclaimers page](/disclaimers/#therapy) says plainly where my role ends, and [where the door is](/blog/tarot-and-mental-health/) has more.

Then close the reading gently rather than letting it trail off. A few calm words, a glass of water finished, a moment before they stand up. There's a short guide to [ending a tarot reading well](/blog/how-to-end-a-tarot-reading/), and it matters most on days like this.
