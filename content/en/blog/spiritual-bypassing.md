Spiritual bypassing is using spiritual ideas or practices to step around something painful you’d otherwise have to face: a feeling, a conflict, a decision. Tarot can be used that way. Noticing when it happens is most of the work, and it doesn’t call for any harshness towards yourself.

## What is spiritual bypassing? {#what-is-it}

The term comes from the psychotherapist John Welwood, who described it in the 1980s: using spiritual practice to avoid unresolved feelings, old wounds and ordinary human needs. In plain words, it’s reaching for a spiritual answer when what’s in front of you is a human problem.

An example. Say someone’s closest friend hurts them badly. Instead of telling the friend, or letting themselves be angry, they decide it was “a karmic lesson”, say they’re “grateful for the growth”, and move on — on paper. Six months later they still can’t hear the friend’s name without a knot in their stomach. The spiritual idea did nothing wrong. It was used to skip a step.

**Bypassing isn’t believing in something; it’s using that belief to avoid a feeling.**

## What it looks like with tarot {#with-tarot}

A few patterns I’d watch for, in yourself or in a reader:

- Drawing a card every morning so that you never have to decide anything on your own.
- Asking whether someone will come back instead of letting yourself grieve. I wrote about [that question, and a kinder one](/blog/will-they-come-back-tarot/).
- Reframing every miss, so that you never have to hear “no”.
- Calling every hard week “energy” or “spirits” when it might be tiredness, worry, or something a professional should look at.
- “It’s my sign”, used to skip an apology. [Astrology as an excuse](/blog/astrology-is-not-an-excuse/) is a close cousin of bypassing.
- Positive thinking used as a lid: repeating that everything is fine so you don’t have to check whether it is. There’s a real difference between [an affirmation and toxic positivity](/blog/affirmations-vs-toxic-positivity/).

There’s a card that pictures this well. The Eight of Swords shows a woman bound and blindfolded, standing among eight swords planted in the ground. In my notes she’s a prisoner of herself while she has every tool she needs to get out: she can take off the blindfold.

Bypassing is a blindfold we tie ourselves, usually for good reasons. [The Eight of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/eight-of-swords/) doesn’t blame her for it. It points at the knot.

## Why we do it, and why it isn’t a character flaw {#why}

Because it protects us. The ego, as I see it, isn’t a mean part of you: it tries to make sense of the world and to shield you from its absurdity and its violence. Bypassing is one of its shields. Sometimes it gets you through a week you couldn’t have got through otherwise.

The trouble starts when the shield stays up. Shadow work, as I define it, is dealing with the things you keep pushing away so you can get through daily life — the same things that end up disabling you. What you step around doesn’t disappear. It works like water: a neglected emotion seeps into the foundations slowly, the way damp eats a house from the inside.

## How to tell a practice from a bypass {#how-to-tell}

Four questions to ask yourself after a reading:

1. Did it lead me to do something, or to keep waiting?
2. Do I feel more, or less? A reading that helps usually lets a feeling through; a bypass makes it go quiet.
3. Could I have heard a “no”? If only one answer was acceptable, the reading was a permission slip.
4. Is this the same question I asked last week?

If the answers worry you, put the deck in a drawer for a few weeks. It isn’t a punishment; it gives the muscle that decides a chance to work. I wrote about that in [why you should quit tarot reading, for a while](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/).

## What helps instead {#what-helps}

Gently, and roughly in this order:

1. Say the plain sentence before you shuffle: “I’m sad.” “I’m angry.” “I’m scared of deciding.” If you can say it, you may not need the cards for this one.
2. Ask about yourself, not about the situation or the other person.
3. Be as gentle and tolerant with yourself as you are with others. That line is from my own notes on shadow work, and it’s the one I come back to most.
4. When it’s heavier than cards can hold, open another door. Not everything is about spirits: sometimes it’s psychological, and a therapist is the right person. [I say where that door is](/disclaimers/#therapy), and seeing one isn’t your practice failing.

If you want somewhere to start, [shadow work for beginners](/blog/shadow-work-beginners/) begins with one card and one honest question about the thing you’ve been walking around.
