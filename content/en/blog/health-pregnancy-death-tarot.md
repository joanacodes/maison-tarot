I don’t read on health, pregnancy or death. People bring these questions because they’re frightened, or because they love someone, and both deserve care. So this isn’t a closed door. It’s an explanation of why the cards are the wrong tool here, what they can hold instead, and how to be with someone who asks.

## Why people bring these questions to the cards {#why-people-ask}

Most people who ask are waiting. For scan results. For a test to turn positive, or not to. For a call from the hospital about a parent. Waiting is its own kind of hard: nothing you do seems to change anything, and the mind fills the empty days with the worst version.

In that state, a deck of cards can look like a window. A picture to hold, an answer of any kind, something that ends the not-knowing. Wanting that isn’t foolish. It’s what fear and love do when they have nowhere to go.

**The question is rarely about the cards; it’s about how to get through the wait.**

## Why I step back: the deeper reasons {#why-i-step-back}

I’ve set out the short version, with a kinder question for each subject, in [questions to avoid asking tarot](/blog/questions-to-avoid-asking-tarot/). Here is the longer why.

A card can’t see a body. It reads a moment, in pictures. Any link from a picture to an organ, a pregnancy or a date is something the reader adds, and the frightened person in front of them can’t tell where the card ends and the reader begins.

Timing is where readings miss most, and these questions are nearly all about timing: when, how long, by spring. By my own count, about one reading in ten misses. On most subjects a miss is a line in my notebook. Here it can be someone delaying an appointment, or grieving something that hasn’t happened.

And words stay. I know this from my own life. After my closest friend died of cancer, I asked silently, in anger, “who’s next?” and heard a man’s voice give me another friend’s name and the words “is going to have an accident”. Two weeks later, that friend’s car was hit by a truck. That friend is alive and well today. The information was wrong in what mattered. Since then, I keep that kind of thing on a shelf labelled “sceptical, but if it happens, I was warned”, and I don’t hand it to anyone else. The full story is in [why a reading can be wrong](/blog/why-a-reading-can-be-wrong/).

Even the card called Death isn’t about dying.

It speaks of the harvest, of clearing the ground and starting again. Reading a literal death into it is a projection, and a frightening one. The [Death card page](/library/tarot-cards/death/) goes through it gently.

## What a reading can hold: the waiting {#the-waiting}

A reading can’t hold the result. It can hold you, while you wait.

Waiting has a shape. There’s rehearsing the worst at 3 a.m. There’s being strong for everyone else and having nowhere to put your own fear. There’s everything you’ve put on hold, and sometimes guilt about the ordinary things you still want. A picture can help you look at those.

So the question changes from “what will happen?” to “how am I carrying this?” Or: “What do I need this week?” “Who could sit with me in this?” “What have I stopped doing for myself?” If the worry is a pregnancy, I go further in [tarot and pregnancy](/blog/tarot-cards-pregnancy/); after a death, in [what the cards can hold in grief](/blog/tarot-for-grief/).

## Who can answer what the cards can’t {#who-to-see}

- For symptoms or results: your GP or doctor, or the specialist who ordered the test. If it’s urgent, call emergency services, not a reader.
- For a pregnancy: a test, then a GP, midwife or gynaecologist; for fertility, a fertility clinic.
- For someone who is dying: their medical team and, if there is one, the palliative care team, who are there for the family too.
- For grief, or for fear that has started to fill every day: a counsellor, a therapist or a support group. That isn’t the cards failing; it’s [the right door](/disclaimers/#therapy).

## If someone asks you {#if-someone-asks-you}

If you read for friends, this question will come, often late in the evening, often as a joke that isn’t one. How you answer matters more than any card.

1. Listen first. Let them say the whole thing before you say anything about tarot.
2. Name what you hear: “You’re really worried about your dad.” People often need that more than an answer.
3. Say gently why you won’t read on it: “I don’t read on that, because a wrong word could stay with you for a long time.”
4. Offer what you can: a reading about how they’re coping, a walk, a lift to the appointment, a message the night before the results.
5. Don’t argue with their hope or their fear. Neither needs correcting tonight.
6. If it surfaces in the middle of a reading, set that thread down kindly and come back to what the cards can hold.

What they’ll remember isn’t the reading you didn’t do. It’s that you stayed. And if the tears come, as they sometimes do, here is [what to do when someone cries during a reading](/blog/someone-cries-during-reading/).
