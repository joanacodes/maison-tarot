Search "tarot" and the algorithm will show you candles within a minute. That's not because tarot is witchcraft; it's because the same audience buys both, and the same accounts sell both. The bundling is commercial, not historical. Let me untangle it, because it puts people off who'd be good readers.

## What tarot is {#what-tarot-is}

Seventy-eight cards, first printed in fifteenth-century Italy as a game. The occult reading of them came four centuries later, in France; the deck almost everyone uses now was drawn in 1909 in England by a woman named Pamela Colman Smith. Reading the cards means looking at the pictures and saying what they show about a person or a situation. That's the whole practice. [The history, sourced, is in the Library](/library/tarot-history/).

Nothing in it requires a belief. You can read as a psychologist, as a storyteller, as a sceptic testing it, or as a mystic. The cards don't care.

## What witchcraft is {#what-witchcraft-is}

A very broad word for practices — spellwork, ritual, working with plants, seasons, deities or none — that aim to affect the world or the self through intention. For some it's a religion (Wicca, various paganisms), for others a craft without belief, for others an aesthetic. It has its own history, mostly unrelated to cards until the twentieth century.

Some witches use tarot. Some don't. Most tarot readers aren't witches. The overlap is real but partial, like guitarists and singers.

## Why it matters for your search history {#search-history}

Because the bundling has two costs.

The first is for the curious. Someone who'd like to learn to read cards sees the candles, the moon phases, the "cleanse your deck under the full moon" advice, and decides this isn't for them — when none of it is required. The [House's whole method](/learn-tarot/) runs without a single ritual: five lenses, a notebook, someone who tells you nothing.

The second is for the believer. Someone whose religion forbids witchcraft assumes tarot falls under it, and never finds out that a deck of pictures used for reflection is a different thing. [I've written separately about tarot and religion](/blog/is-tarot-a-sin/); the short version is that most of the objections target things I don't do.

## What I keep and what I dropped {#what-i-keep}

I'll be honest about my own path. In 2021 I sold sage, candles and "channelled" cards. It was the aesthetic of the moment and I was in it. I stopped, not because I decided witchcraft was wrong — that's not my call — but because none of it improved a reading, and I'd started counting readings. The notebook didn't know the deck had been cleansed. The person across the table didn't know either.

What I kept is the part that's testable: pictures, attention, honesty about misses. If that makes me a witch, the word has lost its meaning. If it makes me a reader, that's the word I use.

## If you want both {#if-you-want-both}

Fine. Many people practise witchcraft and read cards, and the cards work the same for them. What I'd ask is only that you keep them distinct in your head: the reading is what the picture says; the ritual is what you bring to it. When the two blur, readers start believing the deck has moods, needs cleansing, and shouldn't be touched by others — and that's where cardboard starts being treated as a person, which is where the honest practice ends.
