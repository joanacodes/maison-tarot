Almost everyone makes the same dozen mistakes in their first month with tarot. None of them means you can't read. Each one has a fix, and most fixes take less effort than the mistake.

## Before you shuffle {#before-you-shuffle}

### 1. Memorising the booklet

The little book in the box gives each card a few keywords, and the first instinct is to learn all 78. You end up reciting instead of reading, and you freeze on the first card you've forgotten. Learn instead three questions you can ask any card — its colour, its number, its element. [The cheat sheet](/blog/tarot-cheat-sheet/) fits on one page.

### 2. Waiting to feel gifted

Plenty of beginners wait for a sign that they're “one of those people”. No such group exists. Among the people I've helped, the third try was the latest anyone needed, once they stopped trying to be right. Talent varies, as it does with drawing; practice is what makes the reader. Whatever you're waiting to feel, you're more likely to feel it after the first card than before.

### 3. Buying the ritual before learning the deck

Sage, crystals, a full-moon cleanse, a second and third deck by week two. None of it teaches you to read. **One Rider-Waite-Smith deck is enough for your first year**; add one lens, a notebook and someone who tells you nothing, and you have the whole kit from [the method I teach beginners](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/).

## While you read {#while-you-read}

### 4. Starting with ten cards

The Celtic Cross looks like real tarot, so people try it on day two. Ten cards talk at once and you hear none of them. Start with one card a day, describing what's on it, what you see and what you receive. Then three. Then more, when three starts to feel small.

### 5. Looking it up before looking at it

Draw, glance, reach for the phone. The card never gets a chance. Say what you see first, out loud and in plain words — “a man carrying too many sticks, head buried in the bundle, a house not far ahead” — and only then open the book, if at all. You'll be surprised how often you'd already said it.

### 6. Reading reversals from week one

Reversed meanings double what you're trying to hold before you hold anything. Keep every card upright for now. The colour, number and element already tell you what a card is about; its neighbours tell you whether it's flowing or stuck. When you do add reversals later, add them on purpose for a month, and note whether they change what you actually say.

### 7. Reshuffling until you like the answer

The Tower comes out, you sigh, you shuffle again. The second draw isn't a fresh answer; it's an answer you went shopping for. One question, one draw, written down. **If you can't bear the answer, that tells you something about the question.**

## When you read for someone else {#for-someone-else}

### 8. Asking them to explain first

It feels polite to ask what's going on. But once you know, every card bends towards the story you've heard, and you'll never know what you read and what you repeated. Ask for nothing — [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/) — and let them tell you at the end what held.

### 9. Reading someone who didn't ask

A friend across the room at a party, a colleague, an ex out of curiosity. Always ask before reading someone, and read only the person in front of you. You wouldn't want a stranger going through your bedroom; reading someone without their consent is the same.

### 10. Answering every question

Someone asks about a scan, a pregnancy, a court date, or whether to put their savings into something. They ask because they're worried, and you'll want to help. **A wrong word there does real damage**, so these are subjects I don't read on. I'd rather [say so gently](/disclaimers/#refusals), ask what they'd like to feel clearer about, and leave the rest to a doctor, a lawyer or an adviser.

## With yourself {#with-yourself}

### 11. Keeping no notes — and rescuing the misses

Without a notebook, you remember the readings that landed and forget the rest. Write the date, the cards, what you said and what was confirmed — [a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) can be a school exercise book. When something doesn't land, write “miss”, and leave it there: no extra card to rescue it, no “it'll make sense later”. Even now, roughly one of my readings in ten ends up with that word next to it.

### 12. Pulling cards every hour

Three pulls before lunch on the same worry isn't practice. It's looking for reassurance outside yourself, and cards make poor reassurance. If you notice the habit, give the deck a week off. Nothing is lost, and you'll read better when you come back. The question you keep asking usually needs a decision or a conversation, not another card.

## What to do in month two {#month-two}

Pick the one mistake on this list you recognise most, and change only that for a month. Keep the notebook going. And if you're wondering how long the whole thing takes, I've written [an honest timeline](/blog/how-long-to-learn-tarot/): first readings in minutes, fluency in months. If you'd rather practise on people who'll tell you nothing, that's what the [free events](/events/) are for.
