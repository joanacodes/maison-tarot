These three models sell tarot in small pieces: one question, one month, one add-on. Each piece looks affordable, and that’s the point; the cost lives in the total. Nobody has to be dishonest for that to happen, so it helps to know how the model pays before you do.

## Three ways to sell a reading in pieces {#three-models}

Pay-per-question means you pay for one question and receive one answer: written, recorded, or spoken during a live. Take 12 € a question as a model price. One question costs 12 €. A follow-up about the same person, “and what about him?”, is another 12 €. One question a week for a year comes to 624 €.

A subscription is a monthly fee for a set number of readings, questions or messages, or for access to a reader’s private content. It renews by itself until you stop it. Say a plan gives you three questions a month. In a calm month you use none and pay anyway; in a hard one you use all three in a day, and you’re offered more.

An extended reading is an upgrade offered during or after a short one: “the full picture”, “a deeper look at what’s blocking you”, “what this card is hiding”. You’ve paid for the first part of the story; the rest costs more.

Say a short reading ends on the Three of Swords.

“Someone close to you is hiding something. The extended reading will show who.” The card has more than one side: heartbreak, yes, and also, in my notes, a blood pact, a promise sealed in pain. A complete reading would have said both, then stopped.

These models are popular for understandable reasons. A small amount feels safer than an hour’s fee, a written answer can be read in private, and a subscription can feel like having someone in your corner.

## Who earns what {#who-earns-what}

Each model rewards something, and in none of them is it the quality of the answer.

Per question, the seller earns more when you ask more. Short answers, open endings and follow-up questions are good business. Per month, the seller earns most from people who keep paying, whether they use the service often or forget it’s there. An extended reading earns most when the first reading stops at the interesting part. And on a platform, the price is split: the platform keeps a share, and the reader’s income depends on how much they sell. That puts two parties on the side of volume.

**In all three models, the seller earns most while you’re still unsure.**

That doesn’t make everyone who uses them dishonest. Plenty of readers work inside these models because a platform set things up that way, and they do their best within them. It means the frame pulls in one direction, and it’s worth knowing which. When the unit is the minute rather than the question, the pull is the same; I’ve looked at that in [overcharged psychic phone lines](/blog/psychic-phone-lines-france/).

## Upselling and urgency {#upselling-and-urgency}

These models leave room for a handful of familiar moves:

- “I’m seeing more, but I’d need an extended reading to go into it.”
- A first reading that ends on a cliffhanger.
- “Your energy is shifting fast; ask again this week.”
- A discount for buying five questions now.
- A free first question, and a price on the follow-up.

At the far end, the “more” turns out to be a curse that needs lifting, and that’s [a script of its own](/blog/lift-the-curse-scam/).

There’s a quieter move too: what happens after a miss. By my count, about one reading in ten misses, and when one of mine does, I say so and stop there. In a model paid by the question, a miss is also a reason to sell another: rephrase it, add a card, try the extended version. A miss is a miss. It doesn’t need a follow-up you pay for.

Asking the same question again and again belongs here too. The answer rarely changes; the worry does. I’ve written about [what repeat readings do to you](/blog/ask-tarot-the-same-question-twice/), and why the kindest thing is sometimes to stop asking for a while.

## Before you pay {#before-you-pay}

1. Ask for the total, not the unit price: how much for everything you’re about to buy?
2. Know what you’ll receive, in what form, and when.
3. For a subscription, note the renewal date and price, and find the cancel button before you subscribe.
4. Ask what the reader doesn’t read on. “Nothing” is an answer.
5. Set your own ceiling for the month, and stop when you reach it.

## How to cancel {#how-to-cancel}

Cancel where you subscribed. If you paid through an app store, the subscription usually lives in your store account, and deleting the app doesn’t stop it. If you paid a reader or a platform directly, use their cancellation page or write to them, and keep the confirmation. If payments carry on after that, your bank can tell you how to stop them.

There’s no shame in any of this. These models are built to feel small, one piece at a time, and they work on careful people. Adding up three months of statements is often the clearest thing you can do, and it takes ten minutes.
