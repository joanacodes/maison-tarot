The Celtic Cross is a ten-card spread: six cards laid in a cross, four in a column beside it, each with its own question about one situation. It’s the most famous layout in tarot and the one beginners most often drown in — not because it’s a bad spread, but because ten cards arrive before you’ve learned to read one.

## What the Celtic Cross is {#what-it-is}

Arthur Edward Waite printed this spread in the guide he wrote for the Rider-Waite-Smith deck, and it has travelled with the deck ever since. The shape is always the same:

- card 1 in the centre, and card 2 laid across it — the small cross at the heart;
- cards 3 to 6 around them: above, below, to the left, to the right;
- cards 7 to 10 in a column to the right of the cross, from the bottom up.

You’ll find books that number the four cards around the centre in a different order. Pick one version, write it down, and keep it: a layout that changes every week can’t teach you anything.

Don’t confuse it with the cross spread taught in France, which shares the outline of a cross and little else: five cards, no column, and a centre card you calculate by adding up the other four. That one has its own page, [the French cross spread](/blog/cross-tarot-spread/); this one is about the ten-card Celtic Cross.

Waite began by choosing one card to stand for the person — a significator — and laid the spread over it. I leave that step out. [I read without a significator](/blog/significator-card/), so card 1 comes from the shuffle like all the others.

## The ten positions, one by one {#positions}

1. The heart of the matter. What’s going on now — the situation as it stands, often from an angle the person hasn’t put into words.
2. What crosses it. The force working across the first card: an obstacle, a tension, sometimes a help. Read it with card 1, never alone.
3. Above: what you’re aiming for. What you’re aware of, or the best that could come of this.
4. Below: the foundation. What sits underneath — older, deeper, often unspoken. The root that feeds card 1.
5. Behind: what’s passing. The recent past, still in the room but on its way out.
6. Ahead: what’s coming. The next few weeks, the next step that’s already forming.
7. You. Your attitude: how you stand in all this, what you bring to it.
8. Around you. Your surroundings — home, work, the atmosphere you live in. Not what a particular person thinks; they haven’t asked to be read.
9. Hopes and fears. Often one card is both: what you want most can be what scares you most.
10. Where it’s heading. The direction if nothing changes. Not a verdict.

The first six cards describe the situation; the last four describe you inside it. **The cross is the weather; the column is you walking through it.**

## Why beginners feel lost in it {#why-lost}

Four reasons, and none of them is a lack of talent.

Ten positions to hold in your head while you look at ten pictures. Your attention goes into remembering which slot is which, and the cards stop talking.

Positions that overlap. “Above” and “hopes and fears” both sound like what you want; “below” and “behind” both sound like the past. Until you’ve put the difference into your own words, two cards end up saying the same thing.

The eye goes straight to card 10. Many books call it the outcome, so everyone reads it first and the other nine become a waiting room.

And contradictions. Ten cards almost always hold a hopeful one and a heavy one. Beginners try to make them agree, when the tension between them is often the reading itself.

There’s a fifth trap for anyone who reads for others. If you know the question before you lay the cards, ten cards give you ten ways to confirm it. With the silence rule — nothing said to me before the cards are down — card 1 becomes a test: it describes their situation or it doesn’t. My misses run at roughly one in ten, and a big spread doesn’t change that. It only gives you more cards to hide behind.

## A lighter way in {#lighter-way}

Learn it in three stages, a week or so each.

1. The cross: two cards. Lay only cards 1 and 2 — what’s going on, and what crosses it — and read them as one sentence. That alone is a good spread.
2. The circle: six cards. Add the four around them. You now have the whole situation, still in one or two sentences.
3. The full ten. Add the column, and read the spread in three sentences rather than ten: one for the cross, one for the circle, one for the column.

Two habits help from the first day. Write the ten positions on a slip of paper and lay it beside the spread, so your memory isn’t doing the work. And read the lenses across all ten cards before any position: how many major arcana, which element leads, which colour dominates, whether a number repeats. Six Cups out of ten make it about feelings whatever the question was. No major at all, and the situation is in ordinary hands: yours.

**If the full ten still feels like too much, that’s information, not failure.** [A three-card spread](/blog/three-card-tarot-spread/) answers most questions, and [whether you need a set spread at all](/blog/do-you-need-tarot-spreads/) is a fair thing to ask.

## A Celtic Cross, worked through {#example}

Say someone sits down and tells you nothing — no question, no context. You shuffle and lay the cross and the circle: the Eight of Cups at the heart, the Four of Pentacles across it, the Star above, the Six of Cups below, the Five of Wands behind, the Page of Pentacles ahead.

Then the column, from the bottom up: the Two of Swords, the Three of Cups, the Nine of Swords and the Ace of Wands.

Lenses first. One major arcanum out of ten, the [Star](/library/tarot-cards/the-star/), so this is a life-sized situation, in the person’s own hands. Cups are the strongest suit, three of the nine minors: it’s about what they feel, first of all. The colours: the night sky of the Eight of Cups and the black wall of the Nine of Swords; the grey of the Two of Swords; then the green leaves of the Ace of Wands in the last place. The dark sits at the heart and in the fears. The green is where it’s heading.

The cross. A figure in a red cloak walks away by night from eight stacked cups — for me, the card of leaving behind what you’re in the middle of — while across it a crowned man hugs a coin to his chest. An 8 crossed by a 4, Water crossed by Earth: leaving, and holding on to the security that was built.

The circle. Above, the Star: a woman pouring water from two jugs under a large yellow star — hope, renewal. Below, the Six of Cups: children and white flowers, something loved long ago. Behind, the Five of Wands: the noise and the quarrels, passing. Ahead, the Page of Pentacles, gazing at the single coin he holds up — learning, starting small.

The column. You: the Two of Swords, a woman blindfolded with two swords crossed over her chest, almost everything grey — undecided, and not quite looking. Around you: the Three of Cups, three women raising their cups — friends who’d celebrate with you. Hopes and fears: the Nine of Swords, someone sitting up in bed at night, face in hands — the 3 a.m. version of all this, which my notes also read as coming out of a long troubled stretch. Where it’s heading: the Ace of Wands, its green-leaved wand held out from the clouds — Fire, number 1, a new spark.

Three sentences, out loud:

“You’re walking away from something that used to fill you, and part of you is holding on to the security it gave. What draws you is hope, and something you loved long ago; the quarrels are behind you, and what’s ahead is learning, starting small. You haven’t decided and you’re not quite looking yet; your friends are on your side, the nights are the hard part, and the direction, if you keep going, is a fresh start.”

Then the silence rule pays off: the person says nothing until I’ve finished, then tells me what held. If the heart of the matter doesn’t land, the nine other cards don’t get to rescue it, and there’s no eleventh card to clarify it.

## What I do with card ten {#card-ten}

Card 10 gets read last, and lightly. It’s a direction, not a prophecy: where things go if the person carries on as they are. If they don’t like it, the nine cards before it usually show where the course can change.

Tarot isn’t an authority: your own sense of things comes before the card in tenth place. And a big spread doesn’t open the door to what I leave aside: your health or anyone’s, death, pregnancy, an ongoing trial, a money decision, a person who hasn’t agreed to be read. [My refusals page](/disclaimers/#refusals) explains why, and the reasons hold for ten cards as much as for one.
