No, tarot doesn’t have to be free, and a reader who charges isn’t doing anything wrong. Time, training and attention are work, and work gets paid. What I want to look at is what money does to a practice once it becomes a product — because it changes things quietly, even for kind people — and why I ended up selling lessons and giving readings away.

## Why people say tarot should be free {#why-free}

The idea usually rests on two older beliefs. The first is that reading is a gift, and a gift shouldn’t be sold. The second is that money spoils the reading, as if a price could blur the cards.

I don’t hold either. There’s no gift to sell: anyone can learn to read, and the people I’ve helped all got there by their third try. What’s left is a skill, and selling a skill is ordinary.

As for money spoiling accuracy, I have no evidence of it. My own count of misses — roughly one in ten — comes from readings I gave for free, so free didn’t make me more accurate either.

Under the slogan, though, there’s a fair worry. When people say “it should be free”, they often mean “it shouldn’t profit from people who are scared”. **That part is right, and it’s a question of how a reader charges, not whether.**

## The case for charging {#case-for-charging}

A reading takes longer than the reading. There’s the preparation, the hour itself, and the tiredness afterwards: interpreting takes energy, and I’ve learned to stop after two hours. A reader who does this for a living has rent, a phone bill and taxes like anyone else.

A price also sets a frame. It says when the session starts, when it ends, and what was agreed. Without one, reading for strangers can slide into being on call, with messages at midnight and “one more quick question” that’s never quick.

Two cards hold this side of the argument for me. The Eight of Pentacles is the craftsperson at the bench: mastery, necessary effort, a business that works because the work is good. The Six of Pentacles holds the scales: knowing the value of what you give and what you take.

So when I look at someone else’s practice, I don’t ask whether they charge. I ask what I’d ask of any honest trade. **The [red flags of a fake reader](/blog/how-to-spot-a-fake-tarot-reader/) are never the price itself; they’re what the price is attached to.**

## What happens when a practice becomes a product {#practice-to-product}

The incentive changes. A practice asks “did this help?”. A product asks “will they come back, and what else can I sell them?”. Nobody has to be greedy for that second question to start steering; it’s arithmetic.

You can watch it drift in three directions:

- Towards the questions that sell. “Is he coming back?”, “Will I get pregnant?”, “Should I take the money?” are the questions people most want answered, and the ones where a wrong word does most damage. They’re on my list of subjects I don’t read on.
- Towards repeat visits. Sessions that end on a cliffhanger, pay-per-question, [subscriptions and “extended readings”](/blog/tarot-subscriptions/). Each one rewards the reader when you need them again.
- Towards objects. I sold sage, candles and “channelled” cards in 2021, and I explain [why I stopped](/blog/cleanse-tarot-deck/): nothing in that box improved a reading, and a shop pays you for selling things, not for reading well.

None of this needs a villain. It needs a slow month and a calendar to fill.

## Free isn’t automatically pure {#free-isnt-pure}

Free has its own traps. A free reading is often the opening move of a sale: the first card costs nothing, the “blockage” it reveals costs a lot. If you’ve ever wondered how to tell a present from a hook, I wrote down [what to check before a free online reading](/blog/before-a-free-online-reading/).

Free can also wear out the reader, especially the generous ones who end up reading for hours for people who don’t think to ask how they are.

And I’ll be honest about my own events. The readings there are free, but the events are also a way for people to meet me before booking a workshop, and I mention the workshops exactly once, at the end. A free reading at Maison Tarot is still part of a business; it’s a business that doesn’t sell readings.

I can afford that because lessons pay. Not every reader has that option, and judging one who doesn’t would be unfair.

## My answer: lessons paid, readings free {#my-answer}

I sell lessons: one-to-one sessions of sixty minutes, and three-hour workshops for twelve people, one lens each. Readings happen at free events, drawn at random, in front of everyone.

The reason is the incentive again. If readings paid my rent, I’d depend on people coming back for more of them. Lessons work the other way: they’ve done their job when a student needs me less. And at a free event, saying “that’s a miss” costs me nothing, so I say it every time.

[What I’m paid for, and what costs you nothing](/blog/what-i-charge-for/), is set out elsewhere, so I’ll skip it here. If you’re comparing paid readers instead, [what goes into the price of a reading](/blog/tarot-reading-price/) is the companion to this page.

## How to recognise a fair paid reading {#fair-paid-reading}

If you’d rather pay a reader — and plenty of good readers charge — here’s what fair looks like:

1. The price is stated before you start, as a total, not an open meter.
2. Nothing is added afterwards: no candles, no rituals, no “clearing”.
3. The reader tells you what they don’t read on, and why.
4. They can say “that one didn’t land” without rescuing it.
5. The session ends without a cliffhanger.
6. Nobody tells you something is “on you” that only they can remove.

To see what a reading looks like when nothing is for sale afterwards, [come to a free event](/events/). The people I read there tell me nothing first, and you can count my misses with me.
