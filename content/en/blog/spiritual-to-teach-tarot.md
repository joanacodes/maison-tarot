No, you don’t have to be spiritual to teach tarot. You need a method someone else can pick up, a way to check whether a reading held, and respect for everyone at your table — including the people for whom tarot is a spiritual practice.

## What teaching tarot actually asks of you {#what-it-asks}

Teaching means passing something on so that another person can do it without you. With tarot, that something is a way of looking at a card and saying what you see. It isn’t a belief system.

A beginner needs three things from a teacher: a way in that doesn’t depend on memorising 78 meanings, a room where it’s safe to say something wrong, and someone who tells them plainly when a reading missed. None of that requires a spiritual life. It requires patience, a method and honesty.

You can teach art history without being religious, and you can guide people round a cathedral without praying in it. **You teach a way of looking, not a set of beliefs.**

## Why I teach with lenses instead of beliefs {#lenses}

I teach five lenses: colours, the four elements, the numbers 1 to 10, feminine and masculine energies, and the astrological modes, with astrology and symbols as extras. Each one is a way of looking that a sceptic and a believer can use in exactly the same way.

Take the Six of Cups. In my notes, the number 6 is “3 exalted”: the machine is launched and running fast, and it’s possible to rest, even take a holiday. Cups are Water: emotions, relationships. Put the two together and you get something like taking time for yourself, going back to what made you happy as a child, harmony.

A student can get there in two minutes, without believing anything about where a reading comes from. The full set is on [the method page](/method/), and the numbers have their own pages, starting with [the 1–10 cycle](/library/numerology/).

After that, the student adds what comes up: an image, a word, a feeling. That part is personal. It’s where teaching stops and practice begins.

## A medium, not a source {#not-magic}

Tarot cards are a medium. They help you let go and read what’s around you, or around someone else. What comes out comes from you, not from the cards.

That’s why anyone can learn. Nobody I’ve helped has needed more than three tries to read. It works like art: some start with more talent, but practice gets everyone there. The only practice is letting go.

It also means a teacher hands over nothing mysterious. No initiation, no transmission, no gift passed from one hand to the next — I’ve explained why [nobody needs psychic abilities to read](/blog/do-you-need-psychic-abilities/). I started teaching because of one moment: a friend tries a reading, lands on something true, and is the most surprised person in the room. A teacher can’t hand that moment over either; the job is to make room for it.

## For people whose tarot is spiritual {#respect}

For many people, tarot is part of a spiritual life: a conversation with their guides, a ritual at the new moon, a way of meeting something larger than themselves. It’s real for them. It isn’t my place to take it away, rank it, or explain it away.

Say a workshop has two people side by side looking at the Star. One feels her grandmother in the card. The other sees a woman pouring water, so Water, so emotions. Both are reading. I ask both the same thing: say what you see, check it with the person, write down the misses.

Where I draw a line is when spirituality turns into a ladder, with the teacher at the top holding access and the students waiting to be let in. That’s a separate subject, with its own article: [building a practice without becoming a guru](/blog/tarot-practice-without-guru/). And for students worried that tarot clashes with their faith, I’ve written [is tarot a sin?](/blog/is-tarot-a-sin/) with as much respect as I could.

## What you do need to teach tarot {#what-you-need}

If you’re thinking of teaching, here’s the list I’d check myself against.

1. A method you can explain in plain words, so that your students won’t need you for long.
2. A notebook of your own readings: what you said, what was confirmed. Mine has the misses written in, and every student of mine hears the count.
3. A list of subjects you leave out — mine covers medical and legal worries, death, pregnancy, financial choices, curses and absent third parties — and the kindness to explain why.
4. Consent. Ask before reading anyone, and teach your students to ask too.
5. Three words you’re comfortable saying: “I don’t know.”

Nothing on that list asks what you believe. It asks how you work.

## Where to start {#start}

If you want to see a lens-first class from the inside, the [workshops](/workshops/) last three hours, for twelve people, one lens each, with a card on the table before any explanation.

Or start tomorrow, at home. Ask a friend to tell you nothing, hand them one card, and ask them which colour they see first. Then listen.
