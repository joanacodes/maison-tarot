If you think you can’t meditate, you’re probably the person it’s for. Meditation is the art of doing nothing — letting go of control and letting things flow through your mind — and doing nothing turns out to be hard. You don’t need to be good at it. You need two minutes and a timer.

## What meditation is, in plain words {#what-it-is}

Meditation is the practice of sitting with your attention for a set time and letting go of control. Most ways of beginning come down to one movement: notice where your attention has gone, and bring it back, gently, again and again.

Here’s what it looks like. You sit down, you follow your breath, and ten seconds later you’re thinking about an email you forgot to send. You notice. You come back to the breath. Twenty seconds later you’re at the supermarket in your head. You notice, and you come back.

That noticing and coming back isn’t meditation going wrong. **Your mind wandering isn’t the failure; noticing it is the meditation.**

## Why “I can’t meditate” often means you need it most {#i-cant}

In my experience, the people who say they can’t meditate are the ones who need it most.

“I can’t” usually describes a mind that never stops: one that keeps control of everything, plans three steps ahead, and reaches for the phone after thirty seconds of silence. That isn’t a flaw. It’s a mind that has been working very hard, and it has never been asked to put anything down.

Say you tried an app twice. You sat for ten minutes, spent nine of them planning dinner, and decided you’re “not the type”. Nothing was wrong with you. Ten minutes was too long to start with, that’s all. Nobody starts running with a marathon.

## What I noticed, and my first try {#what-i-noticed}

What I noticed in myself, over time: sharper decisions, and less self-doubt. That’s my experience, not a promise, and not a study.

My first meditation is a story I tell as it happened. I had harsh headaches that switched on and off when I approached my desk. I tried meditation for the first time, and within seconds I saw a yellow ribbon and understood: you’re diverging — go home. I moved back to France. Five months later, Covid.

I tell it because it’s true for me, not because meditation will show you ribbons. Most first sessions are much quieter than that, and quiet is fine. And if you have headaches like those, the first door is a doctor’s.

## How to start: two minutes, a timer, your breath {#how-to-start}

1. Pick a moment you already have: after brushing your teeth, before opening your laptop, when the kettle goes on.
2. Set a timer for two minutes. Not ten. The timer means you don’t have to watch the clock.
3. Sit on an ordinary chair, feet flat on the floor, hands on your knees. No cushion, no special posture. Close your eyes or let them rest on a spot on the floor.
4. Breathe as you normally do, and follow the air: in through the nose, out again. Don’t try to change it.
5. If it helps, count: one on the in-breath, two on the out-breath, up to ten, then start again at one.
6. When you notice you’ve drifted — and you will — say “thinking” in your head and go back to one. No telling yourself off.
7. When the timer rings, stop. Don’t grade the session.
8. Do it again tomorrow. After a week, add a minute if you want to.

If you like a small ritual to close it, draw one card afterwards and describe what you see before you think about what it means. The [one-card pull](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/) works well straight after a quiet two minutes.

## When meditation isn’t the right tool {#not-the-tool}

Meditation isn’t a treatment, and I don’t make health promises. If anxiety is serious — panic, nights without sleep, fear that takes over your days — make an appointment with your doctor or a therapist, and there’s no shame in that door. I’ve written about [when a therapist is the better door](/disclaimers/#therapy).

Sitting with your thoughts can also bring up more than you expected. If that happens, open your eyes, stand up, have a glass of water, and talk to someone you trust. Go slowly. Two minutes is not a small amount; it’s the right amount to begin.
