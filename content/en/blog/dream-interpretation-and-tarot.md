Dreams and tarot cards are read the same way: through pictures, colours and the feeling they leave, not through a dictionary. A dream tells you more about where you are now than about what's coming. Tarot can help you look at a dream; it can't decode it for you.

## What is dream interpretation? {#what-is-it}

Dream interpretation is the practice of giving meaning to the images of a dream. There are three main ways of doing it.

The first is the dream dictionary: each symbol has a fixed meaning. Water is emotion, a house is the self, falling is losing control. The second is psychology. Freud, at the turn of the twentieth century, read dreams as wishes in disguise; Jung read them as the unconscious speaking in images that many people share. The third is personal association: what does *this* image mean to *you*?

I lean on the third, with the first as a starting point. A picture doesn't mean the same thing to two people, or even to one person on two different mornings — that's true of a card, and it's true of a dream. A dictionary that says “water means emotion” is a fair first guess; it isn't your dream.

## The gentle tsunami: my dreams of late 2020 {#gentle-tsunami}

In late 2020 I had vivid dreams. Every night, a tsunami came. It was gigantic, and it was gentle, and it was telling me to surrender.

I didn't. I hid in a house with other people.

Another night, I dreamt I was dying, and I was disappointed to wake up.

That's all of it, and I'll leave it as it came. I'm not going to hand you a meaning it didn't arrive with. But a dream like that makes a good example of how the tools I use on cards can be laid over a dream — so let's take it as if it were yours.

## Reading a dream with tarot lenses {#lenses}

Start with the element. A tsunami is Water, in its largest movement. In my notes on [the Water element](/library/four-elements/water-element/), water is both a ride and a risk: let it, and it moves you from one place to another; it can drown you as well. Rough water is a strong feeling; still water, one that has come to rest.

Then look for the contradiction. Gigantic *and* gentle. In a card, when two signals disagree, that's where I look first, because that's usually where the person is.

Then the action. You're told to surrender; you hide instead, with others. Who is in the house? Do you know them? What would surrendering have looked like?

As for dying in a dream: dream dictionaries usually read it as the end of a stage, not as a forecast. Three Rider-Waite-Smith cards sit close to that kind of night:

The Moon is night, uncertainty, the path you can't see the end of. The High Priestess is Water too — her robe falls like a waterfall — and she keeps a hidden side, like still water: a surface, and what's under it. And [Death](/library/tarot-cards/death/) isn't about dying either. It's the harvest: a field cleared and made ready to plant again. **A dream image, like a card, asks a question before it gives an answer.**

## How to use tarot on a dream {#how-to}

1. Write the dream down before you look at your phone: pictures, colours, who was there, what you felt. A dream page in your [reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) works well.
2. Circle the element: water, fire, air, earth.
3. Note how you felt on waking. It often says more than the plot.
4. Draw one card with the question: “What is this dream pointing at in my day?” Not “what will happen?”
5. Read the card through its number, element and colours, then set it next to the dream. Where they agree, look there.
6. If nothing lands, write that down too, and leave it.

Recurring images are worth following over weeks. If something keeps coming back, it's often something you keep pushing away — which is where [shadow work](/blog/shadow-work-beginners/) begins.

## Can dreams predict the future? {#predict}

Some people feel they've dreamt something that then happened. I haven't counted dreams the way I count readings, so I won't claim it either way. What I do know is where I put my attention: I read the future sometimes, but I focus on the present, because a future you'll enjoy starts with standing firmly in today. More on that in [what tarot can and can't predict](/blog/can-tarot-predict-the-future/).

A dream of someone you've lost can be a visit, a memory, or your mind sorting a heavy week — and you're the only one who can feel which. If dreams keep you from sleeping, frighten you night after night, or come with heavy days, take them to your doctor or to a therapist, not to the cards. I've written about [where my role stops and theirs begins](/disclaimers/#therapy), and what the cards can still hold meanwhile: a gentler question, like “what would help me rest this week?”

## Try it for one week {#next-step}

Keep a pen by the bed. Each morning, write the dream, circle the element, draw one card, and write one sentence that links them. At the end of the week, read the seven pages in a row; the pattern is usually in the feelings, not the stories.
