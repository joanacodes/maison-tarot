My shortlist for 2027 is mostly one deck in several editions: the Rider-Waite-Smith, which Pamela Colman Smith illustrated in 1909. Then one Marseille deck and the Thoth, both for later. There are no star ratings and no prices here; what matters is whose drawings are on the cards, how big they are, and whether you can read the colours.

## How I chose these decks {#how-i-chose}

Four things decided the list.

1. *The drawings.* Every card should carry Smith’s scene, the numbered ones too, because a scene lets a beginner start reading straight away. [My case for it as a first deck](/blog/best-tarot-deck-for-beginners/) makes the argument at length.
2. *The colours.* I teach colour as a lens, so the colouring of an edition matters more to me than its box.
3. *The size.* Small cards suit small hands and bags; large cards suit tired eyes and table readings.
4. *Whether you can find it.* A deck you can’t buy where you live isn’t a recommendation.

I’ve left out star ratings on purpose. A deck that suits one pair of hands doesn’t suit another, and a score would pretend otherwise. Instead, each entry below says what the edition keeps from the 1909 deck and what it changes, so you can match it to your own eyes and hands.

## The Rider-Waite-Smith editions {#rws-editions}

All four keep the same 78 scenes. They differ in colouring and format, and that’s how to choose between them.

*The classic Rider-Waite, from U.S. Games Systems.* This is the edition whose images you’ve probably already seen in books and online. Its colours are bolder than the muted tones of the earliest printings. A safe first deck if you want the pictures most teaching material shows.

*The Smith-Waite Centennial, from U.S. Games Systems (2009).* This edition was made for the deck’s hundredth anniversary to reproduce the muted colours of the early printings. It also comes in a small tin, and inside the Pamela Colman Smith Commemorative Set. For the nearest thing to the 1909 original, start here.

*The Universal Waite.* This edition keeps Smith’s line drawings, recoloured by Mary Hanson-Roberts in softer tones, with more detail visible in faces and costumes. Gentle on the eyes, and available in a pocket size.

*The Radiant Rider-Waite.* Here Smith’s drawings are recoloured by Virginijus Poshkus, with vivid colours and lighter black outlines. Readers who find the older colouring dull often prefer it.

In the UK, some of these editions are sold under the Rider imprint, the publisher whose name stuck to the deck in the first place.

**Any edition with Smith’s drawings reads the same way; choose the one you can see clearly and enjoy shuffling.**

One thing to watch with recoloured editions: colour is one of the lenses I teach, and a recolouring can soften or brighten the very tones you read through. Once you have your deck, lay [the colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) next to a few cards and check what your edition does. And if you’re curious about the woman behind every one of these decks, [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/) has her own page.

## One Marseille deck, for later {#marseille}

If you want a Tarot de Marseille, the classic French choice is Grimaud’s *Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, the version Paul Marteau prepared around 1930 from an older Conver pattern. Another well-known option is the Camoin-Jodorowsky restoration, rebuilt by comparing several old Marseille decks.

Neither is a first deck for my way of teaching. Their numbered cards show arrangements of cups, swords, coins and batons rather than people, so there’s no scene to lean on, only number and element. Once the lenses have sunk in, that’s exactly what makes a Marseille worth having. I set the two traditions side by side in [Marseille or Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/).

## The Thoth, for much later {#thoth}

The Thoth tarot was designed by Aleister Crowley and painted by Frieda Harris; today it’s published by AGM-Urania and distributed in the United States by U.S. Games Systems, in several sizes.

It’s a deep, beautiful deck, dense with astrology and symbolism, and in my own notes it sits on the shelf marked “to explore later”. The numbered cards have no scenes, several majors are renamed, and each numbered minor carries a one-word title that decides the mood before your eyes do. Wonderful for someone who loves symbols for their own sake; hard as a first deck. [What changes when you switch](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/) is worth reading before you buy.

## What I left off, and why {#left-off}

Oracle decks, because they aren’t tarot: each author sets the card count and the meanings. Lovely to own; they won’t teach you tarot’s structure.

The many modern redraws of the Rider-Waite-Smith. Some are beautiful, and once you know the original they can be a joy. I’d rather you choose one with a Smith edition next to it, so you can see what the artist kept and what they changed.

Prices, because they change faster than this page will. And anything I couldn’t be sure exists in the shops.

The honest limit: this is a reader’s shortlist, not a laboratory test.
