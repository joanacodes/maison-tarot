A tarot deck has 78 cards, and they fall into two groups. The 22 major arcana are the named cards — the Fool, the Lovers, Death, the World — and they mark the big chapters a life falls into. The 56 minor arcana are the four suits, and they fill in the day-to-day of each chapter.

That’s the whole difference. What makes it useful is counting them when they land on the table.

## What the major arcana are {#major-arcana}

The major arcana are 22 cards numbered from 0 (the Fool) to 21 (the World). Each one has a name and a full scene: a figure, a setting, a story you can tell without a book. *Arcanum* is Latin for a secret; *arcana* is the plural, and the word stuck.

They describe themes that take months or years, not afternoons. A beginning with no plan (the Fool). A long withdrawal to think (the Hermit). An ending that clears the ground for what comes next (Death). A cycle completed (the World).

I read them through the lenses I use on every card — colour, number, element — and they also carry an astrological attribution, which you can take or leave. Their numbers above 10 reduce: the Devil, 15, becomes 1 + 5 = 6. If you want all 22 in one place, [the major arcana page](/blog/major-arcana/) gives each one a paragraph.

## What the minor arcana are {#minor-arcana}

The minor arcana are the other 56 cards. They’re organised like a pack of playing cards. Each of the four suits — Wands, Cups, Swords, Pentacles — has fourteen cards, counting from Ace to Ten before its four court figures, Page up to King. Four times fourteen: 56.

They describe what happens on a Tuesday. Three friends raising their cups in a garden (Three of Cups). Hours at the workbench (Eight of Pentacles). A night you can’t sleep (Nine of Swords). Pamela Colman Smith drew a scene on every one of them in 1909, which is why the Rider-Waite-Smith is readable from the first day; in the Tarot de Marseille, the numbered minors show only arrangements of cups, coins, batons or swords.

You don’t need to learn them one by one. Ten numbers and four suits cover the 40 numbered cards; four ranks cover the other 16. I go deeper into [the four suits](/blog/tarot-suits-explained/) and [the court cards](/blog/court-cards-explained/) separately, and [the minor arcana page](/blog/minor-arcana/) lays out the whole grid.

## The difference, side by side {#side-by-side}

| | Major arcana | Minor arcana |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How many | 22 | 56 |
| Numbering | 0 to 21 | Ace to Ten in each suit, plus four court cards |
| Names | Each card has its own (the Hermit, the Star) | Number and suit (Five of Cups, Queen of Swords) |
| Time scale | Months, years, a chapter | Days, weeks, a situation |
| The question they answer | What is this period about? | What’s happening, and what can you do with it? |

**A major isn’t more important than a minor; it’s slower.** The Five of Cups is a minor, and for me no card in the deck is harder to draw: loss, gloom, and having to face it on your own. It still describes a moment, not a life.

## What it changes in a spread {#in-a-spread}

Count the majors before you read anything. 22 cards out of 78 is a little over a quarter, so in a three-card spread you’d expect fewer than one major on average. About a third of three-card spreads have none at all; three majors out of three happens roughly once in fifty draws.

So the number of majors tells you how large the matter is:

- No major: the matter is everyday and in the person’s hands. Nothing larger is pressing on it. I look more closely at [a spread with no majors](/blog/no-major-arcana-in-spread/) in another piece.
- One major: it sets the theme, and the minors say where it plays out.
- Two or three majors in a small spread: the person is in the middle of something bigger than the question. Read the majors first, slowly.

Take three cards drawn by a person whose question you haven’t heard: the Hermit, the Eight of Pentacles and the Two of Cups. One major: the Hermit, a 9 — the end of a cycle, introspection. Two minors: Earth and 8, mastery and effort; Water and 2, a bond between two people being agreed.

Put together, that becomes: “You’re in a longer period of stepping back. Inside it, day to day, you’re working hard, and something between you and one other person is being settled.” Then go quiet and let them judge whether any of it fits.

## Which group to learn first {#which-first}

Neither — learn the lenses, and they read both. Colour and number work on a major exactly as they work on a Three of Cups; the major has a bigger scene to look at, that’s all.

If you’d like an order anyway, start with the numbers of the minors, because the 1–10 cycle also runs under the majors: the Wheel of Fortune is a 10, the World reduces to 2 + 1 = 3. [Numerology](/library/numerology/) is the lens that ties the two groups together.

## Try it this week {#try-it}

Draw ten three-card spreads over a few days and write down one thing each time: how many majors. Then look back at the days with two or three, and the days with none, and see what you were living through.
