In most Tarot de Marseille decks, card XIII has a number and a picture — a skeleton with a scythe — but no name. It's the only one of the 22 major arcana like that, and French readers call it *l'Arcane sans nom*, the nameless arcanum. Nobody knows for certain why: no card maker left an explanation. The Rider-Waite-Smith did the opposite in 1909, printing the name, Death, and redrawing the card from scratch. Both choices change what you feel before you've read a thing.

## What card XIII looks like in the Tarot de Marseille {#marseille-xiii}

A skeleton swings a scythe across dark ground. Around its feet lie heads — one of them crowned — hands and feet, among small plants pushing up from the earth. At the top, the number XIII. At the bottom, the banner where every other trump carries its title stays empty.

The rest of the Marseille majors are all named: le Bateleur, la Papesse, l'Empereur, la Maison Dieu, le Soleil and so on. The only other card that lacks something is its mirror, le Mat, the Fool, who has a name but no number. Readers have built a great deal on that symmetry: one card outside the count, one card outside language.

The deck itself is worth knowing before you go further, and [the history of the Tarot de Marseille](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/) tells where the pattern comes from.

## Why does card XIII have no name? {#why-no-name}

Here's what history can say, and where it stops.

The oldest surviving tarot cards, painted by hand for the dukes of Milan in the fifteenth century, have neither titles nor numbers on their trumps. As [the Metropolitan Museum explains](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/697863), because the trumps weren't numbered, their order varied from one region to another. Players knew the cards by sight.

Writing a title on every trump became a French habit, and the Marseille pattern, printed in France from the seventeenth century, carries one on each card — except XIII. Yet even that isn't a fixed rule. The oldest known deck of the Marseille type, made by Jean Noblet in Paris around 1650, does write *La Mort* on it ([a description of Noblet's deck](https://tarot-history.com/jean-noblet/)). Most of the Marseille decks that followed leave the banner empty, and that is the version that was copied and recopied.

Why it settled in, nobody wrote down. There are three explanations people usually give:

1. *A reluctance to write the word.* Naming death on a card in a game felt like tempting fate. It's the most common explanation, and plausible, but I haven't found a document that proves it.
2. *The picture already said it.* A skeleton with a scythe was one of the most familiar images in Europe. A title added nothing.
3. *Copying.* Card makers cut their woodblocks after each other's decks. Once a widely copied model had an empty banner, the empty banner travelled.

We don't know which is true, or whether it's a mix. I'd rather tell you that plainly than choose the prettiest story. And the name *l'Arcane sans nom* comes from readers, not from card makers: it's what people started calling a card that had no title of its own.

## Why the Rider-Waite-Smith calls it Death {#rws-death}

Arthur Edward Waite didn't share the Marseille reluctance. He printed the name at the bottom of the card, DEATH, and he asked Pamela Colman Smith for a different picture. In his own book, [*The Pictorial Key to the Tarot*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Pictorial_Key_to_the_Tarot), he explains why: “The veil or mask of life is perpetuated in change, transformation and passage from lower to higher, and this is more fitly represented in the rectified Tarot by one of the apocalyptic visions than by the crude notion of the reaping skeleton.”

In plain words: the skeleton with the scythe was too blunt for what he meant, which was change and passage. So Smith drew a skeleton in black armour on a white horse, carrying a black banner with a white rose. A king lies fallen, a bishop stands with joined hands, a young woman turns away, a child looks straight up. Behind them a river flows, and on the horizon, between two towers, the sun.

To be honest about it, the old fear didn't vanish with the scythe. Waite's own list of meanings for the card, in that same book, still opens with “end, mortality, destruction, corruption.” Most readers today, me included, read it as change and ending rather than mortality — but the word on the card still arrives first.

## What the missing name changes for a reader {#what-it-changes}

**Without a name, you see the picture first; with a name, the word gets there before the picture does.** That's the whole difference, and it's a big one.

Look at the Marseille card without its title and what you see is someone mowing, with plants growing between the bones. That's very close to my own note on this card: Death is linked to the harvest and to starting again, like someone who has lost their goal and wants to make life fertile again. You cut what's ripe so the field can be sown.

Look at the Rider-Waite-Smith card and you read DEATH before you notice the rising sun or the child looking up. People react to the word, then sometimes never get to the drawing.

The lenses help either way. Thirteen reduces to 1 + 3 = 4, and [the number 4](/library/numerology/number-4/) is foundations, structure — with a minus side: carrying on when you should stop. Death answers that minus. It's a Water card, slow rather than sudden, and the Golden Dawn tradition gives it Scorpio. All of this is on [the Death card page](/library/tarot-cards/death/).

Try this with your own deck. Cover the name at the bottom of your Death card with a strip of paper and describe the drawing out loud for one minute: colours, figures, what's moving, what's growing. Then take the paper off. Notice what changes in your voice. That's the Marseille experience, in a Rider-Waite-Smith.

## If the card frightened you {#if-it-frightened-you}

If you drew this card and came here worried, for yourself or for someone you love, I understand; the word does that. In either deck, a card doesn't announce a death or an illness. I don't read on those questions, because a wrong word can frighten someone for nothing, and a health worry belongs with a doctor and with the people close to you. I explain this more fully in [why I step back from health, pregnancy and death questions](/blog/health-pregnancy-death-tarot/).

What the card can hold is a change you're living through: a chapter closing, a habit ending, a version of you that has done its time.
