A significator is a card chosen before a reading to stand for the person being read — or, sometimes, for the question. It’s taken out of the deck before the shuffle and the spread is laid around it or over it. I read without one, for reasons that have more to do with method than with taste.

## What a significator card is {#what-it-is}

Before shuffling, the reader takes one card out of the deck to represent the person. It isn’t read like the other cards; it marks whose life is on the table.

The best-known use is in the Celtic Cross. In the guide Arthur Edward Waite wrote to go with the Rider-Waite-Smith deck, the significator is the first card chosen, and the first card of the spread is laid on top of it. Say a reader is about to lay a Celtic Cross for a woman in her twenties with very fair hair: under the old rules, she takes out the Page of Wands, sets it face up, and lays the first card across it. I go through the full layout in [the Celtic Cross, position by position](/blog/celtic-cross-spread/).

Some readers use one in any spread: a significator in the middle of a three-card row, for instance, as a reminder of who the reading is for.

## How readers choose a significator {#how-to-choose}

There are four common ways.

1. *By description.* Waite’s guide matches the court cards to the person’s age and gender for the rank, and to their colouring — hair, eyes, complexion — for the suit.
2. *By star sign.* Fire signs get a Wands court card, Water signs a Cups one, and so on. Some readers use the major arcana card linked to the sign instead.
3. *By choice.* The person looks through the court cards face up and picks the one they feel most like.
4. *By subject.* The card stands for the question rather than the person: the Ten of Pentacles for a question about a family home, say.

Here’s what the first method gives in a single suit.

{{< cards "waki wakn waqu wapa" >}}

The King, the Knight, the Queen and the Page of Wands. Under Waite’s rule, Wands are for very fair people with blue eyes, and the rank follows age and gender: a King for a younger man, a Knight for a man of forty or more, a Queen for a woman over forty, a Page for a younger woman. Yes, the Knight is the older man; that’s how Waite wrote it.

## Why I read without one {#why-without}

Three reasons.

The first is the silence rule. The person I read for tells me nothing beforehand: no question, no context. Choosing a significator means knowing their age, their looks, their star sign or their question — exactly what I’ve asked them to keep to themselves. Online, I often have a username and a voice; I couldn’t pick one if I wanted to. I explain the rule in [why I ask you to tell me nothing](/blog/the-silence-rule/).

The second is the court cards themselves. I read a rank as a temperament, not a hair colour: [a Queen](/library/numerology/queen/) is a way of holding power, whoever holds it. I go further into that in [court cards explained](/blog/court-cards-explained/). Fixing someone to a card by their looks before the reading starts tells the cards who they are, instead of letting the cards show it.

The third is arithmetic. A significator leaves seventy-seven cards in the deck. If the Queen of Cups is sitting in the centre as the person, she can’t come up in the spread — and she might have been the card that needed to.

**A significator asks me to decide who you are before the cards have said anything.** I’d rather let them speak first.

## What to try if you like the idea {#instead}

Plenty of readers use a significator and read well with it. What it does well is give the reading a visible centre: the person sees a card that is “them”, and a nervous reader has somewhere to rest their eyes. If you like having a card that stands for the person, there are two ways to keep it without breaking the silence.

Let the shuffle choose it. Draw the first card face up and read it as “how you arrive today”. Say it’s the Knight of Pentacles: a rider on a heavy black horse that isn’t moving, holding up a pentacle and looking at it.

{{< cards "pekn" >}}

Earth, a Knight — Air of Earth in the court system: someone moving at their own steady pace, carefully, not to be hurried. The person now has a card that represents them, and you didn’t choose it. More on this card on its [Library page](/library/tarot-cards/knight-of-pentacles/).

Or keep your usual significator, but count. Note in your notebook which readings used one and which didn’t, and compare after a month. Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.

## Where to go from here {#next-step}

Without a significator, the remaining question is how many cards to lay out — one, three or ten. I give a rule of thumb, question by question, in [how many tarot cards to pull](/blog/how-many-tarot-cards-to-pull/). Pick a number, shuffle all seventy-eight, and let the first card be whatever it is.
