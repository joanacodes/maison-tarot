Yes, reading tarot is legal in France and in the UK, and in neither country is there a law written for it today. What both punish is the abuse around it: fraud, exploiting someone vulnerable, misleading or aggressive selling. I read cards; I'm not a lawyer. This is a map of the ground, not legal advice.

## France: legal since 1994 {#france}

France's old penal code made a trade of fortune-telling a minor offence, under its article R. 34-7. That article disappeared when the new penal code came into force on 1 March 1994. Since then, fortune-telling has been a free commercial activity with no rules of its own: no diploma, no professional body, no licence.

That isn't my interpretation. A French MP raised it in a written question in 2023, and the government confirmed in [its answer](https://questions.assemblee-nationale.fr/q16/16-6608QE.htm) that fortune-telling is a legal activity. The same answer quotes the agency that monitors cult-like abuses, which says it passes no judgement on beliefs as such.

In practice, a French reader registers like any other self-employed person and follows everyone's rules: tax, price information, consumer law. What French law does punish:

- Fraud (*escroquerie*, article 313-1 of the penal code): getting money out of someone through staged deception, not a mere lie. Up to five years in prison and a €375,000 fine. The curse "discovered" at the end of a reading, to sell the ritual that lifts it, is the textbook case; I've walked through [that script act by act](/blog/lift-the-curse-scam/).
- Exploiting vulnerability (*abus de faiblesse*, article 223-15-2): taking advantage of someone made fragile by age, illness or disability to make them act against themselves. Since a law of 10 May 2024 on cult-like abuses, psychological subjection has its own article.
- Consumer law: misleading or aggressive commercial practices, enforced by the consumer authority, the DGCCRF. Complaints go through its SignalConso service.

An honest limit: these laws act after the fact, and someone has to prove something. A wrong reading isn't a crime; a reading sold through staged deception can be. Between the two lies a lot of grey, and sorting it out is a judge's work, not mine.

For the per-minute phone platforms, there's a recent French case worth knowing: [what a French TV investigation found about psychic phone lines](/blog/psychic-phone-lines-france/).

## The UK: from the Witchcraft Act to consumer law {#uk}

The British road is longer and more colourful, and it explains a notice you've probably seen on every psychic website in English. The Witchcraft Act 1735 was replaced by the Fraudulent Mediums Act 1951, which made it an offence to claim to be a medium or clairvoyant with intent to deceive, for money — with an exemption for anything done solely for entertainment.

That Act was [repealed on 26 May 2008](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2008/1277/schedule/4/part/1/2008-05-26) by the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, which treat psychics like any other trader: no misleading claims, no aggressive pressure. Since 6 April 2025, those rules on unfair commercial practices sit in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, which also lets the Competition and Markets Authority fine businesses directly. General fraud law, including fraud by false representation under the Fraud Act 2006, applies too.

**In both countries the law doesn't ask whether you believe; it asks how you were made to pay.**

## What "for entertainment purposes only" actually does {#entertainment-only}

The line is usually traced back to the 1951 Act's entertainment exemption, and it outlived the Act. As far as I can find, the consumer regulations don't require it of every reader.

Where a rule does exist is on British television. [Ofcom's broadcasting code](https://ofcom.org.uk/tv-radio-and-on-demand/broadcast-standards/section-two-harm-offence) says that when divination is shown as entertainment, this must be made clear to viewers and listeners, and that such programmes must not give individuals life-changing advice — on health, finance, employment or relationships. That last rule is close to my own list of subjects I leave out.

The notice doesn't make a misleading sale legal. A reader who writes "for entertainment only" and then sells you a curse removal is still selling you a curse removal. I've explained elsewhere why [I like the notice anyway](/blog/entertainment-purposes-only/): it's honest about what a reading is.

## What this means for you, and for me {#for-you-and-me}

For you: legal doesn't mean vetted. Nobody checks a reader's competence before they open a line. The law protects you from deception, not from a mediocre reading, and it acts after the fact — so keep messages, receipts and statements.

If you think you've been taken in, there's no shame in it: these methods are built to work on intelligent people at the moment they're struggling. Stop paying, keep everything, report it (Trading Standards via Citizens Advice in the UK; SignalConso in France), and see a lawyer or a consumer association if you want your money back.

For me: I don't sell readings. I sell lessons, on the terms set out in [my terms of sale](/terms/); readings I give for free, in public. And ongoing court cases are among the subjects [I leave out of readings](/disclaimers/#refusals). A legal question deserves a lawyer, not a card. What a reading can hold meanwhile is how you're living through the wait.

## Before you consult anyone {#before-you-consult}

Before you call a reader or pay for a session, take ten minutes with [the nine red flags of a fake tarot reader](/blog/how-to-spot-a-fake-tarot-reader/). They won't tell you whether a reading is right. They'll tell you whether someone is trying to sell you fear, and that's most of the protection you need.
