Christian texts reject divination, and most churches say so plainly. Tarot, meanwhile, was born in Christian Europe as a game, with a pope and an angel among its trumps. And plenty of Christians are drawn to the cards anyway. All three are true at once; I'll set them side by side without deciding for anyone.

## What the Bible says about divination {#the-bible}

The passage quoted most is Deuteronomy 18:10–12, which lists among detestable practices consulting diviners, omens, sorcery and the dead. Leviticus says the same (19:26 and 19:31): don't practise divination, don't turn to those who call up spirits. In Acts 16:16–18, Paul drives a spirit of divination out of a young slave girl whose owners were making money from her.

The best-known story is Saul's. In 1 Samuel 28, the king, cut off from any answer, goes in disguise to a medium at Endor and asks her to call up the prophet Samuel. The scene ends badly for him, and 1 Chronicles 10:13 counts that consultation among the reasons for his death. It's the passage people reach for when they want to show that the question isn't new.

The same texts also know the casting of lots. The high priest carries the Urim and Thummim (Exodus 28:30). Proverbs says the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord (16:33). And the apostles choose Matthias by lot (Acts 1:26). The Magi of Matthew 2, who followed a star, are quoted by both sides for opposite conclusions.

Those who oppose tarot answer that those lots were addressed to God, whereas divination looks for answers elsewhere. Those who use the cards for reflection answer that the prohibition targets claims to know the future through other powers, not looking at pictures. **The argument isn't really about the cards; it's about what you ask of them.**

## What the churches say {#the-churches}

The Catholic Church is the most explicit. The Catechism, at paragraph 2116, says all forms of divination are to be rejected: recourse to demons, conjuring up the dead, and practices falsely supposed to "unveil" the future. It names consulting horoscopes, astrology, palm reading, the interpretation of omens and lots, clairvoyance and recourse to mediums, and sees in them a desire for power over time, history and other people. The text is [on the Vatican's website](https://www.vatican.va/archive/ENG0015/__P7E.HTM).

Many Protestant churches, evangelical ones especially, teach the same from Deuteronomy. For them the matter is settled, and settled consistently.

At the other end, some Christians look at the cards as images for contemplation. The American writer Brittany Muller, a former Catholic who came back to Christianity, published *The Contemplative Tarot*, which traces tarot's history and offers a short reflection on the Christian imagery of each card, as [Religion News Service reported](https://religionnews.com/2022/09/13/brittany-muller-combines-christianity-and-the-cards-in-the-contemplative-tarot/). That isn't a church position; it's a personal practice, and it exists.

## A game born in Christian Europe {#history}

Tarot didn't arrive from outside. It appears in fifteenth-century Italian courts as a card game, with a series of trumps then called "triumphs". One of the earliest written mentions is in an Italian sermon against gambling from the second half of that century, which lists the triumphs alongside dice and ordinary cards. Reading the cards for divination came much later, in the eighteenth century; I've told that story in [from card game to divination](/library/tarot-history/from-game-to-divination/).

The pictures carry that origin. The Hierophant was the Pope in the Tarot de Marseille, and the High Priestess was the Papess. Temperance is an angel pouring water from one cup into another. Judgement shows an angel blowing a trumpet, a cross on its banner, and the dead rising. The Devil holds two figures in chains, loose enough for them to lift off.

In France and parts of Europe, tarot is still mostly a card game, played at kitchen tables and in clubs with twenty-one trumps and the Excuse. Around that table, the question of sin rarely comes up.

## What believers actually do {#what-believers-do}

Between the texts and life there's a gap, and there's nothing shameful in it. In 2018 the Pew Research Center found that [six in ten American Christians](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2018/10/01/new-age-beliefs-common-among-both-religious-and-nonreligious-americans/) hold at least one so-called New Age belief — in psychics, astrology, reincarnation, or spiritual energy in physical objects. The share runs from 47% of evangelical Protestants to roughly seven in ten Catholics and members of historically Black Protestant churches.

An honest limit: that survey measures beliefs, not tarot practice, and it covers the United States only. I haven't found a British or French equivalent as detailed.

What it does show is that people rarely live their faith like a rulebook. Some Christians read cards with a quiet conscience, some abstain out of faithfulness, some hesitate. That last group is usually where the question reaches me, and it rarely sounds like "what does doctrine say?" It sounds like "am I betraying something?"

## My view, without preaching {#my-view}

I'm not a theologian, and I won't tell you what your church allows; I've said why in [is tarot a sin?](/blog/is-tarot-a-sin/), where I also explain where I'm speaking from. My father prays at a Buddhist temple, I read cards, and neither of us has ever asked the other to choose.

What I can tell you is what I do and don't do. I don't call on spirits, I don't promise anything, and I never present a reading as a verdict. I read the situation of the person in front of me, and they decide: [the cards aren't an authority](/disclaimers/#readings). If your church's teaching is aimed at claims to know the future, it's for you and those who guide you to say whether my practice falls under it.

If you're the one holding the deck, the same respect applies the other way. I always ask before reading anyone, and a hesitation counts as a no. A Christian friend who says "I'd rather not" doesn't need a speech about symbolism or psychology; they need you to put the cards away and pour the tea.

**A faith that says no deserves exactly the same respect as a curiosity that says yes.** If your church asks you to abstain and you hold to that, it's reason enough, and you have nothing to prove to anyone. If the pictures interest you and you don't want to draw, they can be looked at the way you'd look at a stained-glass window; [the Hierophant](/library/tarot-cards/the-hierophant/) is a good place to start.
