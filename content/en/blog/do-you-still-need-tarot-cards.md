Strictly speaking, maybe not. The more I read, the more often something came before I’d even shuffled. But my cards stay on the table, for two reasons that have nothing to do with power: the person in front of me wants to see them, and they help me get my own thoughts out of the way. This is my experience, not a rule.

## What changed the more I read {#what-changed}

In my notes from 2021 there’s a line I still recognise: I don’t need the cards anymore.

What I meant was this. At first, I went through the card: the colour, the number, the scene, then whatever the image set off. After many readings, something began to arrive earlier — a word, a picture, a direction — while the deck was still in my hands, before I’d shuffled.

I can’t tell you what that is, and I don’t claim it proves anything. It may be practice, the way a cook stops weighing the flour after enough years. It may be something I can’t explain. What I can tell you is that a message arriving early is still a message that can miss, like any other. It doesn’t come with a guarantee because it came first.

## Reason one: the person wants to see the cards {#the-person}

People want to see what I’m looking at. That’s the first reason, and it’s a good one.

A card on the table gives the person something to hold. They can look at it, remember it, write it down, check what I say against what’s in front of them. Say the Eight of Swords comes up. I could say “you feel trapped, but you have every tool to get out”. Or I can turn the card towards the person and let them see her: blindfolded and bound, swords all around, and her feet free. In my notes, this card is someone prisoner of themselves while they have every tool, and the blindfold can come off. The person can see that for themselves, and what you find on your own stays with you longer than what you’re told.

There’s a plainer reason too. When something hard comes up in a reading, it’s easier for both of us to look at a card than at each other.

It also changes the shape of the conversation. With the cards between us, we’re looking at something together. Without them, it would be me pronouncing things from across the table, and that’s a position I don’t want. [Tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/), and neither am I.

## Reason two: the cards help me focus, or unfocus {#focus}

The second reason is about me. The cards help me focus or unfocus, so that my own thoughts and biases step aside.

Focus, because the image gives my attention somewhere to land. Instead of drifting towards what I think I know about the person, I look at what’s there: the blue of the water, the number at the top, the element of the suit. [The colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) is often my first step, because colour reaches you before thought does.

Unfocus, because while my eyes are busy with the card, the part of me that wants to be right has less room. Say I’m reading for a friend and I already have an opinion about her partner. Without cards, my opinion would talk first. With a card in front of me, I have to start from the picture, and the picture doesn’t know what I think.

**The cards don’t bring the message; they keep me out of its way.**

## Should you try reading without cards? {#without-cards}

If you’re learning, no. Keep the cards. They’re how you’ll learn to tell the difference between what comes up and what you already thought, and that difference is most of the skill. [Reading intuitively](/blog/read-tarot-intuitively/) still starts with a card.

If you’ve read for a long time and things arrive early for you too, don’t make it into a rank. The reader who no longer needs cards is only a short step from the reader who feels above everyone who does, and that’s one of the egos I describe in [building a practice without becoming a guru](/blog/tarot-practice-without-guru/). Notice it and check it like everything else. Here’s how I’d test it:

1. Read for someone who tells you nothing first — the [silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/) matters even more here, since an early impression means nothing if you already know their story.
2. Before you shuffle, write down anything that’s already there, even a single word.
3. Draw and read as usual.
4. At the end, compare both with what the person confirms.
5. Do it ten times and count.

Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.
