Yes, you can let other people touch your tarot cards. A deck is cardboard and ink, and whatever makes a reading work, it isn’t whose fingers were on the cards last. Whether you let them is a preference — and preferences are allowed, as long as you know that’s what they are.

## Can someone else touch your tarot cards? {#can-they}

Yes. Cards aren’t magic, you are. The deck is a medium: it helps you let go and read what’s around a person. It doesn’t store anything the next reading depends on.

My own practice is the clearest example I have. Most of my readings for strangers happened online, on Discord servers and in group calls. Nobody touched my cards but me, and that didn’t stop the readings from landing. If touch were what tied a person to the cards, those readings would have had nothing to stand on.

The reverse holds too. Face to face, people shuffle my deck, or ask me to; both work. **A reading comes from the reader, not from the fingerprints on the deck.**

## Why some readers keep their deck to themselves {#why-not}

Plenty of good readers don’t let anyone handle their cards, and their reasons deserve a fair hearing.

Some believe a deck absorbs the energy of whoever handles it, so that another person’s touch leaves something behind that can blur the next reading. They keep the deck for themselves, or cleanse it after someone else has used it: with smoke, salt, moonlight, or a knock on the top of the deck.

Others have reasons that have nothing to do with belief:

- The deck was a gift and means something. My first deck came from friends, at Christmas, so I understand the attachment — though being given one isn’t a rule either, as I explain in [the gifted-deck myth](/blog/do-you-have-to-be-gifted-your-first-tarot-deck/).
- They want it to last. Tarot cards are tall and stiff, and an enthusiastic riffle bends them.
- Hygiene, at a café table or a busy event.
- Focus. For some readers, shuffling is the moment they settle, and handing it over unsettles them.

None of these is silly. They’re reasons about you and the object, and they’re good ones.

## Preference or superstition: how to tell {#preference-or-superstition}

Ask yourself what you think would happen if someone touched your deck.

If the answer is about you — “I’d feel less focused”, “I’d worry about the edges”, “it’s precious to me” — that’s a preference. Keep it without apologising.

If the answer is about the cards — “the readings would go wrong”, “their energy would stay in the deck” — that’s a belief about how tarot works, and it’s one you can test. For a fortnight, let the person shuffle in every other reading and note each result in your notebook. Then compare the two columns. If the readings where someone else shuffled miss more often, you’ve learned something about your practice. If they don’t, you’ve freed yourself from a rule.

I’ve been through something similar. In 2021 I sold sage and candles, and then I stopped: nothing in them improved a reading. I tell that story in [why I stopped cleansing decks](/blog/cleanse-tarot-deck/). And if your worry is that cards wear out in a less visible way, [do tarot cards get tired](/blog/do-tarot-cards-get-tired/) looks at that question.

## Letting the person shuffle — or cut {#shuffle-or-cut}

Face to face, inviting the person to shuffle has one real advantage: it gives them a part in the reading and a minute to settle before you begin.

If you’d rather keep the cards in your hands, there’s a middle way. Shuffle yourself, then ask them to cut the deck into three piles and put it back together in any order they like. They’ve taken part, and your deck has stayed with you.

If they do shuffle, a few practical words help everyone: dry hands, drinks moved aside, cards kept face down. Show them the overhand shuffle if they’re unsure; it’s the gentlest on tall cards. The three main ways are in [how to shuffle tarot cards](/blog/how-to-shuffle-tarot-cards/).

## How to say no without making it strange {#saying-no}

If you prefer that nobody touches your deck, say it as a preference, not a warning: “I like to shuffle my own cards — would you cut them for me?” Nobody needs to hear that their touch might spoil something.

Some readers keep a second deck for handling: a sturdy one for events, or for friends who want to look through the pictures. It settles the question without a conversation.

And the courtesy runs both ways. If another reader keeps their deck to themselves, ask before you touch it, and take the answer as it comes. Their habit is theirs. There’s more on that kind of courtesy in [tarot reading etiquette](/blog/tarot-reading-etiquette/).

## Try it with your next deck {#next-step}

If you’ve bought a new deck, this is a good moment to decide how you’ll use it: yours alone, or one others can handle. I suggest a week of short sessions with any new deck in [getting to know a new tarot deck](/blog/new-tarot-deck/). Somewhere in that week, try one reading where the other person shuffles, write it down, and see what you notice.
