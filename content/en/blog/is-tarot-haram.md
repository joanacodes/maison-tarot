In most schools of Islam, the answer is yes: consulting a fortune-teller is forbidden, and believing what they announce is more serious still. I'm not a religious scholar, and it isn't my place to give a ruling. Here is what I read, the range of positions I found, and how I answer when someone asks me.

A word on the word itself. *Haram* means forbidden: in Islamic law, an act that is prohibited, as opposed to *halal*, what is permitted. Asking whether tarot is haram is asking which side of that line consulting the cards falls on.

## What the Qur'an and the hadith say {#the-texts}

The principle comes down to one word: the *ghayb*, what is hidden. The Qur'an says that no one in the heavens or on earth knows the unseen except God ([sura 27, verse 65](https://quran.com/27:65)), and elsewhere that the keys of the unseen are with Him alone (6:59). In the sura of the Table Spread (5:3 and 5:90) it also forbids the divining arrows used before Islam to settle a matter.

Two hadith come up in almost every answer. The first, in Sahih Muslim: whoever goes to a fortune-teller (*ʿarrāf*) and asks about anything, their prayer is not accepted for forty nights ([the text and its explanation](https://hadeethenc.com/en/browse/hadith/5986)). The second, reported by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah among others: whoever goes to a soothsayer (*kāhin*) and believes what they say has disbelieved in what was revealed to the Prophet Muhammad.

**The heart of the prohibition isn't the object; it's the claim to tell what is hidden, which only God knows.** Cards, coffee grounds and palms are only supports; what's targeted is the promise of knowing.

## What scholars say today {#scholars}

The institutions I read point the same way. Egypt's Dar al-Ifta treats [astrology, palm reading and cup reading](https://www.dar-alifta.org/en/fatwa/details/7985/are-muslims-allowed-to-believe-in-palmistry-and-horoscopes) as deception: anyone who claims to tell what will happen is, in its words, a liar who cannot substantiate the claim. Answers from other schools, Sunni and Shia, reach the same conclusion.

The tension isn't new, though. The medieval Muslim world had famous astrologers, such as Abu Ma'shar in ninth-century Baghdad, later translated into Latin and read across Europe, while scholars condemned their art. There has always been a gap between what texts say and what societies do; Islam is no exception, and neither is Christianity.

The differences between views are about nuance, and they matter:

- Consulting isn't believing. The two hadith carry different consequences. Asking a fortune-teller is a serious fault; believing they know the hidden touches faith itself.
- The word "disbelief" (*kufr*) in the second hadith is debated. For many scholars it's a so-called minor *kufr*, unless the person truly believes the fortune-teller knows the unseen.
- Astronomy isn't astrology. Calculating the movements of the sky, for prayer times or lunar months, is a recognised science; what's rejected is astrology that claims to read the future.
- Opening the Qur'an at random to look for a sign, *tafa'ul*, divides commentators: some reject it, others approach it with caution.
- For a hard decision, the tradition has its own way: *istikhara*, a prayer of two units followed by a supplication, taught in a hadith reported by al-Bukhari. You ask God to choose what is good. It isn't a prediction; it's a handing over.

What I didn't find is a recognised ruling that makes an exception for tarot used purely as a prompt for reflection. The texts I read are concerned with the act of consulting and with belief, not with the intention you bring. I'd rather tell you that plainly than let you assume otherwise.

## How I answer when I'm asked {#how-i-answer}

The question rarely comes from a taste for theology. It comes from a curiosity that worries: a friend who reads cards, a video watched late at night, the wish to try and the fear of doing wrong. That worry deserves an honest answer, not a sales pitch.

So I won't try to convince a Muslim that tarot fits with their faith. It isn't my role, and it would be disrespectful to someone who takes their tradition seriously. I've explained in [is tarot a sin?](/blog/is-tarot-a-sin/) why I never tell anyone what their religion permits.

What I can tell you is what I do. I don't claim to know the hidden. I call on no spirits, promise nothing, and never present a reading as a verdict, because [a card has no say over your choices](/disclaimers/#readings). I mostly read the present of the person in front of me. But I won't conclude from that that it's halal: that would be convenient, and it isn't mine to say.

If you're Muslim, you've been reading cards and you now feel uneasy, guilt isn't a good adviser either. Talk it through with someone you trust in your tradition, and whatever you decide, decide it calmly: a choice made in fear rarely holds, in either direction.

If a decision is weighing on you, your tradition has *istikhara*, and an imam you trust will tell you more than I can. If it's the history of the pictures that intrigues you, you can read it without drawing a single card: [the history of tarot](/library/tarot-history/) begins with an Italian court game, not with divination.

## Respect, both ways {#respect}

For a time my father belonged to a branch of Buddhism; I read cards. I never had to look down on his way of taking the invisible seriously to keep mine, and that's what I try to hold to here.

I don't ask anyone's religion before a reading. I only ask whether they want to be read, and a hesitation counts as a no. If you read for others, do the same: don't guess anyone's faith from their name or their appearance, and ask every time. At [the free events](/events/), the people read are drawn at random, and I always ask before I begin; nobody has to explain a no.

**Giving up tarot out of faithfulness isn't superstition; it's consistency, and it deserves respect.** And a Muslim who reads cards doesn't need me to put them on trial either: that's between them, their faith and those who guide them in it.
