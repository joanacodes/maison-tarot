Each card has a side that gives and a side that takes, and a card you’ve never liked is usually one you’ve only met from one side. The answer isn’t to slip it out of the deck. It’s to meet it properly, through its lenses rather than its reputation.

## Why some cards put you off {#why}

There are four usual reasons, and it helps to know which one is yours.

The drawing. Ten swords in a man’s back is hard to look at, whatever it means. The reputation: Death and the Tower arrive with everything films and friends have said about them. A memory: the card fell on a bad day, and now it is that day’s card. And boredom, which people mention less: a card that seems to say nothing, like the Four of Pentacles clutching his coin.

Each reason calls for something different. A drawing you dislike needs a closer look. A reputation needs the lenses. A memory needs a little distance. Boredom needs a week of attention.

If you’re not sure which one is yours, notice the first thing that happens when the card turns up: a flinch at the picture, a sentence someone once said about it, a date, or a shrug. That first reaction usually names the reason. Most readers have one card like this, and it usually says more about how you met the card than about the card. Sometimes it also says something about you, which is why a disliked card is a good place to start [shadow work](/blog/shadow-work-beginners/).

## Read it by its lenses, not its reputation {#lenses}

Take the card, put aside everything you’ve heard about it, and say three things out loud: its number, its element, its dominant colour.

The Ten of Swords, read that way. [Ten](/library/numerology/number-10/) closes a cycle, with the next one already in view. Swords are [Air](/library/four-elements/air-element/): thinking, decisions, the mind that cuts cleanly and can also go round in circles. The colours: a black sky over the figure, and under it, along the horizon, a band of light.

Put together: a way of thinking has gone as far as it can go, and it’s over. The worst is already on the ground; the light is at the edge of the picture. **The lenses read the card in front of you, not the one you’ve been told about.**

## Find its plus, then its minus {#plus-and-minus}

No card is good or bad; every one has both sides. That’s true of the cards you love too.

I don’t soften the [Five of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/five-of-cups/), which my own notes call the hardest card of them all: what’s lost, the darkness it leaves, and the loneliness of carrying it. But look at the drawing again: three cups are spilled, and two are still standing behind the figure, with a bridge across the river. The minus is real. So is the plus.

Death is another one people keep at arm’s length. My notes tie it to reaping and beginning over, not to dying: picture someone who has lost their aim and wants something to grow in their life again.

When you’ve found both sides of a card you dislike, you can read it for someone without flinching, and without forcing it into good news either.

## Spend a week with it {#a-week}

1. Take the card out of the deck and put it somewhere you’ll see it every morning.
2. Each day, describe it out loud as if for the first time: what’s on it, what you see, what you receive.
3. Write one line in your notebook.
4. At the end of the week, put it back, and notice how it feels the next time it turns up.

The same image reads differently from one day to the next, and a card you look at fresh every morning slowly stops being the card you’d decided it was. It works like the daily card in [practising tarot with nobody to read for](/blog/practise-tarot-alone/), with one card held still for a whole week.

## When the dislike is a memory {#memory}

If a card fell on a hard day, it can feel as if it brought the day with it. It didn’t. It described a moment; it didn’t cause one. Giving it a week, as above, is a gentle way to loosen the link.

If it keeps turning up and that unsettles you, I’ve written about [why the same card keeps coming up](/blog/same-tarot-card-keeps-coming-up/). And if the fear is about what a card might announce — an illness, a death — that fear makes sense, and a card is the wrong tool for it: it doesn’t announce either. Those questions stay off my table, since one wrong word there does real harm. What the card can speak about is change, endings, and what comes next.

## You don’t have to love it {#keep-it}

You don’t have to like every card. You need to be able to read it when it turns up for someone. So keep it in the deck: without it, your deck can no longer say that thing, and sometimes that thing is exactly what a person needs to hear.
