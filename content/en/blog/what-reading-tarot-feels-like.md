Most of the time, reading a card feels ordinary: a word or an image turns up in your own voice, and you say it. Sometimes it’s anything but ordinary — I’ve had my body shake during a live. Both are honest answers, and neither will teach you as much as trying it once.

## Mostly, it sounds like your own thoughts {#own-thoughts}

We all receive impressions: images, sounds, words, smells, sensations, emotions. Some people are sharper in one channel than the others — seeing, hearing, feeling. When you read, these arrive the way thoughts do. There’s no bell and no special voice. A word turns up, and it sounds exactly like you.

Say you turn over the Six of Cups for a friend: two children in a courtyard, one handing the other a cup filled with flowers. In my notes the card is about childhood, taking time for yourself, flowering again. But the word that crosses your mind is “school”. Not a meaning from a book, just a word. That word is what reading feels like.

Because it sounds like you, it gets thrown away, and most beginners worry they’re inventing it. There are ways to [tell reading from making it up](/blog/am-i-making-it-up-tarot/), and none of them happen in the moment.

The cards help in a modest way. They give your eyes somewhere to rest, so your own thoughts and assumptions step aside long enough for something else to come through. In my notes from 2021, I wrote that the more I read, the more things arrived before I’d even shuffled, and that I used the cards mostly because people wanted to see them.

## Sometimes it’s in your body {#in-the-body}

During a live, my body started shaking while I was reading. I stayed calm. The emotions running through me weren’t mine.

That’s the other end of the scale, and it’s what empathy can look like when you read. In my teaching notes, empathy means feeling and understanding another person’s emotions while keeping your own. Very empathic readers can end up feeling the other person’s emotions, and sometimes expressing them, as if they were theirs.

That is where it needs care. If you start crying someone else’s tears, you’ve stopped reading for them; they’re now looking after you.

**Feel it, know whose it is, and keep it at arm’s length: you’re there to read the emotion, not to carry it.**

## How to stay calm when it isn’t yours {#staying-calm}

1. Notice it, and tell yourself where it’s coming from: this isn’t mine.
2. Slow down. Keep your voice level and your words plain. “There’s a lot of emotion around this” helps the person; a description of your shaking hands doesn’t.
3. Listen to the emotion without letting it take over. It’s information about the reading, not an instruction.
4. If it’s too much, stop. You can always finish another day, and nobody is owed a reading at your expense.
5. Afterwards, rest. Interpreting takes energy, and you can feel very tired after a reading; listen to your body. I sometimes stop after two hours of readings, out of caution. There’s more on [feeling drained after a reading](/blog/energy-after-tarot-reading/) and what helps.

None of this is proven. It’s my experience, and what I’ve seen in the people I’ve taught.

## The moment you surprise yourself {#surprise}

There’s a third feeling, and it’s the one I teach for. When I made friends and family try reading, there was always a moment when one of them said something true about the person in front of them, and was shocked by what they’d said.

You hear a sentence come out of your mouth that you didn’t know you knew. Then the other person’s face changes. That moment is the reason I teach, and I wrote about it in [what I learned making my friends try](/blog/how-to-teach-tarot/).

If none of this has happened to you yet, that’s where almost everyone starts. I wrote a whole page on [what to do if you don’t feel anything](/blog/dont-feel-anything-reading-tarot/), and the short version is: say what you see, and let the rest catch up.

## Try it rather than read about it {#try-it}

You can’t be told what it’s like. You can only try.

1. Find someone who will tell you nothing: no question, no context.
2. Draw one card.
3. Tell yourself the sentence from [my method for beginners](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/): *I’m about to talk nonsense.*
4. Say what you see and whatever turns up, word or image, even if it seems random.
5. Write down what you said, then ask whether it meant anything.

If you’d rather try it with someone beside you, every [workshop at Maison Tarot](/workshops/) starts with a card in front of you in the first ten minutes, and the explanations after.
