Most of the time, reading a card feels ordinary: a word or an image turns up in your own voice, and you say it. Sometimes it’s an emotion that plainly isn’t yours, and the work is to stay calm. Both are honest answers, and neither will teach you as much as trying it once.

## Mostly, it sounds like your own thoughts {#own-thoughts}

In my notes, impressions come through every channel we have: images, sounds, words, smells, sensations, emotions, and some people are sharper in one of them. When you read, they arrive the way thoughts do. There’s no bell and no special voice: what comes speaks in your own voice, at the speed of an ordinary thought.

Say you turn over the Six of Cups for a friend: two children in a courtyard, one handing the other a cup filled with flowers. In my notes the card is about childhood, taking time for yourself, flowering again. But the word that crosses your mind is “school”. Not a meaning from a book, just a word. That word is what reading feels like.

Because it sounds like you, it’s tempting to throw it away, or to suspect you invented it. There are ways to [tell reading from making it up](/blog/am-i-making-it-up-tarot/), and none of them happen in the moment: they happen afterwards, when you check.

The cards’ own part is a small one. They give your eyes somewhere to rest, so your own thoughts and assumptions step aside long enough for something else to come through. With practice, the word can come before you’ve even shuffled. I noted that in my first full year of reading, and I’ve put [that line from my 2021 notes](/blog/what-i-believed-about-tarot/) in context elsewhere.

## Sometimes it’s an emotion that isn’t yours {#not-yours}

At the other end of the scale, what arrives isn’t a word but a feeling. Sensations and emotions are channels too, and for some readers they’re the loudest ones.

Say you’re reading for someone and a heaviness settles on you that wasn’t there a minute ago. Nothing in your own day explains it. That is what empathy can look like when you read. My notes define it as taking in and making sense of someone else’s feelings without giving up yours. Readers with a lot of it can find another person’s mood settling in them, even coming out of their own mouths, as though it belonged to them.

That is where it needs care. If you start crying the other person’s tears, you’ve stopped reading for them; they’re now looking after you.

**Feel it, know whose it is, and keep it at arm’s length: you’re there to read the emotion, not to carry it.**

## How to stay calm when it isn’t yours {#staying-calm}

1. Notice it, and tell yourself where it’s coming from: this isn’t mine.
2. Slow down. Keep your voice level and your words plain. “There’s a lot of emotion around this” helps the person; a running commentary on what’s happening in your chest doesn’t.
3. Listen to the emotion without letting it take over. It’s information about the reading, not an instruction.
4. If it’s too much, stop. You can always finish another day, and nobody is owed a reading at your expense.
5. Afterwards, rest. Interpreting takes energy, and you can feel very tired after a reading; listen to your body. There’s more on [feeling drained after a reading](/blog/energy-after-tarot-reading/) and what helps.

None of this is proven. It’s my experience, and the hypothesis I make from it.

## The moment you surprise yourself {#surprise}

There’s a third feeling, and it’s the one I teach for. I made friends and family try reading, and the moment that counts is the one where someone says something true about the person in front of them, and is shocked by what they’ve just said.

From the inside, it feels like hearing a sentence come out of your mouth that you didn’t know you knew. That moment is the reason I teach, and I wrote about it in [what I learned making my friends try](/blog/how-to-teach-tarot/).

If none of this has happened to you yet, that’s where almost everyone starts. I wrote a whole page on [what to do if you don’t feel anything](/blog/dont-feel-anything-reading-tarot/), and the short version is: say what you see, and let the rest catch up.

## Try it rather than read about it {#try-it}

You can’t be told what it’s like. You can only try.

1. Find someone willing to sit down without saying why.
2. Draw one card.
3. Lower the stakes the way [my method for beginners](/blog/how-to-read-tarot-beginners/) does: expect to talk rubbish.
4. Say what you see and whatever turns up, word or image, even if it seems random.
5. Write down what you said, then ask whether it meant anything.

If you’d rather not try it alone, a [Maison Tarot workshop](/workshops/) puts a card before you almost at once and saves the explaining for later.
