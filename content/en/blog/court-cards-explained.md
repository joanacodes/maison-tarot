The court cards are the sixteen people of the minor arcana: a Page, a Knight, a Queen and a King in each of the four suits. Their rank says how far someone has got with the element — learning it, acting with it, holding it, deciding with it — and their suit says which element.

In a reading, a court card can be a person or a state of mind. The person in front of you is the one who tells you which.

## What the court cards are {#what-they-are}

The court cards are the four highest cards of each suit, the way playing cards have a Jack, a Queen and a King. Tarot keeps one more: the Knight. Some decks rename them — the Thoth deck, for instance, has Princesses and Princes — but the logic stays the same.

Each court card is a rank crossed with an element. Take the Knight of Cups: a Knight is the element in motion, and Cups are Water, feelings. So the Knight of Cups is a feeling being acted on — a proposal arriving, someone turning up with their heart held out in front of them, the way he holds his cup.

Court cards confuse people for a good reason. A numbered card shows an event: a party, a fight, a harvest. A court card shows a character standing, sitting or riding, and nothing happens. **Read a court card the way you’d read a stranger across a café: posture first, then what they’re holding.**

## The four ranks: four stages of mastery {#the-ranks}

The [Page](/library/numerology/page/) is learning. They already hold their suit’s tool and look at it like something they don’t know how to use yet. They’re also the messenger: news, a letter, an invitation. A Page has no age; you can be one at fifty.

The [Knight](/library/numerology/knight/) is acting. Look at the horse: it rears for Wands, charges for Swords, walks for Cups and stands still for Pentacles. Knights are made for leaving, not for arriving.

The [Queen](/library/numerology/queen/) is holding. She has absorbed her element so well that it’s part of her. She’s seated, she knows, and she proves nothing.

The [King](/library/numerology/king/) is deciding. He doesn’t look at his tool any more; he looks at the world he manages with it. The question to ask him is always: for whom?

None of this is about sex. A Queen can be a man, a King can be a woman. Pages and Queens lean towards [feminine energy](/library/feminine-masculine-energies/), Knights and Kings towards masculine — dynamics that every one of us carries in different proportions.

## One rank, four suits: the Queens {#the-queens}

The fastest way to see how rank and suit combine is to hold one rank still and change the suit. Here are the four Queens.

The Queen of Wands has sunflowers and a black cat at her feet: warmth that’s sure of itself. The Queen of Cups stares into a closed, ornate cup: feelings held deep, not poured out. The Queen of Swords raises one hand, sword upright in the other: she lets you speak, then she cuts. The Queen of Pentacles holds her coin in her lap with a rabbit in the garden: she looks after what’s real. Same mastery from the inside; four different things being mastered.

## A person or a state of mind? How to tell {#person-or-state}

Both are possible, and I don’t decide alone. Four clues help:

1. The position. A court card in a position about the person themselves is usually a state. In a position about the people around them, it may be someone.
2. How many there are. One court card among numbered cards is often a state of mind. Three or four in a small spread is a cast of people.
3. The story around it. If the numbered cards tell a story, the court card is often the one acting in it.
4. Saying both out loud. The person tells me nothing before the reading, so I don’t know who’s in their life — and that’s the point. Tell me nothing; I’ll name both readings, and you’ll know which one is yours.

Say someone draws the King of Swords next to the Two of Cups. A King decides; Swords are Air, thought. The Two of Cups is a bond between two people. You might say: “There’s a cool-headed decision inside a relationship. Either someone near you is making it — or it’s you, and lately you’ve been more judge than partner. Which is it?”

Some older books read court cards as physical types: hair colour, age. I don’t; a rank is a temperament. It’s also why I read without [a significator card](/blog/significator-card/), the court card traditionally picked to stand for the person.

## Practise with all sixteen {#practise}

Take the sixteen court cards out and lay them in a grid: ranks in rows, suits in columns. For each card, say one sentence made of the rank’s verb and the element’s subject — “learning to feel”, “acting on an idea”, “holding the money”, “deciding about the project”. Ten minutes, and they stop being strangers.

If the suits are still blurry, start with [the four suits in plain words](/blog/tarot-suits-explained/), then come back to the grid.
