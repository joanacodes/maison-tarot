My working definition of magic is this: the things we know exist but can’t explain yet. Not spells, not powers, not something I’m asking you to believe in — a waiting room for what we’ve seen happen and can’t account for. It keeps me curious, and it keeps me honest.

## What is magic? Three meanings of the word {#three-meanings}

The word does three different jobs, and most arguments about magic come from mixing them up.

Stage magic is illusion. Someone knows exactly how it works and hides it from you on purpose; the pleasure is in being fooled. A coin vanishes, and the explanation is up a sleeve.

Ritual magic is a set of practices meant to change something through symbols, words and intention: spells, ritual, a candle lit for a reason. It belongs to witchcraft and to many folk and religious traditions, and it’s a practice rather than an explanation. [Whether tarot counts as witchcraft](/blog/is-tarot-witchcraft/) is its own question.

Everyday magic is the “it was magic” kind: a first concert, a coincidence that made you laugh out loud, an evening that went beyond what you could describe.

My definition sits closest to the third, with one change. I want the thing to be real before I call it magic. **Magic, for me, is what we know exists but can’t explain yet.**

## Why the word “yet” matters {#yet}

Every part of that sentence does some work. “Know exists” means observed and repeated, not hoped for. “Can’t explain” means nobody can say how. And “yet” means magic is a waiting room, not a final verdict.

History is full of things that sat in that room. Lodestones pulled iron for thousands of years before anyone could say why. People watched lightning for as long as there were people, and it took experiments in the 1750s to show it was electricity. Surgeons have used general anaesthesia since the 1840s, and exactly how it switches consciousness off is still being researched.

By my definition, all three were magic, and two of them have mostly stopped being so. That’s the honest part: this definition expects magic to shrink. It doesn’t flatter it. It also doesn’t rule out that some things will stay in the waiting room for a very long time.

## Where tarot sits in that definition {#where-tarot-sits}

What I know happens: people say true things about strangers who’ve told them nothing. [By my own count](/blog/does-tarot-work-300-readings/), about nine in ten of my readings held. It isn’t a study, but it’s more than a feeling. When friends try it for the first time, they say something true and look shocked by what came out of their own mouth. That look is why I teach.

Part of it I can explain. Some of what feels like a hit is general enough to fit anyone, which is why I check my readings against [the Barnum effect](/blog/barnum-effect-tarot/). Memory keeps the hits and drops the misses. A face reacts before its owner decides to.

Part of it I can’t. After reading for a friend, I once felt her grandfather with me at home, and the message he gave me, a five and “under the house”, led her to a basement storage cell number five full of paint pots. I tell it properly in [what one very polite grandfather taught me](/blog/is-tarot-evil/).

What it was, I can’t say. I know it happened, and I can’t explain it. That’s exactly where I file it.

If you want the idea in two cards, take the Magician and the High Priestess. In my notes, the Magician has all four elements on his table and knows how to do everything — and his minus is that he can pretend, if you trust him blindly. Both meanings of “magician” in one card. The High Priestess knows a great deal and shows you only a façade, like the hidden face of the moon: what exists and isn’t visible yet.

## What this definition doesn’t claim {#doesnt-claim}

It doesn’t claim powers. I don’t have abilities other people lack; anyone can learn to read. What happens in a reading comes from the two people at the table, not the cardboard, and nobody can fully explain it yet.

It doesn’t claim that the unexplained is dangerous, evil or holy. “Unexplained” describes the state of our knowledge, not the thing itself.

And it doesn’t claim certainty. When [a pen-pal once asked whether I could read minds or was a magician](/blog/where-to-find-people-to-read-for/), the honest answer was neither, and also that I can’t fully explain how a reading works — which is exactly where my definition begins.

The limit, said calmly: calling something magic is an admission, not an explanation. The risk is stopping there.

## How to stay curious without believing everything {#stay-curious}

Keep a page at the back of your notebook for things you’ve seen happen and can’t explain. Date each one. A year later, reread it.

Some entries will have found an ordinary explanation: a coincidence, a memory that tidied itself up, something you’d been told and forgot. Some won’t. Those go on the shelf I label “sceptical, but if it happens, I was warned”.
