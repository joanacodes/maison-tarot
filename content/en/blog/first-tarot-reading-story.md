My first reading for a stranger missed. She told me it didn’t resonate, and on the day, that made it a miss. Three or four days later she texted me, shocked: all of it had happened. That reading is why I write down what I say, and why I learned to wait.

## What happened {#what-happened}

I’d started reading at the very end of 2020, on a deck my friends had given me for Christmas. My first reading for someone I didn’t know happened on a Discord group I’d found through Tumblr.

I read for a stranger there. I knew nothing about her life. What came out was a story about a relationship.

When I’d finished, she told me it didn’t resonate. She wasn’t looking for a relationship.

Three or four days later, she texted me. She was shocked. Everything had happened. And the reading had shown one more thing, a less flattering one: she hadn’t been kind to the other person.

That is the whole story, told the way it happened. I’d rather give you less of it than dress it up.

## On the day, it was a miss {#on-the-day}

When someone tells you a reading doesn’t fit, the honest thing is to believe them. She was the one living her life; I was the one describing it from a few cards. “I’m not looking for a relationship” is a clear answer, and it deserves to be taken as one.

There are ways to rescue a moment like that, and every reader feels the pull. You reframe it: maybe it’s someone you haven’t met yet. You draw an extra card to “clarify”. You ask questions until something fits. Each of those turns a clear no into a maybe, and a maybe can never be checked. I’ve written more about [what to do when a reading is wrong](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/), and it mostly comes down to saying so.

A miss is a miss. You say it, you note it, and you move on to the next person.

**If you argue with a “no” on the day, any “yes” that comes later is worth nothing.** The text three or four days later only means something if nobody pushed her towards it. A reader who spends the evening convincing someone that a reading fits has also, quietly, written the message they’ll receive next week.

## Then she texted {#then-she-texted}

Nothing about the reading changed in those few days. What changed was her life.

Either the reading described something that hadn’t happened yet, or something already starting that she couldn’t see from where she stood. I don’t know which, and I don’t need to. Both come down to the same thing: timing. A card shows you something; it rarely shows you when. Here, timing made a reading look wrong for three or four days.

Timing can also do the opposite, and make a message look right in a way that matters far less than it seems. The story that taught me that is in [why a reading can be wrong](/blog/why-a-reading-can-be-wrong/), and I’d read the two side by side.

The detail that matters most to me is the unkind one. Nobody invents an unflattering detail to please a stranger. If I’d been guessing at what she wanted to hear, that’s the first thing I would have left out. It isn’t an easy thing to hear from anyone, let alone a stranger. It was also true, and it was hers to know.

Say you draw for a friend and something comes up that you’d rather not say: that she’s been short with someone, or that she’s avoiding a person who deserves better. The temptation is to drop it and keep the pleasant half. That half is usually the part anyone could have guessed.

## What I took from it {#what-i-took}

Three habits, and I’d give them to any beginner.

1. **Write down what you said, before they answer.** In your own words, at the time. Memory improves a reading after the fact; paper doesn’t. My notebook has two columns: what I said, and what was confirmed. The format is in [how I keep a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/).
2. Write down the answer too, including the no. If it’s a miss, mark it as a miss. No third box for “we’ll see”.
3. Wait, and don’t chase. Don’t message the person to ask whether it happened; that’s fishing with a delay. If they come back on their own, add a new line with the date. Don’t rewrite the first one. On the day it was a miss, and later it wasn’t, and a notebook should be able to hold both.

I’m careful with this story, because it’s the kind that’s tempting to turn into a rule. It isn’t one. By my own count, about one reading in ten missed, and I don’t treat any of those as a hit that hasn’t arrived yet. If you start believing that every no will turn into a yes, you’ve stopped checking anything at all, and your notebook becomes a list of things you’re waiting for.

What the story gave me isn’t proof. It’s permission to be wrong in front of someone, and to let the record show it.

## Why I tell beginners this story {#why-i-tell-it}

Because fear of being wrong freezes more beginners than anything else, and my first reading for a stranger began as a miss. I’m still reading.

It’s also why I ask people to tell me nothing. She hadn’t told me anything before I read. If she’d opened with “I’m not looking for anyone,” I would probably have softened what came, steered towards work or family, and never said it. I’d have been less wrong on the day and of no use to her four days later. That is [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/), and this reading is the clearest example I have of why it matters.

If you want a story of your own, you need people who’ll tell you nothing and won’t mind a miss. I found my first ones through Tumblr and Discord; there are other ideas in [where to find people to read for](/blog/where-to-find-people-to-read-for/). Or come to one of the [free events](/events/), where the cards are drawn at random, nobody explains anything first, and you compare afterwards. Bring a notebook.
