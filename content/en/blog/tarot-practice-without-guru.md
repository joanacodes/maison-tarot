You avoid becoming a guru by building a practice that hands every decision back to the person in front of you. Most readers who slide into it never planned to. They were kind, they were good at it, and people started asking them what to do.

## How a reader becomes a guru without meaning to {#how-it-happens}

It starts with something good. A reading lands. The person comes back, then comes back before every decision, then brings friends. Somewhere along the way, the reader’s word starts weighing more than the person’s own judgement — and the reader starts to enjoy the weight.

Nobody wakes up wanting followers. It happens by small steps, and that’s why it’s hard to see from the inside.

Two cards hold the risk well. In my notes, the Magician knows how to use everything on his table, but trusted blindly he can pretend. The Emperor gives orders and doesn’t receive them, and like fire he can destroy what he built. A reader with a following can become either, without changing a single card in the deck.

## The three egos of the spiritual world {#three-egos}

I keep a short list of three egos you meet in spiritual circles, and they’re worth watching for in yourself too. They’re very human. Most of us brush against one at some point.

- *The saviour.* The one who thinks they’re saving the world. Say a reader sees a stranger’s sad post and messages them unprompted: “I’ve seen something about you, you need a reading.” The intention may be kind. The result is a frightened person who now feels they need that reader.
- *The chosen one.* The one who believes they belong to a small group with abilities other people don’t have. Picture a teacher who tells students they’re “not ready” for certain cards, or that only a few people can really read. That small group doesn’t exist; anyone can learn.
- *The “awakened”.* The one who has had an experience that changed them and now feels above ordinary people. Imagine a friend who answers your worry about rent with “you’re still stuck in the material world”, and stops listening.

People usually fall into these after something that mattered: a reading that helped, an experience they can’t explain, a lot of thanks in a short time. Wanting that to have made you special is natural. The way out isn’t shame. It’s noticing, and I’ve written more about the word itself in [‘spiritual awakening’](/blog/spiritual-awakening/).

**Each of these egos puts the reader at the centre; a practice works when the person being read stays there.** It’s also why I [keep ‘spiritual’ groups at arm’s length](/blog/spiritual-but-not-in-spiritual-circles/): not the people in them, the ladders.

## Hand the decision back {#you-decide}

A reading can put words on something, show an angle you’d missed, confirm what you already knew. It can’t decide for you, and I’ve written why in [tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/).

A guru can’t afford to miss. A reader has to. My notebook has its share of misses, and I say each one out loud when it happens. Saying “that one’s a miss” in front of someone is one of the best protections against becoming an oracle in their eyes.

In practice, that means no instructions — no “leave him”, no “take the job”. It means asking questions back: what did you feel when I said that? And it means noticing when someone comes back with the same question as last week, and kindly suggesting [a break from readings](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/) instead of another one.

## Know which door isn’t yours {#other-doors}

A guru has an answer for everything. A reader knows where their answers stop.

Some of what people bring isn’t a question for cards at all. A health worry, a court case, a money decision: I don’t read on those, and I say why. A wrong word there does damage, and there are people trained for exactly that. Grief that won’t move, or a fear that keeps someone awake night after night, is often a therapist’s door rather than mine. Tarot should never replace therapy, or become a circle of dependency, for the reader or for the person.

So I point to the right door — a doctor, a lawyer, a therapist — without making anyone feel sent away. My page on [when a therapist is the better door](/disclaimers/#therapy) says it more fully.

## Habits that keep a practice the right size {#habits}

1. Ask permission before reading anyone, every time.
2. Ask the person to tell you nothing first, so you can’t mistake their story for your reading.
3. Log every reading, your claims on one side and the person’s answers on the other. Count the misses and say them.
4. Teach the method rather than selling answers. It’s why I sell lessons and give readings away for free at events.
5. Stop when you’re tired.
6. Hand the compliments back. When someone tells you you’re special, [hand it back gently](/blog/you-have-a-gift/): it was their reading too.

## Where to start {#start}

Open your notebook at your last five readings. For each, ask one question: did the person leave with a decision of their own, or with mine?

If most of them left with their own, you’re building a practice. If you’re about to teach others, the next question is what you’ll pass on, and I’ve answered it in [do you have to be spiritual to teach tarot?](/blog/spiritual-to-teach-tarot/)
