A yes-or-no tarot reading asks one card for a verdict: yes, no, maybe. I don’t read that way, and it isn’t because the question is silly. “Will I get it?” is often the most honest question a person has. It’s because a card holds a whole scene, and a yes throws most of it away.

## What a yes-or-no tarot reading is {#what-it-is}

A yes-or-no reading is a single draw — sometimes three — to answer a closed question. Each card is given a value in advance. The most common system: upright means yes, reversed means no. Another uses a list: the Sun, the Star and the Ace of Cups say yes; the Tower and the Ten of Swords say no; the Hanged Man says wait.

Say someone asks “Will I get the flat?” and turns over the Sun, upright. Under that system the answer is yes, and the reading is over in four seconds.

I understand the appeal. Waiting hurts, and a closed question is what worry sounds like: will he, should I, is it. A yes-or-no promises to end the waiting. It fits on a phone screen, it’s quick, and for a moment it feels like relief.

## Why I answer differently {#why-differently}

Three reasons, all practical.

The first is that a card isn’t a switch. The Four of Wands has a number, an element, colours, people, a garland. Calling it “yes” is like reading the first word of a letter and throwing the rest away.

The second is that a verdict moves the decision onto cardboard. A card [carries no authority](/disclaimers/#readings): a reading describes a moment, and you’re the one who lives with the choice. That’s the deal at every reading I give, and a yes-or-no quietly turns it round.

The third is the reshuffle. Notice what you would do with a no. **If a no would make you shuffle again, the answer you were hoping for is already your answer.** You didn’t need the card for that, and you’ll trust it more coming from you.

The honest limit: I can’t tell you whether you’ll get the flat. Nobody can, from a card. What I can do is look at what the card says about you, now, in front of that question.

## What a card can say instead of yes or no {#what-a-card-can-say}

Say someone asks, silently, “Should I take the job in Bristol?” and draws one of these three.

The [Seven of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/seven-of-cups/): someone gazes up at a bank of cloud where seven cups hover, a castle in one, jewels in another, a snake in a third. A yes-or-no reader would call it “no”, or “unclear”. I’d say: you’re not choosing between yes and no, you’re choosing between several dreams, and some of them are made of cloud. Which one is Bristol?

The Four of Wands: a garlanded gateway of four staffs, with a celebration going on beyond it. Four is foundations, the fruit of your work; for me this card is roots, a home being built. It doesn’t say “go”. It asks whether this job builds something you’d want to live in.

The Two of Swords: a woman blindfolded, holding two crossed swords, the sea behind her. [Two is the number of the contract](/library/numerology/number-2/), and what it advises is checking the terms and setting gains against costs. The card says: you’re refusing to look. Take the blindfold off, then decide.

None of the three is a verdict. Each gives you a direction and a condition, which is more than a yes ever could.

## How to turn your question into one a card can answer {#better-questions}

Three moves: from *will* to *what*, from *them* to *you*, from *later* to *now*.

- “Will I get the job?” → “What should I bring into that interview?”
- “Should I text him?” → “What am I hoping that message will do for me?”
- “Is this the right flat?” → “What do I need from a home right now?”
- “Will it work out?” → “What can I do this week to help it work?”

The new question is longer and less comfortable. It’s also the one you can act on tomorrow morning. For choices with more on the line, I go through the whole approach in [how to use tarot for a decision without letting the cards decide](/blog/tarot-for-decision-making/).

Some closed questions are closed because they’re heavy: am I ill, will she pull through, will I win the case, should I invest. Those are worries that deserve better than a card, and I explain why, gently, in [the questions I’d leave aside and the kinder ones to ask](/blog/questions-to-avoid-asking-tarot/). The same goes for “will they come back”, which has [a kinder question of its own](/blog/will-they-come-back-tarot/).

## If you still want a yes or no {#coin}

Flip a coin. I mean it. While it’s in the air, notice which side you’re hoping for. That flicker is the information, and it costs nothing.

Tarot is worth taking lightly, and a coin is lighter than a deck. Keep the cards for the questions that deserve a scene. The difference is easiest to hear in person: bring a closed question to one of the [free events](/events/), keep it to yourself, and listen to what the card says around it.
