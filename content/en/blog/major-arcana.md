The 22 major arcana are the big scenes of the deck, from the Fool setting off (0) to the World dancing at the end (21). You don’t need to learn 22 meanings by heart: each card already carries a number, an element, a sign or planet and an energy, and with those four you can say something true about it before you’ve opened a book. Below, all 22 sit in one table, sorted into families you can learn two or three at a time, and every card links to its own page, where its drawing is read in detail.

## How the major arcana work {#how-they-work}

The majors are the 22 trumps of the tarot: the cards with a name, a number and a scene, but no suit. In a reading they carry the big themes — a beginning, a choice, an ending, a renewal — while the minors carry the everyday detail.

Their numbers tell a story, and it helps to know its shape. From 0 to 6, the Fool to the Lovers, the deck lays out the tools: a beginning, a skill, an inner knowing, a mother, a father, a teacher, a choice. From 7 to 13, the Chariot to Death, it tests what was built: will, courage, solitude, luck, fairness, waiting, endings. From 14 to 21, Temperance to the World, it goes from the deepest shadow to the widest light, and ends where everything comes together.

That arc is a way to hold 22 cards in your head as one story rather than 22 facts. A spread never lays them out in order, so don’t read it as a timetable.

**Every card has a plus and a minus, and the lenses show you both.** In my notes, Judgement is neither good nor bad, and Death isn’t necessarily bad either: even the darkest drawing has its plus.

## The 22 cards through their lenses {#lenses}

I read every major through the same lenses I use for the rest of the deck. The first two do most of the work.

1. The number. Cards 0 to 10 sit directly on the [cycle of numbers](/library/numerology/), from the start (1) to the corridor between two rooms (10). From 11 to 21, add the digits: 13 becomes 1 + 3 = 4, 18 becomes 9. The card then plays its number on a bigger stage.
2. The element. Each major has Fire, Water, Air or Earth — the same [four elements](/library/four-elements/) as the minor suits. Fire acts, Water feels, Air thinks and decides, Earth builds and keeps.
3. The astrology. At the end of the nineteenth century the Golden Dawn gave each major a sign, a planet or an element; Uranus, Neptune and Pluto were added later by modern astrologers. [Astrology](/library/astrology/) is an extra lens: keep it if it speaks to you.
4. The energy. [Feminine and masculine](/library/feminine-masculine-energies/feminine-masculine/) here aren’t about women and men. Feminine holds, waits, imagines, works behind the scenes; masculine acts, shows itself, builds, decides fast. We all carry both.

Then look at the drawing. Pamela Colman Smith gave every card a scene in 1909, and each card’s page reads it with you.

These correspondences are a system people built over time, not facts carved into the cards. They’re handles. Use the ones that help you see.

Say you draw Temperance and know nothing about it. Fourteen gives 1 + 4 = 5: the trial, making do with what’s left. Fire: energy, impulse. Sagittarius: “I see, I try”. Feminine: patience, holding. Out loud: “You’re being tested, and the way through is patience with your own fire — try, adjust, try again.” Then look at the drawing, an angel pouring from one cup into another, and see whether it agrees.

## Learn them in families, not one by one {#families}

Once you reduce the numbers, the 22 majors fall into ten families. Learn a family and you’ve learned two or three cards at once: they share a stage of the cycle, and each one plays it differently.

One caution before you start. In a Marseille deck, Strength is 11 and Justice is 8; the Golden Dawn swapped them and Waite followed. So with a Marseille deck, Strength joins the 2s and Justice the 8s. Reduce the number printed on your own cards, not the one in a book.

### 1: the Magician, the Wheel of Fortune, the Sun {#ones}

One is the initiator, the raw diamond at the start of a cycle. The Magician has every tool on his table. The Wheel, in my notes, says it’s up to you to turn it: create your own luck. The Sun goes 19, 10, then 1, closing a cycle in full light and opening the next. Their shared minus is 1’s: a lack of humility, when we are all students our whole life.

### 2: the High Priestess, Justice, Judgement {#twos}

Two is the bond, the contract, weighing pros and cons. The High Priestess and Justice both sit between two pillars: one keeps what she knows, the other weighs it. Judgement is a new agreement with your own past. Their minus is 2’s: stubbornness, and the head running the show.

### 3: the Empress, the Hanged Man, the World {#threes}

Three is the machine running well, the project that works. The Empress feeds and makes things grow; the Hanged Man pauses the rhythm so it can start again; the World is, in my notes, action led by the whole, both hands working. Three’s advice fits all three: finish one project before you start another.

### 4: the Emperor and Death {#fours}

Four is structure, the house standing, the fruit of work. The Emperor builds it and gives the orders — and, in my notes, like fire he can also destroy everything. Death answers 4’s minus, carrying on when you should stop: it’s the harvest, cutting what’s ripe so the field can be sown again.

### 5: the Hierophant and Temperance {#fives}

Five is the trial: trying, making do, not being afraid to fail. The Hierophant tests a rule — follow it, question it, outgrow it. Temperance handles the trial by dosage, pouring until the measure is right. Five’s advice: avoid useless fights, and be honest with yourself.

### 6: the Lovers and the Devil {#sixes}

Six is harmony and success, the machine running fast. The Devil is drawn as an echo of the Lovers, two figures under a larger one. One is a choice made with open eyes; the other is a choice that hardened into attachment, with chains loose enough to lift off.

### 7: the Chariot and the Tower {#sevens}

Seven is reflection, analysis before action, and the reminder that everything is temporary. In my notes the Chariot receives his orders: he waits for the call before he moves. The Tower is what happens to a building nobody checked. Seven’s advice holds for both: your situation, good or bad, will change.

### 8: Strength and the Star {#eights}

Eight is mastery, and a gate between the tangible and the intangible; lie it down and you get the infinity sign floating over Strength’s head. Strength masters the lion without force. The Star pours one jug onto the land and one into the water, into both worlds at once.

### 9: the Hermit and the Moon {#nines}

Nine is the finish line of a stage: introspection, patience, last efforts — but keep moving. The Hermit climbs alone with his lantern; the Moon’s path winds off into the fog. Both ask you to keep going before you can see the end.

### 0: the Fool {#zero}

He stands outside the count, or at the 10 that opens the next cycle: the corridor between two rooms. In my notes, the bundle on his stick means he can set up anywhere, free in his decisions. His minus is naivety, and the little dog at his heels is trying to remind him.

### By element {#elements}

The elements give you a second way to sort them. Fire has seven majors — the Emperor, Strength, the Wheel, Temperance, the Tower, the Sun and Judgement: creation and destruction, the energy that gets things moving and spreads fast when nobody masters it. Water has five — the High Priestess, the Chariot, the Hanged Man, Death and the Moon: the slow cards, where things seep in rather than strike. Air has five — the Fool, the Magician, the Lovers, Justice and the Star: decisions and words, a mind that can cut cleanly or go round in circles. Earth has five — the Empress, the Hierophant, the Hermit, the Devil and the World: matter, endurance, and stubbornness too. In my notes, not all riches are gold.

The element sort pays off in a spread. Several Fire majors together and something is moving fast, perhaps faster than anyone planned; several Water majors, and it’s being felt more than decided.

## Where to start {#where-to-start}

Not with a list of 22 to memorise. Here’s the order I’d suggest.

1. Draw one major a day. Before you read anything, describe what’s on it, what you see and what you receive. It’s the exercise I give beginners: you’re reading before anyone has told you what to think.
2. Say the four lenses out loud, then open the card’s page and check.
3. Once a week, take one family out of the deck and lay its cards side by side. Say what changes from one card to the next. In ten weeks you’ve met all 22.
4. When single cards feel familiar, draw three at a time, then more.

If you’d rather start with cards that are already yours, your [tarot birth card](/blog/tarot-birth-card/) gives you a pair from your date of birth. And if one card frightens you, learn that one early: the cards people fear most have [a page of their own](/blog/scariest-tarot-cards/), and they’re gentler than their drawings.

## The major arcana in a spread {#in-a-spread}

**A major in a spread names the theme; the minors around it show how it plays out.** The 56 [minor arcana](/blog/minor-arcana/) carry this week, this conversation, this email.

Several majors in one spread usually mean a period that matters, something larger than the day-to-day. None at all isn’t a bad sign: it often means the situation is in your hands, made of ordinary choices — I explain how I read that in [no major arcana in your spread](/blog/no-major-arcana-in-spread/). Some readers work with the 22 alone; it’s a good way to start, and I go through what you gain and lose in [reading with only the major arcana](/blog/major-arcana-only/).

When a major shares its reduced number with a minor in the same spread, read the two as one stage on two scales: the theme, and the detail. Death next to the Four of Pentacles, say: both are 4. Death clears, the Four clutches, and the question becomes what you’re holding on to that has already finished.

Reversed, a major is the same card slowed down, blocked or in excess: the Hermit who can’t come down from the mountain, the Sun that burns.
