You can practise tarot alone every day, and most of what makes a reader is trained that way: the eye, the lenses, the habit of saying things out loud. What you can’t do alone is check whether you read a person. So practise on your own for the vocabulary, and keep a notebook ready for the day someone sits down in front of you.

## One card a day, described out loud {#one-card}

This is the exercise from my own notes, and it’s still the one I’d start with. Draw one card. Describe what’s on it. Then say what you see in it. Then say what you receive — an image, a word, a feeling that has nothing to do with the drawing.

Do it out loud. Reading is something you say, and the words come differently when you speak them than when you think them. After a week of single cards, move to three. Then more.

**Describe before you interpret.** If you jump to the meaning, you skip the part that trains your eye. The same image can read differently from one day to the next, and you only notice that if you look at it fresh each time. I’ve written more about [the one-card pull as a daily practice](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/).

## Drills with the lenses {#lens-drills}

Drills sound dull; these aren’t. They turn the lenses into reflexes, so that when someone is finally in front of you, your attention is free for them.

1. Sort the whole deck into piles by dominant colour. Yellow, blue, red, grey, black: you’ll be surprised how fast the [colour lens](/library/tarot-colours/) starts telling you what a card is about.
2. Line up the same number across the four suits. Same number, four elements, four ways of living the same stage.
3. Shuffle, turn a card, and name its number, its element and one colour in three seconds. No meaning, only the three facts.

The fives make a good first line-up. The Five of Wands: they seem to fight, without any coordination. The Five of Cups: in my notes the most negative card, alone with your problems. The Five of Swords: an Air conflict, people not sharing the same point of view. The Five of Pentacles: poverty, snow, and a church window where warmth could be found.

Four cards, one [number 5](/library/numerology/number-5/): conflict, the test, making do with what’s left. Once you’ve seen that, you can read any five you meet.

## Read things that aren’t cards {#read-anything}

A painting, a photograph in a newspaper, coffee grounds at the bottom of a cup: all of them can be read. The cards are one surface among many.

Stand in front of a painting, or open a magazine at a photo you’ve never seen. Read it the way you’d read a card. Which colour dominates? Is the person in it turned towards you or away? Acting or waiting? Then say, in one sentence, what the scene is about.

This trains the same muscle without the comfort of a card name you half remember. If you can read a stranger’s painting, a Rider-Waite-Smith card will feel generous.

## Read for yourself, honestly {#for-yourself}

You can read for yourself, and it’s a good way to practise, as long as you stay honest. I give the details in [reading tarot for yourself](/blog/reading-tarot-for-yourself/); here are the three habits that matter most.

Ask about today, not about the big decisions. Tarot isn’t an authority: if you’re choosing whether to move or leave a job, act on what you feel, and let a card be a mirror at most.

Don’t draw again because you didn’t like the answer. The second card is a negotiation, not a reading.

And don’t pull twenty times a day. If you catch yourself reaching for the deck every hour, it’s no longer practice; it’s a search for reassurance.

## Keep a notebook, even with nobody to check {#notebook}

Two columns, as for any reading: on the left what you said, on the right what happened. For yourself, the right-hand column is your day. Write the card in the morning, look again at night.

It won’t prove anything; you know your own life too well for that. What it shows you is how you read — which lens you lean on, which cards you always describe the same way, which ones you skip. That’s worth knowing before anyone else sits in front of you. [How I keep a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) has the layout I use.

## What practice alone can’t give you {#next}

Alone, you’ll never hear “how did you know that?” — because you knew. The real check is someone who tells you nothing and confirms or not afterwards. That’s the only moment that shows you whether you were reading or remembering.

So practise alone for as long as you like, and when you’re ready, find a person. I’ve gathered the places I found mine in [where to find people to read for](/blog/where-to-find-people-to-read-for/). And if you’d like a room full of people who also want to practise, the [free events](/events/) are a good first step.
