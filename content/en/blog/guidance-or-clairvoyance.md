The words around card reading overlap, and each one promises something different. Clairvoyance claims to see what you can't. Guidance claims to point you somewhere. Cartomancy is divination with cards. A reading is someone looking at cards and saying what they see.

That last one is my word. I read and I teach. I don't predict.

## Why the words shift between French and English {#two-languages}

I read and teach in both languages, and the same word doesn't sell the same thing on each side of the Channel.

In French, *voyance* is the everyday word for the whole trade: the phone lines, the websites, the reader at a table with a pack of cards. In English, “clairvoyance” is narrower and a little old-fashioned; most people say “psychic reading”. And there's a trap for English readers: in everyday French, *clairvoyance* mostly means lucidity, seeing a situation clearly. A French colleague who calls you *clairvoyant* is praising your judgement, not your powers.

## The words, defined {#the-words}

Here they are, in plain terms, with the French word beside each.

- *Clairvoyance* (French *voyance*). The claim to perceive what the senses can't reach — usually the future, sometimes hidden facts — with or without a tool. A clairvoyant (*voyant*, *voyante*) might tell you: “You'll move house before the summer.” In English the word is narrow: “clear seeing”, one of several claimed abilities named after a sense, beside clairaudience (hearing) and clairsentience (feeling). For the trade as a whole, English speakers say psychic reading.
- *Guidance* (the same word in French, which took it from English). Messages meant to help you find a direction: advice for a coming period, often presented as channelled. On French social media, *une guidance* often means a collective reading — “guidance for Scorpios, November”. In English you'd call that a general reading or a pick-a-card.
- *Cartomancy* (French *cartomancie*). Divination with cards of any kind: tarot, oracle decks, Lenormand, ordinary playing cards. It's an everyday word in French, where a *cartomancienne* is a woman who reads cards, and a rare one in English, where people say card reading. Lenormand is the clearest example of [a card system with its own grammar](/blog/lenormand-vs-tarot/).
- *Tarology* (French *tarologie*). The practice of reading tarot specifically; the person who does it is a *tarologue*. English has no common single word; people say tarot reader. In French you'll sometimes see *tarologie*, reading the cards as a mirror of the present, set against *taromancie*, using them to foretell.
- *Fortune-telling* (French *dire la bonne aventure*). The old, plain phrase for telling someone what's going to happen to them, traditionally for a coin at a fair; the *diseuse de bonne aventure* is the fortune-teller. It's the image most people still have of tarot.
- *Medium* (French *médium*). Someone who says they pass on messages from the dead. A different claim from all the others.
- *Reading* (French *lecture*). The plainest word. Someone looks at the cards and says what they see. It promises nothing about the future.

**Each word is a promise: to see, to steer, to foretell, to pass on — or only to read.** When you meet one of them on a website, that's the first thing to notice.

## The one I use: reading {#reading}

I haven't got a crystal ball, and I don't sell what one would show. Things about what's ahead do come up in readings sometimes, and when they do I say them. But I don't build a reading on them, I don't promise them, and timing is [where I miss most](/blog/tarot-timing/). I'd rather look at the present: the surest way to a future you enjoy is to anchor yourself in it.

I don't call what I do clairvoyance, because I don't claim to see. I keep count instead, and about one reading in ten misses, by my own tally. I don't call it guidance, because I don't want to steer anyone. And I don't call it a gift, because there isn't one to have: [anyone can learn this](/blog/do-you-need-psychic-abilities/), and teaching it is most of my work.

Have I had experiences I can't explain? A few, and when I tell them, I tell them as they happened. I don't sell them, and they're not what a reading at Maison Tarot rests on.

## What the word tells you before you pay {#before-you-pay}

The word on a page tells you what's being sold. Clairvoyance with dates and certainties sells a prediction. Guidance sells a direction. A reading sells, at most, someone's attention for an hour — and at Maison Tarot it isn't sold at all: [I charge for lessons](/blog/what-i-charge-for/), and readings are free at events.

In France, *voyance* is also a business of premium-rate phone lines billed by the minute, and I've looked at [what one investigation into overcharged lines revealed](/blog/psychic-phone-lines-france/). Whatever the label, one test works everywhere: does the person describe, or promise? Someone who describes can be wrong, and will say so. Someone who promises is selling something cards can't deliver — I've written about [what makes a reader risky](/blog/are-oracle-cards-dangerous/), and it's never the deck.

## If you read, choose your word {#choose-your-word}

If you read for other people, even for free, pick the word that matches what you actually do. It protects the person in front of you from expecting what you can't give, and it protects you from having to pretend. Say “I'm a clairvoyant” and people will ask you when. Say “I read cards” and they'll ask what you see — a question you can actually answer.

If you're learning for yourself, you don't need a label at all. Draw one card tomorrow morning and say what you see — that's a reading. When you want to know what tarot can and can't say about the future, I've set out [what I read instead](/blog/can-tarot-predict-the-future/).
