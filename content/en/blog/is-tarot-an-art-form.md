Reading tarot is an art form, in the plain sense of the word: a practice with a medium, where two people can do the same thing and make different work, and where no result can be repeated exactly. Here are ten reasons, taken from my own notes, and the one place where the comparison stops.

## The deck is already a work of art {#the-deck}

### 1. It starts with a drawing

The deck I use was drawn in 1909 by [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/). She gave every one of the 78 cards a scene, the minor arcana included, which is why you can read them without a book. Her choices are what we read. The High Priestess’s robe falls like a waterfall, and that detail carries much of what the card says about Water.

### 2. Decks are collected like paintings

I treat my decks, tarot and oracle alike, the way a collector treats prints: every card a small work with messages in it. A deck designer makes art meant to be used, a little like a product designer who mixes art and calculation. Few objects sit that neatly between the two.

## Reading is interpretation, not translation {#interpretation}

### 3. There are set forms, and there’s improvisation

Some readers work from fixed layouts: a career question always gets the same shape and the same positions. Others let intuition decide and [design a fresh spread for every sitter](/blog/build-your-own-tarot-spread/). It’s the difference between a sonnet and free verse. Both are poetry.

### 4. Two readers, two stories

Put the same cards in front of two readers and one will see one story, the other a different one, and both can tell you why. Say the High Priestess comes up next to the Eight of Wands.

Are the wands flying, or falling? In my notes that card is “glass half full or half empty”. One reader sees news arriving fast; another sees something coming down that was kept hidden. **Interpretation isn’t a flaw in the method; it’s the method.** More on that in [whether two readers can get different answers](/blog/two-readers-different-answers/).

### 5. The same image changes from day to day

A card changes with the person reading it, and with the day it’s read. Like a novel you reread at twenty and at forty: the words haven’t moved, you have. It’s also why I’d rather nobody learned the cards from a list of keywords: the list stays still, and the reader doesn’t.

## It’s learned like an art {#learned}

### 6. Anyone can learn it; some have a knack

Everyone I’ve coached through a first reading managed it in three goes or fewer. Like drawing: some people have more talent, and anyone who practises gets there. There are no Chosen Ones, which is why I [gently hand back the “you have a gift” compliment](/blog/you-have-a-gift/).

### 7. Its only real technique is letting go

You learn the lenses — colours, numbers, elements — the way a musician learns scales: so you can stop thinking about them. After that, the only practice is letting go and saying what comes. A lens can be learned in an afternoon. Letting go takes longer, and nobody can do it for you.

## It lives like an art {#lives}

### 8. Some are paid a fortune, others nothing

Some readers are paid thousands; others read for nothing. As with painters, the price says little about the work. I give readings away at events and charge only for lessons, and I’ve written about [what a reading costs and what justifies it](/blog/tarot-reading-price/).

### 9. Every spread is a moment you can’t capture again

You can photograph the cards. You can’t photograph what came up, the face across the table, the silence before “how did you know?”. Like a concert, it happens once.

### 10. People think it’s fake, or they swear by it

Tarot divides people the way contemporary art does: “my child could do that” on one side, people who’d travel across the world for it on the other. And I think there’s a reason for that. Art is the only accepted spiritual thing in our society. People who’d never call themselves spiritual will stand in silence in front of a painting and feel something they can’t explain. Tarot asks for that same kind of attention, minus the museum.

## Where the comparison stops {#limits}

A painting can’t be wrong. A reading can.

When I tell someone something specific, I’m making a claim that can be checked, and a tenth of mine, more or less, fail that check. Calling tarot an art doesn’t excuse it from that. I still say “that’s a miss”, write it down, and don’t dress it up as a deeper meaning. And just as a painting doesn’t make your decisions, neither should a reading.

## Try it as an art {#try-it}

The best way to find out what kind of reader you are is to put a card in front of you and someone across the table, and see what comes. That’s how every [workshop](/workshops/) at Maison Tarot begins: a card in the first ten minutes, the explanation after.
