The Marseille tarot and the Rider-Waite-Smith have the same 78-card skeleton and two different ideas of what a card should show. In the Marseille, the forty numbered minor cards are pips: cups, swords, coins or batons arranged in a pattern. In the Rider-Waite-Smith, every card is a scene.

Neither is the “real” tarot. They train different muscles, and I'll try to be fair to both.

## What is the Tarot de Marseille? {#what-is-marseille}

The Tarot de Marseille is a pattern — a standard design that French cardmakers printed for centuries from carved woodblocks, then coloured by stencil in a handful of flat colours: red, blue, yellow, flesh, green, black. Marseille was one of the cities that produced it in quantity; the name stuck later. The story of how the pattern travelled is in the [history of the Marseille tarot](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/).

Its suits are Batons, Cups, Swords and Coins, and its court cards are the Valet, the Knight, the Queen and the King. Its numbered majors carry Roman numerals, written the old way — four is IIII, nine is VIIII. And its numbered minors show no people at all. The Eight of Cups is eight cups arranged in rows between stylised flowers. That's the whole card.

## What is the Rider-Waite-Smith? {#what-is-rws}

The Rider-Waite-Smith is the deck published in London in 1909 by the firm of Rider, designed under the direction of Arthur Edward Waite and drawn by [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/). It keeps the 78-card structure and changes the minors completely: each of the 56 has a scene, with people doing something.

Its suits are Wands, Cups, Swords and Pentacles. The Eight of Cups is a figure in a red cloak walking away from eight stacked cups, towards the mountains, under a moon. You understand something is being left behind before you've counted anything.

## Numbered pips or pictures: how you read each {#pips-or-pictures}

On a Marseille pip, the number and the suit are almost all you have, so that's what you read. Eight is four “exalted”: lasting stability, control, a gate. Cups are Water, the emotions. Put them together and you get emotional stability that holds — and the question of what you do with it. The arrangement on the card helps: are the cups packed tight or spread out, is something growing between them?

On a Rider-Waite-Smith card, you read the scene first and the number comes to confirm it. My note for that Eight of Cups is “moving on from what you're already doing”, and the figure walking away says it without a word.

**The pips make you think in numbers; the pictures make you look.** That's why the Marseille pushes you to know your [numerology](/library/numerology/) cold, and why the Rider-Waite-Smith lets you read a card on the first day. The lenses I teach work on both. On a Marseille pip, number and element do nearly all the work; on a Rider-Waite-Smith card, they check what the picture already told you.

## The names and the order: Bateleur, Papesse, the card with no name {#names-and-order}

The majors are the same twenty-two cards, but several of them go by other names, and two swap places.

| Number | Marseille | Rider-Waite-Smith |
|---|---|---|
| — / 0 | Le Mat | The Fool |
| I | Le Bateleur | The Magician |
| II | La Papesse | The High Priestess |
| V | Le Pape | The Hierophant |
| VI | L'Amoureux | The Lovers |
| VIII | La Justice | Strength |
| XI | La Force | Justice |
| XIII | no name | Death |
| XVI | La Maison Dieu | The Tower |

The Bateleur is a street performer at a table of objects; Waite's Magician holds up a wand, with all four suit symbols laid out in front of him. The Papesse becomes a High Priestess between two pillars. Strength and Justice were swapped by the Golden Dawn, the occult order Waite belonged to, so that the cards would follow the zodiac — Strength with Leo, Justice with Libra. If someone trained on the Marseille talks about “card eight”, they mean Justice; a Rider-Waite-Smith reader will hear Strength. Ask before you argue.

And XIII has a number but, in the Marseille, no name: a skeleton mowing a field. It's often called the nameless arcane. The Rider-Waite-Smith writes “Death” on it and puts a skeleton in black armour on a white horse. In both, the card is about an ending that clears the ground, not about dying — the Marseille's mowing makes my own reading of it as a harvest very visible. More on that in [why Death is the card with no name](/blog/death-card-no-name/).

## Which deck should you learn on? {#which-deck}

The Marseille, if the people who'll teach you read it, if numbers speak to you, or if you'd like to read with the majors alone, as plenty of Marseille readers do — [what you gain and lose with only the 22 majors](/blog/major-arcana-only/) is its own question. It's an old pattern with a long line of readers behind it.

The Rider-Waite-Smith, if you want to read without a book from the first card. It's the deck I read and teach with, and the one the whole Maison Tarot Library is built on, because every card is a scene.

My honest limit: the notebook where I count my hits and misses was kept with the Rider-Waite-Smith, so I have nothing to say about which deck “works better”, and I'd be wary of anyone who claims to know. Don't take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.

## Try both on the same card {#try-both}

Draw one Rider-Waite-Smith card, then find the same card in a Marseille deck — or a picture of one — and read them side by side: number, element, colours, then what you see. You'll know within a week which one talks to you. If you're still choosing your first deck, start with [why I put beginners on the Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/best-tarot-deck-for-beginners/); and for a third way of drawing the same 78 cards, there's [the Thoth tarot compared with the Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/).
