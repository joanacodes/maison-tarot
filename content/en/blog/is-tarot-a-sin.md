I get this question in two forms. The first is theological: *does my religion forbid this?* The second is personal, and it's usually the real one: *can I do this without betraying something I love?* I can help with the second. The first is yours.

## What the traditions actually say {#what-traditions-say}

I'll be brief and careful, because I'm not a theologian and I don't want to be quoted as one.

Several strands of Christianity read Deuteronomy's prohibition on divination as covering tarot, and some churches teach that plainly. Others draw a line between divination (asking the future) and reflection (using pictures to think), and don't object to the second. Most Islamic scholarship treats fortune-telling as forbidden, with the same distinction sometimes made about tools used for reflection rather than prediction. Judaism has a long argument with itself about the topic and, being Judaism, keeps having it. Buddhism mostly shrugs: the question is what you're doing with your mind, not what's in your hand.

If you belong to one of these, the person to ask is the one who guides you in it, not a tarot reader on the internet. I'd be suspicious of any reader who tells you your religion is fine with it. That's not theirs to say.

## What I saw at home {#at-home}

My father prays. The night I heard him call my name from six thousand kilometres away — I was in London, he was in Paris, at his temple, thinking about me — he wasn't reading cards, he was praying. I've told that story elsewhere; what matters here is that nothing in it asked me to choose. He didn't stop praying because his daughter started reading. I didn't stop being his daughter because I did.

I grew up between two ways of taking the invisible seriously, and what I learned is that they weren't competing for the same space. One was devotion. The other was attention. You can hold both.

## What I refuse, and why it helps here {#what-i-refuse}

Most of what religious objections target isn't in my practice, because I removed it for other reasons.

I don't predict. I read a person's present, and I say so. I don't summon, invoke, cleanse, protect or channel anything — [I sold sage in 2021 and stopped](/blog/should-you-cleanse-your-tarot-deck/), because it wasn't doing anything. I don't tell people what to do; [the cards aren't an authority](/disclaimers/#readings). I don't claim a gift. I refuse the questions where a wrong word does damage — health, death, pregnancy — and I refuse anything to do with curses.

Take away prediction, ritual, authority and spirits, and what's left is a person looking at a picture and saying what they see. If that's a sin, so is a novel.

## The question under the question {#under-the-question}

When someone asks me whether tarot is a sin, what I usually hear is: *I'm curious about this, and I'm afraid the curiosity itself is a betrayal.* I can't answer for your tradition. I can tell you that in my experience the curiosity is not where the harm is. The harm, when it comes, is in dependency — asking the cards every day instead of deciding — and in the readers who sell fear. Neither of those requires a deck; both are worth avoiding whatever you believe.

If you want to keep your faith and your curiosity, keep them in separate rooms and don't let either pretend to be the other. That's what I do, and it has held.
