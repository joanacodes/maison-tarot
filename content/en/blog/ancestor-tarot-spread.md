An ancestor tarot spread is a layout for remembering the people who came before you: what they passed on, what you still carry, what you’d like to thank them for. It doesn’t call anyone and it doesn’t carry messages from the dead. The cards are prompts; the memories are yours.

## What is an ancestor tarot spread? {#what-it-is}

An ancestor spread is a set of card positions that turns your attention to the people behind you, one question at a time. Ancestors, here, means anyone whose life shaped yours: a grandmother you knew well, a great-grandfather you only know from one photograph, or someone who raised you a little without being related — a neighbour, a teacher, an aunt by choice.

Samhain is a natural time for it. Many traditions set these weeks aside for the dead: All Saints and All Souls on 1 and 2 November, the Day of the Dead in Mexico, and the Gaelic festival that [opens winter on the night of 31 October](/blog/samhain-tarot/). People who keep Samhain often set out photographs or cook a dish someone loved.

Say you lay it out thinking of the grandmother who taught you to knit. You aren’t asking her anything. You’re asking yourself what she left in your hands.

**If your family is a painful subject, you choose who you remember.** Nobody owes thanks to someone who hurt them, and an ancestor of the heart counts as much as one by blood.

## The five positions {#positions}

Lay five cards in a row, left to right.

1. *Who I’m thinking of.* The card as a portrait: a quality you remember in them, the way they stood in a room. It describes your memory, not their spirit.
2. *What they passed on.* A skill, a habit, a phrase, a way of getting through hard days.
3. *What I carry that isn’t mine.* A fear, a family rule, a silence — something you can set down, with respect.
4. *What I’m grateful for.* Name it plainly, even if it’s small.
5. *What I’ll keep alive.* One concrete thing to do this week: a recipe, a song, a walk they liked, a call to a cousin.

Read the row as one story first, then card by card. Keep a notebook open; [the way I keep a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) works here too — what the card showed on one side, what came back to you on the other.

## An ancestor spread, card by card {#example}

Say someone thinks of a grandfather who kept a vegetable garden and mended everything in the house, then draws the King of Pentacles, the Eight of Pentacles, the Five of Swords, the Ten of Pentacles and the Six of Cups.

Three Pentacles out of five already says a lot. [Earth](/library/four-elements/earth-element/) is matter, things built with your hands, whatever has value for you — and not all riches are gold. The King, on a throne carved with bulls and covered in grapes, is a portrait of someone steady, who provided. The Eight of Pentacles, a craftsman carving coin after coin, is what was passed on: patience in work, the effort that turns into mastery.

The Five of Swords is what isn’t yours: one man gathering swords while two others walk away. Five is conflict, Swords are the mind — perhaps a family habit of having to win every argument. It can be set down.

Gratitude is the Ten of Pentacles: an old man sitting by an archway, dogs at his feet, the whole family present. And what to keep alive is the Six of Cups: a child handing another a cup full of flowers — childhood, taking time, flowering again. In the notebook, that might become one line: “Plant something with a child this spring, the way he did with me.”

## What this spread leaves aside {#what-it-leaves-aside}

This spread asks what someone left you, not what they would say to you now. So it leaves aside the questions people long to put to the dead — are you at peace, what should I do, would you be proud of me — because no card can know their answer. It also leaves aside how and when anyone died or will die, illnesses that run in the family, and who should get the house. Those belong with doctors, solicitors and long family conversations; [why I step back from health and death questions](/blog/health-pregnancy-death-tarot/) explains the rest.

If a question like “did you forgive me?” keeps rising while you shuffle, give it to the third position instead. It may be a weight you no longer need to carry, and the third card asks exactly what you could set down, with respect.

And if the loss is recent, or the grief feels heavy rather than tender, start with [what tarot can hold in grief, and when you need a person](/blog/tarot-for-grief/) before this spread. Sometimes the right door is [a counsellor’s or a therapist’s](/disclaimers/#therapy), and knocking on it is a kindness to yourself.

## Before you lay it out {#before}

Choose a quiet evening. Put a photograph or an object of theirs on the table if you have one — a watch, a handwritten recipe. Shuffle while you think of them, and stop whenever it gets heavy; the cards will wait.

The cards don’t hold your grandfather. You do. Cards aren’t magic, you are — and at Samhain, remembering is the whole of the work. If you’d like to learn the lenses I used above, start with [one card, today](/learn-tarot/).
