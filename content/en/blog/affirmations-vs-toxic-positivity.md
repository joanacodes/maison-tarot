A positive affirmation is a short sentence you choose to say to yourself to steady or encourage yourself. Toxic positivity is the insistence that everyone, you included, should stay positive whatever happens, so hard feelings get pushed out instead of heard. The difference fits in one question: does the sentence leave room for what you actually feel?

## What a positive affirmation is {#affirmation}

An affirmation is a sentence in the first person, in the present tense, that you repeat on purpose: on the way to an interview, before a hard phone call, on a morning that started badly.

Some that work: “I can do hard things slowly.” “I’ve handled worse than this.” “I’m allowed to take up space in this meeting.” “I can be nervous and still speak well.”

What they have in common: they’re believable today, they’re about something you can do, and they don’t argue with your nerves. The last one doesn’t say “I’m not nervous”. It lets the nerves and the ability sit in the same chair.

**A good affirmation sits beside the feeling; it doesn’t try to replace it.**

## What toxic positivity is {#toxic-positivity}

Toxic positivity is positivity used to shut a feeling down — someone else’s, or your own. It sounds like: “Everything happens for a reason.” “Look on the bright side.” “At least it wasn’t worse.” “Positive thoughts only.” “Don’t dwell on it.” And, said to yourself: “I shouldn’t feel this, I have so much to be grateful for.”

People rarely say these things to be cruel. Usually they don’t know what else to say, someone else’s pain makes them uncomfortable, or they grew up hearing these sentences and never questioned them. The intention is kind.

The effect isn’t. The person learns that their feeling isn’t welcome here, so they hide it, and now they’re alone with it.

The kinder version of “everything happens for a reason” is usually shorter: “That sounds hard. Do you want to talk about it, or would you rather be distracted?” It doesn’t fix anything. It lets the other person choose.

In spiritual circles there’s a harsher version: if something went wrong, your negative thoughts attracted it. That’s where [manifestation culture turns into blaming the victim](/blog/delulu-manifestation-tarot/), and where positivity becomes [a way around your life instead of through it](/blog/spiritual-bypassing/).

## How to tell them apart {#tell-apart}

Five questions. You’ll usually know by the second.

1. Does it leave room for the feeling? “I’m scared and I’m going” leaves room. “Don’t be scared” doesn’t.
2. Who is it for? An affirmation is chosen by you, for you. Toxic positivity is often said to someone else to end a discomfort — and sometimes the discomfort is ours.
3. Is it believable today? “I’m learning to manage my money” is. “Money flows to me without effort” usually isn’t, at least not yet.
4. Does it lead somewhere? A good affirmation gets you out of the door. Toxic positivity ends the conversation.
5. What happens if it doesn’t come true? With an affirmation, nothing: you try again tomorrow. With toxic positivity, it becomes your fault for not being positive enough.

## What tarot taught me about this {#tarot}

Every card has a plus and a minus. It’s the first line of my card notes, and the best protection I know against painting every reading pink.

Take [the Five of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/five-of-cups/), which I consider the most negative card in the deck: losing, a dark emotion, being alone with your problems. A toxic-positive reading jumps straight to the two cups still standing behind the figure. An honest one names the three spilled cups first, stays there long enough for the person to feel heard, and only then turns round.

The Nine of Swords works the same way: a long, troubled night, and protection above you that you can’t see yet. The Star is hope after a hard time — after, not instead of.

**The honest reading names what spilled before it counts what’s still standing.** When a card lands hard on someone, the kind thing isn’t to rush to the bright side; it’s to [stay with what the reading touched](/blog/reading-lands-too-hard/) for a moment.

## Try it: one card, one honest affirmation {#try-it}

1. Draw one card. Describe what’s on it before you interpret anything.
2. Name its minus first, in plain words.
3. Then name its plus.
4. Write one sentence that holds both, in the first person, believable today.

An example. [The Eight of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/eight-of-swords/), in my notes, is being a prisoner of yourself while you have every tool: the blindfold can come off. An honest affirmation from it: “I feel stuck, and I have more tools than I’m using today.” From any card numbered seven, whose advice is that everything is temporary: “This is hard, and it won’t stay this way.”

The honest limit: affirmations don’t change your circumstances, and they don’t replace a person. If a feeling is too heavy to sit beside any sentence, that’s [a door for a therapist](/disclaimers/#therapy), not a failure of your positivity.

Reading the plus and the minus of a card out loud, with other people, is what we practise in a [workshop](/workshops/). It’s a good place to learn to say a hard thing kindly.
