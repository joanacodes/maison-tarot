A reversed card is a card that lands upside down when you turn it over. Should you read them? Not in your first weeks: turn the card the right way up and carry on. After that, yes — and without learning a second list of 78 meanings, because a reversed card is the same card, slowed down or in excess.

## What a reversed card is {#what-it-is}

When you shuffle, some cards turn round in your hands. Lay one out and the picture is upside down: the three women of the Three of Cups dancing with their heads at the bottom, the child of the Sun riding along the ceiling. That’s a reversed card, or a reversal.

Readers split on them, and both camps read well. Some keep every card upright, always. Others read each card in the direction it lands. Many of the little booklets sold with decks give every card a second, reversed meaning, which turns 78 meanings into 156.

Nothing on the drawing changes when the card turns. The Three of Cups is still three women and three cups; the Sun is still yellow. **Only the direction changes — and the direction is what you read.**

## Should you read them? My answer {#my-answer}

Not at first. In your first weeks you’re learning to look at 78 pictures, and that’s plenty. If a card lands upside down, turn it the right way up and read it as it is. It’s the same advice I give in [your first reading, step by step](/blog/how-to-do-a-tarot-reading/): reversals are a second lesson, not a first.

Turn them on when upright cards start talking to you — when you look at a card and its colour, number and element arrive before the effort does. There’s no date for that. Your notebook will tell you.

Then make one rule and keep it: **decide before you shuffle, not after you’ve seen the card.** If you read reversals only when the card is a pleasant one, or turn the heavy ones upright when they come, you’re no longer reading the cards. You’re editing them.

## The shortcut: the same card, slowed down or in excess {#the-shortcut}

Here’s what replaces the second list. Read the card upright first, through its lenses — colour, number, element. Then ask one question: is this energy held back, or is it overflowing?

Slowed down means the energy is there but blocked, delayed or turned inward. In excess means there’s too much of it, spilling over. Same card, two speeds. Four examples:

| Card | Upright | Slowed down | In excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Empress | care that feeds and makes things grow | care that has run dry; nobody feeds the one who feeds | care that smothers |
| Eight of Wands | things moving fast, news arriving | the reply that doesn’t come, plans on hold | everything at once, energy scattered |
| Two of Cups | two people meeting, a bond agreed | the conversation put off | two cups poured into one, until you lose your own |
| The Chariot | moving forward, reins in hand | stalled, the two sphinxes pulling apart | forcing it, charging through |

Which of the two is it? The person in front of you usually knows within a second. If you’re reading for yourself, look at the neighbours: a reversed card among still, heavy cards leans towards blocked; one surrounded by Wands leans towards excess. The [Empress page](/library/tarot-cards/the-empress/) shows how far one card can go both ways, and every card page in the Library gives a few lines on its reversed side.

## Why I don’t memorise reversed meanings {#why-not-memorise}

Lists of reversed meanings often give you the opposite of the upright card. Opposites turn every card into a coin — heads it’s good, tails it’s bad — and the picture stops mattering.

The lenses don’t flip. A reversed Five of Cups is still a 5, the number of conflict and trying; still Water; still a figure in a black cloak over three spilled cups. Upside down it doesn’t become a happy card. It becomes the same grief, either held in and not yet cried, or spilling into every room. [The cycle of numbers](/library/numerology/) and the four elements carry over intact, so everything you’ve already learned keeps working.

And reversed doesn’t mean bad. Every card has a plus and a minus, upright as much as reversed. A reversed Chariot isn’t a disaster; it’s a week to stop pushing and look at which way the sphinxes are facing.

## Try it for a week {#try-it}

1. Before you shuffle, decide: this week, I read reversals.
2. Give your deck some reversals: turn half of it round, then shuffle as you usually do. [Any shuffle works](/blog/how-to-shuffle-tarot-cards/), as long as you turn cards over the same way each time you lay them.
3. Draw one card a day. If it’s reversed, read it upright first, then ask: held back, or overflowing?
4. Write one sentence in your notebook, and in the evening, whether it held.
5. At the end of the week, compare with a week of upright-only cards.

If a whole spread comes out upside down one day, don’t reshuffle in a hurry: [check the shuffle first](/blog/all-cards-reversed-tarot/), then read it. And if you’d like to see the shortcut at work on cards you’ve never studied, the [learn-tarot path](/learn-tarot/) starts with the three lenses it relies on.
