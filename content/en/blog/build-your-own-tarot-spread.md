A tarot spread is a set of positions, each one a question, laid out before you shuffle. You can build your own with four questions: what do I want to know, what do I need to see to answer it, how many cards does that take, and how will I know whether the reading held.

Answer those on paper and you'll have a spread that fits your question better than any layout you could copy.

## Question 1: what do I actually want to know? {#what-to-know}

Write it in one sentence. If it takes three sentences, you have three questions, and probably three readings.

Then check that it's a question the cards can hold. The good ones are about your own life, in the present, with something you can act on. “What's getting in the way of finishing my portfolio?” works. “Will he leave her?” is harder: it's about someone who isn't at your table, and it asks the cards to decide something that isn't theirs to decide. Health, pregnancy, court cases and money decisions stay outside my readings too: on those, a mistake is too expensive for the person asking. I've gathered [kinder ways to ask the questions behind them](/blog/questions-to-avoid-asking-tarot/).

**A spread can only be as good as the question it's built around.**

## Question 2: what do I need to see to answer it? {#positions}

List what you'd want to understand about the situation. Each item becomes a position, and each position is written as a question.

For a stuck project, you might want: what keeps pulling me towards it, what's in the way, what would help me take the next step. Three positions. For a choice between two paths: what each path offers, what each one costs, what I'm not looking at. Five.

Two rules keep positions kind and useful. Make each one about the person in front of the cards, not about what someone else thinks or feels. And make at least one about what can be done. A spread that only lists obstacles hands you a weight with no handle.

## Question 3: how many cards, and in what shape? {#shape}

As few as the question needs. One card answers one thing. Three hold a situation with something stuck in it, which is why, once someone has drawn one card daily for a few weeks, I move them on to [three cards, all about now](/blog/three-card-tarot-spread/). Five give room for a real crossroads. Beyond seven, most beginners stop reading and start listing.

Shape matters less than people think, but it does something. A row reads left to right, like a sentence. A cross puts one card at the centre and lets the others lean on it. A circle has no first card, which suits a question about something that keeps coming round.

Decide the number before you shuffle, and don't add a card halfway through. A card drawn because the first ones disappointed you is a second opinion you went looking for.

## Question 4: how will I know if it held? {#check}

Read each position aloud and ask: whatever card lands here, will I be able to say a sentence someone could confirm or contradict? “What's in the way” passes. “The energy around you” doesn't, because any card fits it.

**If no card could prove a position wrong, that position isn't asking anything.**

Then write the spread, the cards and your sentences in [your reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/), with the date. If you'll ask about the same area again — work, a move, a long project — keep the same positions next time, so you can lay two readings side by side and see what moved.

## A spread built from scratch {#example}

Say the question is: “What do I need to know about the evening class I keep putting off?” Three positions: what draws me to it, what's in the way, what would help me start. A row of three, decided before shuffling.

The Page of Pentacles lands first, then the Seven of Cups, then the Eight of Pentacles.

What draws you: a Page, the student, in Earth — a youth in a green field, one coin raised to his eyes. Learning something you can touch, for the pleasure of it. What's in the way: a Seven in Water, a dark figure facing seven cups, each with its own fantasy inside. Too many choices, so none gets picked. What would help: an Eight in Earth, a craftsman at his bench making the same coin again and again. Fixed hours, repeated effort.

As one sentence: “You want this for its own sake; what stops you is that it's one dream among many; choose it and give it set hours.” If you can't read the cards yet without a book, [the three-question cheat sheet](/blog/tarot-cheat-sheet/) — colour, number, element — is all I used here.

## Building a spread for someone who tells you nothing {#blind}

When I read for someone, they don't tell me their question. That means I can't build the spread around it, so I use positions that fit any life: the present situation, what blocks it, what's in your hands. The person keeps the question to themselves and checks at the end whether it got an answer. That's [the silence rule](/blog/the-silence-rule/), and it works for a home-made spread as well as a classic one.

That's my way, not the only one. Some readers lay the same pattern for every career question; others invent a new layout for each person, and both can explain why — one reason a spread, to me, has [something of an artwork about it](/blog/is-tarot-an-art-form/). Each spread captures a moment that won't come back, so build the one that fits it.

## Try it tonight {#try-it}

1. Write your question in one sentence.
2. List what you'd need to see, and turn each item into a position.
3. Cut until you have three to five.
4. Check that each position can produce a sentence someone could contradict.
5. Decide the number, shuffle, lay the cards, write.

Before you build, you can compare with ready-made layouts: I've set out [when a spread helps and when it gets in the way](/blog/do-you-need-tarot-spreads/).
