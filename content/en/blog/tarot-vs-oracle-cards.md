A tarot deck and an oracle deck look alike on a shop shelf, and they don't work the same way. Tarot is a system: 78 cards built on the same skeleton in every deck ever printed. An oracle deck is a set of pictures written by one person, with whatever skeleton that person chose — or none.

Both can be read. Only one of them teaches you a grammar you can carry to the next deck.

## What is a tarot deck? {#what-is-tarot}

A tarot deck is 78 cards in two groups. The 22 major arcana are named, numbered figures: the Fool, the Magician, Death, the World. The 56 minor arcana are four suits of fourteen cards — Wands, Cups, Swords and Pentacles — each running from Ace to Ten, followed by four court cards: Page, Knight, Queen, King.

That structure holds in the 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith, in a Marseille deck, in a modern deck full of cats. The names shift a little, the art changes completely, the skeleton stays. Pick up a deck you've never seen and you still know where the Three of Cups lives.

**That skeleton is what lets you read a card you've never learned.** Every minor card carries a number and an element. Take the four Eights:

In my [numerology table](/library/numerology/number-8/), eight is four “exalted”: stability that has been earned, control, a gate between what you can touch and what you can't. Put it in Fire and the wands are flying or falling — glass half full or half empty. In Water, it's moving on from what you're already doing. In Air, a prisoner of yourself who has every tool to get out: you can take off the blindfold. In Earth, a prosperous business, mastery, the effort it costs. One number, four houses, four readings, and you memorised none of them.

## What is an oracle deck? {#what-is-an-oracle}

An oracle deck is any deck of cards for reflection or divination that doesn't follow the tarot structure. Its creator decides everything: how many cards (thirty, forty-four, sixty), what each one is about, whether there are families, and whether a word or a short phrase is printed at the bottom. A guidebook by the same person gives the intended meaning of each card.

Say you draw an oracle card showing an open hand letting a bird go, with the word “Release” underneath. The message is right there: the creator has done the work of meaning and handed you the result.

That's the kindness of oracles, and their limit. Each card is a single, finished message. It doesn't belong to a family, so you can't cross-check it against its neighbours the way an Eight can be checked against the other Eights. And two oracle decks share no grammar at all: learning one teaches you nothing about the next.

One deck sits in between: the Lenormand, 36 cards with fixed images — the Rider, the Clover, the Ship, the Tree… — read in pairs and lines, almost like words in a sentence. It has its own grammar; I've set out [how Lenormand and tarot differ](/blog/lenormand-vs-tarot/) separately.

## What actually changes when you read {#what-changes}

Side by side, the difference looks like this.

| | Tarot | Oracle |
|---|---|---|
| Number of cards | Always 78 | Whatever the creator chose |
| Structure | 22 majors, four suits, numbers 1 to 10, four court ranks | Free, often none |
| Words on the card | Usually only the name | Often a keyword or a phrase |
| What you read | A scene, a number, an element, colours | The picture and its printed message |
| What one deck teaches you | Every other tarot deck | That deck |

In a reading, a tarot card gives you several ways in. If the picture says nothing to you that day, the number still speaks, and so do the element and the colours. An oracle card gives you one way in, and it's usually a very clear one. On a tired evening, that clarity is welcome. When you're learning to read, it can do the work you should be doing yourself.

One lens crosses over completely: colour. Blue says emotion and yellow says joy on any deck ever printed, so [the colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/) works on an oracle too.

## Which one should a beginner start with? {#which-first}

Tarot — ideally the Rider-Waite-Smith, where every one of the 78 cards is a scene you can read without a book. I explain [why I start everyone on that deck](/blog/best-tarot-deck-for-beginners/). Learn the structure once and every tarot deck after it opens by itself.

An oracle makes a lovely second deck. Some readers draw one oracle card at the end of a tarot spread as a closing note; others keep one for the mornings they don't want to think in numbers. Collecting decks, for me, is like collecting art: each card is a picture through which you can see messages, and that's as true of a beautiful oracle as of a tarot.

What no deck can do is read for you. Cards aren't magic, you are. The reading comes from the person holding the cards, whichever cards they are.

## Is an oracle weaker, or riskier? {#weaker-or-riskier}

Not weaker, as far as I can tell. A card with a word printed on it isn't less “powerful” than a card with a number; it's a different tool doing a different job. I'll be honest about my limit, though: the notebook where I count my hits and misses was kept with tarot, so I have no numbers for oracles.

What I'd watch is the wording. A printed message is absolute in a way a picture never is, and a card that says something alarming on a bad day can land hard. If one card in a deck always makes your stomach drop, take it out — it's your deck. I've written more on [what's actually risky with oracle cards](/blog/are-oracle-cards-dangerous/), and it isn't the cardboard.

## Try both this week {#try-it}

For seven mornings, draw one tarot card and say its number and its element out loud before you look anything up. If you own an oracle, draw one card from it too, and notice how differently the two talk to you.
