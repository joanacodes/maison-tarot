Yes, young adults are the people most likely to read tarot and to believe in practices like it, in the United States as in France. My reading isn't that they're more gullible than their parents. It's that a generation reaching the age of big decisions, at a time that's hard to read, is looking for a language for uncertainty; tarot offers one, in pictures, on a phone.

## What the numbers actually say {#the-numbers}

The clearest recent figures are American. The Pew Research Center surveyed 9,593 adults in October 2024: [23% of 18-to-29-year-olds](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/21/3-in-10-americans-consult-astrology-tarot-cards-or-fortune-tellers/) said they consult tarot cards at least once a year, against 6% of 50-to-64-year-olds and 3% of those over 65. The same report found that most people who use these practices do it for fun, and that only 1% of adults rely a lot on them for major life decisions.

In France, where I live, a figure that circulates is "70% of 18–24s". It comes from an Ifop poll for the magazine *Femme Actuelle*, run online from 10 to 12 November 2020 among 1,007 people. The exact figure is 69%, and it isn't about tarot. It counts 18–24s who say they believe in at least one of six practices — astrology, palm reading, witchcraft, clairvoyance, numerology, card reading — against 54% of 50–64s and 58% of the whole sample, according to [Ifop's own analysis](https://www.ifop.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/focus-211-voyance-sorcellerie-astrologie.pdf).

**Believing in something and relying on it aren't the same, and the numbers show it.** A survey measures what people declare, not what they do with their cards the next morning.

## The post-pandemic boom: real or not? {#after-the-pandemic}

The Ifop poll was taken in the middle of France's second lockdown, and the analysis that comes with it opens on the health crisis. The timing isn't a coincidence.

In the US, the publisher U.S. Games Systems estimated that its tarot sales roughly tripled in the first year of the pandemic, after doubling over the previous five years. It had seen a similar jump during the 2008 financial crisis, as [the Washington Post reported](https://www.washingtonpost.com/religion/2021/12/10/tarot-cards-pandemic-trend/).

That last detail tells you the most. Tarot rises when the future becomes unreadable: a crash, a pandemic. It doesn't make the future readable. It gives you a place to talk about it, with pictures, when words run short.

Two honest limits. The curve was already rising before 2020, so the pandemic sped up a movement rather than starting one. And a poll taken during a lockdown is a photograph, not a trend. I haven't found comparable sales figures for the UK or France. If the "esoteric boom" after Covid is as large as people say, it still needs measuring over time.

## Why 18-to-24-year-olds in particular {#why-them}

What follows is my reading, not a study.

The age of choices without guarantees. Between eighteen and twenty-four you choose a course, a first job, a city, a person — often all at once, with no idea how it will turn out. Tarot doesn't choose for you, but it lets you ask the question out loud. Say you're twenty-two, torn between a master's place and a job offer. A spread won't pick for you; but laying out two paths on a table and saying aloud what each card makes you feel often shows you which way you were already leaning.

A language in pictures, free, in your pocket. A generation met tarot on TikTok and YouTube, not in an occult shop; I've explained [TarotTok for people who don't use it](/blog/tarottok/). My own start was a pick-a-card video: for the first time, someone put words on my situation, and I sobbed — the reader cried too, on screen. I'm not going to sneer at the door I came in by. I've written separately about what [pick-a-card readings can and can't do](/blog/do-pick-a-card-readings-work/).

A ritual without dogma. You can use the cards without signing up to anything, and put them down one day without betraying anyone. For people wary of big institutions, it's a form of meaning that doesn't ask for a membership card.

Telling your own story. **A card is often an excuse to finally say what you think about your own life.** Picture the Five of Cups on the table: a figure in a black cloak, three cups spilled, two still standing behind. Sometimes that image is all it takes for someone to talk about a loss they had never named.

The cards help you focus, or unfocus, so your own assumptions step aside for a moment.

A community. Online, tarot is rarely practised alone: Discord servers, Facebook groups, lives. For a generation in lockdown, it was also a way of talking to someone, and being listened to.

Keeping a hand on the wheel. Drawing a card is doing something when you feel you control nothing. That's the strength of the gesture; it's also the trap, when you start drawing every hour.

## What I'd tell someone of twenty {#at-twenty}

Not rules; a few landmarks.

1. You don't need a gift. Anyone can learn to read, and everyone I've helped managed it between the first and third try.
2. Tarot isn't an authority. If a reading tells you to stay and you feel you should go, listen to what you feel.
3. Drawing every hour isn't tarot any more; it's looking for validation outside yourself. Give the deck a rest; the reasons are in [my piece on taking breaks from tarot](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/).
4. Be wary of paid readings "via the link in bio", of curses to lift, of "he's coming back" sold to thousands of strangers at once. [The nine red flags of a fake reader](/blog/how-to-spot-a-fake-tarot-reader/) catch almost all of them.
5. Some questions have another door. Your health belongs with a doctor; what weighs too much, for too long, deserves [a therapist](/disclaimers/#therapy). The cards can keep you company while you wait; they don't replace those appointments.
6. Keep a notebook: on the left what you saw in the cards, on the right what actually happened. It's the best cure for both gullibility and cynicism, and you'll soon know when you're reading well.

## Learn rather than consult {#learn}

If tarot draws you in, the best present you can give yourself is to learn to read it rather than pay someone to do it for you. It costs less, it's more honest, and you'll find out quickly how much you already know.
