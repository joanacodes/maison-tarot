When the same tarot card keeps coming up, there are three explanations, and they’re not exclusive: the shuffle, your attention, and a theme you’re circling. The first two are ordinary and worth ruling out. The third is the one worth your time.

## The first explanation: the shuffle {#the-shuffle}

Cards are objects. A new deck sticks together. An overhand shuffle moves cards in clumps, so neighbours tend to stay neighbours. A card that’s slightly bent, worn at the edge or often reversed is easier to cut to, because it leaves a gap. If you shuffle the same way and cut at the same spot every morning, you’re helping the same cards come back.

Then there’s the arithmetic, which surprises almost everyone. Draw one card a day from a full, well-shuffled deck of 78. After two weeks, the chance that at least one card has already come up twice is around seven in ten. After a month, a repeat is close to certain, and there’s roughly a four-in-ten chance that some card has shown up three times. Pull three cards every morning for a month and it’s about nine in ten that one card appears four times or more.

**Repeats are what a 78-card deck does; on its own, a card coming back isn’t a message.**

So start there. Change your shuffle for a week: spread the cards face down on the table and mix them with both hands, then gather them and cut somewhere new. I explain the different ways in [how to shuffle tarot cards](/blog/how-to-shuffle-tarot-cards/), and why none of them is wrong.

## The second explanation: your attention {#attention}

You remember the card that means something to you and forget the twenty that came and went. If the Tower frightens you, every Tower counts; the Two of Pentacles drifts by unnoticed. That’s ordinary selective memory, and readers have it too.

The test is to count. Write down every card you draw for a month, with the date, in a notebook — the same habit I describe in [keeping a tarot journal](/blog/tarot-journal/). At the end, look at the real tally. Sometimes the card you were sure was haunting you came up twice, like several others you never noticed. Sometimes it really did come up more often than the rest. Either way, you’ll know instead of feeling it.

Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.

## The third explanation: a theme you’re circling {#a-theme}

Sometimes a card keeps coming because what it describes keeps being true. Not a spirit knocking on the door — a question you haven’t answered yet.

Say someone pulls the Eight of Swords five times in two weeks.

{{< cards "sw08" >}}

A woman stands bound and blindfolded, eight swords planted in the ground around her. Her feet aren’t tied, and the swords don’t close the circle. For me, [the Eight of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/eight-of-swords/) is being a prisoner of yourself while you’re holding every tool you need: you can take the blindfold off. If that person has spent weeks saying they can’t leave a job, the card isn’t stuck. They are, and they know it.

What to do with it: treat the repeated card as a question rather than a verdict. Write down, “If this card were right about me, what would I do this week?” Then do the smallest version of the answer. Sometimes, once something moves, the card stops turning up — or you stop noticing it, which, as the section above shows, can amount to the same thing.

## When the card that keeps coming scares you {#when-it-scares}

The cards people write to ask about are rarely the Sun. They’re these:

{{< cards "ar16 ar13 sw10" >}}

The Tower, Death, the Ten of Swords. Seeing one of them three times in a week can feel like a warning. It isn’t one. Death isn’t about dying; in my notes it’s the harvest, the end of something so that something else can be planted. The Tower is a sudden change, often of something that was already cracked. The Ten of Swords is rock bottom — and the sky at the edge of the card is getting lighter. I go through each in [the scariest tarot cards, and why they’re kinder than they look](/blog/scariest-tarot-cards/).

A heavy card that repeats doesn’t announce a disaster. Read it the way you would read any card: the number, the element, the colours. And if the worry is bigger than the card — you’re reshuffling at night, you can’t settle — put the deck away for a while. The cards will keep.

## What to do the next time it turns up {#next-time}

1. Write it down, with the date and what you asked.
2. Change your shuffle for a week.
3. Describe the card out loud before you decide what it means.
4. Ask: if this card were right about me, what would I do?
5. After a month, count.

If you don’t have a daily practice yet, [the one-card pull](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/) is the best place to start one — and the best way to find out which cards are really following you.
