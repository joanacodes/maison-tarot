Some are good, for some jobs. A tarot app is useful when it shows you a card and lets you think; it’s less useful when it does the thinking for you, keeps your data, or charges you to keep asking. Apps change faster than articles, so instead of a ranking, here are the criteria I’d use.

## What a tarot app does {#what-it-does}

A tarot app draws cards for you on a screen, at random, and shows you the picture with some text about it. Around that core, apps add different things: a card of the day, a choice of spreads, a journal, quizzes for learning, AI-generated “readings”, or a marketplace of paid readers you can message.

Those are very different products under one name. A card-of-the-day app with a notebook is close to a deck and a pen. An app that sells you minutes with a reader is close to a phone line. Knowing which one you’ve downloaded is the first check.

## What to look for {#what-to-look-for}

Five things, in the order I’d check them.

- The deck. Which images does it use, and can you see them properly? Some apps use the 1909 [Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/rider-waite-smith-tarot/) scenes, whose images are in the public domain; others commission their own. Either can work, as long as the card is big enough to read the picture: the colours, the figures, the number. A tiny thumbnail above a long paragraph teaches you to read the paragraph.
- Whether you can turn off the “predictions”. Can you hide the text and see only the card? Can you switch off the yes-or-no answers and the “your future” screens? An app that won’t let you look before it tells you is doing the reading for you.
- Privacy. What does it ask for: your birth date, your location, your contacts? Where do your journal entries go? Anything you type into an app is data held by someone else, and a reading journal can be very personal. Read the privacy page before you write in it.
- Paywalls. What’s free, what isn’t, and how does paying work? Look for credits, per-question prices, free trials that turn into subscriptions, and how to cancel. I’ve set out [how subscriptions and pay-per-question work](/blog/tarot-subscriptions/) on their own page.
- Whether it teaches. Does it ask what you see before it shows you its text? Does it explain why, through the element, the number, the colour, rather than handing you a keyword? An app that teaches makes itself less necessary over time.

**The best tarot app is the one that shows you the card and waits.**

## What to be wary of {#what-to-be-wary-of}

- Notifications that push you to draw. “Your card is waiting” is about keeping you in the app, not about insight.
- Streaks and daily rewards, which turn a practice into a habit loop.
- Yes-or-no answers about health, money, pregnancy or someone else’s feelings. Those are subjects I leave out of readings, because a wrong word there does damage, and a screen can’t see your face when it lands.
- AI “readings” built on what you type. You give it your situation, and it hands it back to you in warmer words. I tested that in [is AI tarot accurate?](/blog/is-ai-tarot-accurate/)
- Paid readers inside the app, billed by the minute or in credits, with a counter you can’t easily see.

None of this means the people who use these apps are doing anything wrong. Plenty of people download one out of curiosity, or because a deck felt like too big a step. The point is to know which parts of the app are there for you, and which are there to keep you.

## Test any app in ten minutes {#ten-minute-test}

1. Draw one card. Before reading anything, write down what you see.
2. Then read the app’s text. Did it add something to the picture, or replace it?
3. Look for a setting that hides interpretations and predictions.
4. Open the subscription page: the price, the renewal, how to cancel.
5. Open the privacy page: what’s collected, and whether you can delete it.

A good app shows you the lightning, the crown knocked from the top, the two figures falling against a black sky, and lets you sit with it. A poor one shows a small image and the word “disaster”. [The Tower](/library/tarot-cards/the-tower/) is richer than that: a structure that can’t hold any more, the moment it gives way, and often the room it clears for something sturdier.

## What I’d use instead, mostly {#what-id-use}

An app does what a deck and a notebook already do, with more distractions. One card a day, drawn by hand, described in your own words and checked in the evening, is [the daily practice I’d recommend](/blog/one-card-tarot-pull/). Write it in a [reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) and you’ll keep a record nobody else holds. If you’d like to learn to read without a screen in between, [start here](/learn-tarot/).
