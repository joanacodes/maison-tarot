The Ten of Swords in a career reading is rock bottom: a job, a project or a way of working that has gone as far as it can go. It’s the hardest drawing in the deck, and it doesn’t announce a dismissal. Look at the horizon, though: behind the man on the ground, the sky is turning yellow.

## The Ten of Swords at work, through its lenses {#lenses}

A man lies face down by still water, ten swords planted in his back from neck to waist, a red cloak over his legs. Half the card is black sky. Low down, above a line of blue hills, runs a band of yellow light. Nobody else is there.

The number is [10](/library/numerology/number-10/): the transition between two stages, the corridor between two rooms. Freedom, change, breathing — and its minus, that it tells you to start something else, ready or not. My notes on the tens hold a sentence I keep for this card: from the outside you might look knocked down, while in reality the butterfly is already far away, out of its chrysalis.

The element is Air: plans, decisions, words. At work, what ends under this card is usually of that kind — a strategy, a role, the story you were telling yourself about where your career was going. And count the swords. One would have done. Ten is the mind after a hard stretch: every “I should have”, every worst case, planted at once. The picture is louder than the facts, which doesn’t make it hurt less.

The colours: black for reality, the end you can no longer avoid seeing; a narrow band of yellow for joy and will coming back; blue for feelings at rest; red for strength laid down. The astrology, if you use it, is the Sun in Gemini, which the Golden Dawn titled the Lord of Ruin: the way of thinking you built everything on, finished. Optional. Every lens is on [the card’s own page](/library/tarot-cards/ten-of-swords/).

## What the Ten of Swords means at work {#work}

At work, the card often describes something that has already happened. The project cancelled after a year of effort. The restructuring that removed your role. The promotion given to someone else, again. Or a slower ending: the job you’ve been holding together with willpower until, one Tuesday, you can’t.

The classic reading is the stab in the back, and sometimes it fits — a colleague who took the credit, a decision made over your head. I’d still count the swords before deciding who planted them all. Several are usually our own: the late nights, the warnings we didn’t listen to, the thoughts we keep replaying.

Say someone sits down, tells you nothing, and draws the Ten of Swords. Lenses before any meaning: an end, the mind going round, a black sky with a strip of light. The sentence you might say: “Something at work is over, really over — and what still hurts is the thinking about it.” Then check. If it doesn’t land, a miss is a miss: don’t stretch the card to fit.

**At work, the Ten of Swords marks the floor, and a floor is something you can push off from.**

## The Ten of Swords reversed at work {#reversed}

Reversed, the swords come out one at a time. You’re getting up slowly: a first application sent, what happened said out loud, a night of real sleep. It’s a recovery that doesn’t hurry.

It can also be the ending refused — staying on the ground in a job that has finished with you, or pulling the swords out only to push them back in, rereading the email that hurt. Or the drama in excess: every setback treated as rock bottom, every difficult meeting as the end of your career.

## The Ten of Swords next to other cards {#combinations}

Next to the Ace of Swords, the cycle closes and a clear new idea opens the next one: after ten comes one. Next to the Six of Swords — a ferryman poling a boat towards calmer water — it’s moving on, and accepting help to get there. Next to the Five of Pentacles, two figures walk through the snow past a lit church window; in my notes, it’s hardship and the place where warmth could be found. Rock bottom, and help closer than it looks: friends, former colleagues, the people whose job it is to help you find work.

If the hurt came from one word or one person rather than a whole collapse, [the Three of Swords at work](/blog/three-of-swords-tarot-career/) is the earlier, sharper part of the same story.

## “Is my career over?” — and a kinder question {#question}

That’s the question this card brings, often with “Am I going to lose my job?” behind it. It comes from fear, and the fear is concrete: the rent, the family, the sense of who you are that comes with a job. I understand.

I don’t read on whether you’ll be dismissed or how your money will hold. A card can’t see a company’s decisions, and money questions are among [the subjects I leave out of readings](/disclaimers/#refusals). If you’re worn out to the point where work follows you home every night, that’s a door for a doctor, not for a deck.

A gentler question: “What has ended, and what is the first small thing I can stand up for this week?” Or, closer to the drawing: “What is dawning on the horizon that I can’t see from the ground?”

## Where to go from here {#next-step}

Write down the ten swords: ten things that hurt about this ending. Then cross out the ones that are thoughts rather than facts. What’s left is the real ground you’ll work from, and it’s usually less than ten.
