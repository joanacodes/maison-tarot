A reading can be wrong in the part that matters most, even when some of its details come true. The clearest lesson I’ve had about that didn’t come from a card. It came from a voice, in a moment of grief.

## The voice {#the-voice}

My closest friend died of cancer.

I was enraged. And in that rage, I asked a question silently: *who’s next?*

I heard a man’s voice. It gave me the name of another friend, and it said she “is going to have an accident”.

Two weeks later, a truck hit her car.

She is alive and well today. I want to say that clearly, early, and without any suspense, because it’s the most important sentence in this article.

## What was right, and what was wrong {#right-and-wrong}

If you count details, a lot of it landed. A name. An accident. A truck, two weeks later. Told that way, it sounds like a perfect premonition, and people who hear the story often stop there.

But I hadn’t asked who would have an accident. I had asked who was next. In the middle of a death, “next” meant one thing. On that, the information was wrong. My friend didn’t die. She is alive, and she is well.

**Information can be right in its details and wrong in what matters.**

That is the most useful thing I know about readings, and I learned it outside tarot. The details are what impress us; they’re what we repeat to friends. What matters is the part we act on — the part that changes how we sleep, what we say, who we call. And that part can be wrong while the details shine.

When I look back at why it went wrong, I find the same three things I find when a card reading misses: timing, interpretation, and the plain fact that sometimes a reading misses.

## Timing: the part that slips most {#timing}

A message, a card or an impression shows you something. It rarely shows you when. With the voice, I had no way of knowing whether “an accident” meant tomorrow, next year or never. It turned out to be two weeks, and I couldn’t have known that, any more than I could have known what kind of accident it would be.

In card readings, timing is the weak spot. I gave it [a whole article on tarot and timing](/blog/tarot-timing/) for that reason.

Timing cuts the other way too. [My very first reading for a stranger](/blog/first-tarot-reading-story/) seemed to miss completely, and three or four days later she texted me that everything had happened. On the day, I would have written it down as a miss. Four days on, it wasn’t one.

So a reading can look wrong today and be right on Friday. It can also be “right” on Friday in a way nobody expected, as the voice was. Neither tells you the reading was useless. Both tell you to hold the timing loosely, and to be slow to decide what a message meant before it has finished arriving.

## Interpretation: where the question fills in the answer {#interpretation}

The words I received were “is going to have an accident”. The word “next” was mine. My grief took a message about an accident and heard it as a message about death, because death was the question I had asked.

Cards work the same way, only with more steps. A picture comes up; the reader turns it into a sentence; the person hearing it turns that sentence into a meaning. Every step is a place where fear or hope can slip in.

Take two cards people dread. [Death](/library/tarot-cards/death/), in my notes, isn’t about dying at all: it’s linked to the harvest, to starting again, to making life fertile once more. The Tower shows a tower on a rock, struck by lightning, its crown knocked off and two figures falling — a sudden shake, something that couldn’t stand any longer coming down.

Someone in grief who draws either one will hear the worst. Someone calm might hear a change they’ve been putting off. Same cards, same reader, two readings — and the difference is the state of the person listening.

It happens with gentler cards too. The Three of Swords — a heart pierced by three swords under a grey, rainy sky — is usually read as heartbreak. In my notes it can also be a blood pact: three people bound by something that cost them. Say a reader draws it for someone who has just argued with a business partner and says “a painful bond”. The reader meant a pact under strain. The person hears “it’s over”, and acts on that. Nobody lied. The meaning changed hands twice and arrived somewhere new.

The reader’s state matters just as much. I was enraged when I heard that voice. Anger and grief are loud, and they get into whatever you receive. It’s one of the reasons I’m careful about [reading tarot when you’re upset](/blog/reading-tarot-when-upset/): not because nothing will come, but because whatever comes will be read through the state you’re in.

And sometimes it’s the other way round: nothing comes at all. In my notes I put it like this — sometimes you can’t see anything in the cards, because of your own block (too much pressure) or because of the person’s (they’re afraid of hearing something). That isn’t the cards failing either. It’s a reading meeting a person where they are.

## And sometimes it misses {#a-miss}

By my own count, about one reading in ten misses. Not “misses for now”, not “means something you haven’t understood yet”. Misses.

With a story like the voice, it’s tempting to rescue it after the fact: there *was* an accident, so it counts. I understand the pull. But to make it count, I’d have to quietly change the question I asked so that the answer fits. I don’t do that with cards either: no clarifier card, no reshuffle, no “the cards meant something deeper”.

Misses aren’t always mysterious, either. Interpreting takes energy, and after a long run of readings you can feel very tired; I sometimes stop after two hours of readings, out of caution. A tired reader is more likely to fill the gaps with guesses. Sometimes the reader is off. Sometimes the person is somewhere else in their head. Sometimes there’s no reason anyone can find.

A miss is a miss. Saying so, out loud, is what keeps the other nine honest.

If you’ve just had a reading that didn’t hold, I wrote separately about [what to do when a tarot reading is wrong](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/) — the practical side, for the reader and for the person who was read.

## The shelf {#the-shelf}

What I do now with information like that voice — or with a card that says something I can’t check — is put it on a shelf. The label on the shelf says: *sceptical, but if it happens, I was warned.*

The shelf isn’t belief. I don’t build my week around what sits on it, I don’t call people in a panic, I don’t rearrange my life. It isn’t dismissal either: I don’t throw the message away and pretend I never heard it. It sits there. If something happens, I’ll remember I was told. If nothing happens, it stays on the shelf, and I was right to be sceptical.

I think that’s the honest place for most predictions, whatever their source. It lets you stay open without being frightened, and sceptical without being closed.

You can use the same shelf with cards. When a reading says something you can’t check yet — an event, a change, a date — write it down with the day you heard it, and put it on the shelf. Then leave it there. Don’t draw again to confirm it, and don’t go looking for it in every phone call. If it comes, you’ll have it in writing. If it doesn’t, you’ll have that in writing too, which is worth just as much.

I still don’t know what that voice was. I can tell you what it said, what happened, and what didn’t. Not everything that comes to us in a moment like that is a message from outside; sometimes it’s our own mind, doing its best under a weight it can’t carry. Sometimes it may be more. I don’t need to settle which to know what to do with it.

If you’re grieving and something like this happens to you, talk to someone — a friend, or a therapist. Not because you were wrong to hear it, but because grief is heavy to carry alone, and [a therapist’s door is sometimes the right one](/disclaimers/#therapy).

## Keep your own record {#your-record}

The most useful thing I can suggest is a notebook with three columns: what was said, what happened, and when. Readings you give, readings you receive, impressions that arrive on their own — all of it.

After a few months, you’ll see your own pattern of misses. The voice taught me to watch timing first. Your weak spot might be somewhere else, and you can only find it by writing it down. That’s what [a reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) is for: not to prove you were right, but to know how you tend to be wrong.
