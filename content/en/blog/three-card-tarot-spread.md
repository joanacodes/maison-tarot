A three-card spread is three cards laid in a row, each with a question attached. The classic version reads them as past, present and future, and it’s a fine place to start. The version I prefer keeps all three cards in the present — what’s here, what’s in the way, what you can do — because everything in it can be checked tonight.

## The classic: past, present, future {#past-present-future}

Shuffle, draw three cards, lay them left to right.

1. The past: what led here. Something already lived that still shapes the situation.
2. The present: where things stand today.
3. The future: where it’s heading if nothing changes.

It’s popular because it’s clear — a beginning, a middle, an end — and it teaches you to see three cards as one story rather than three separate meanings.

It has two weak spots. The past card often tells you what you already know, which feels like accuracy and proves very little: you read it through your own memories. And the future card swallows the attention. People skim the first two to reach the third, then wait for it to happen instead of doing anything. I do read the future sometimes, but **the best way to ensure an enjoyable future is to anchor ourselves in the present.**

## The version that works better: three cards in the present {#three-in-the-present}

Same three cards, same row, different questions.

1. What’s here. The situation as it is today, including the part you don’t say out loud.
2. What’s in the way. What blocks it, or what you’re not looking at.
3. What you can do. One thing within reach this week.

The first card can be checked the moment it’s read: it lands or it doesn’t. The second gives you something to look at rather than something to wait for. The third ends the reading on an action, which is where a reading should end.

Tarot isn’t an authority, so the third card suggests and you decide whether it’s worth doing. Every position is about now, which means every position can be tested now.

## A three-card spread, worked through {#example}

Say someone sits down, tells you nothing, and draws the Five of Wands, the Eight of Swords and the Six of Cups.

What’s here: the Five of Wands — five young men with staffs who seem to fight, without any coordination. Fire, number 5: conflict, trying, plenty of energy and no agreement on where to put it.

What’s in the way: the [Eight of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/eight-of-swords/) — a woman blindfolded and loosely bound, eight swords planted around her, a castle on a hill behind. Air, number 8. In my notes this card is being a prisoner of yourself while you hold every tool: the blindfold can come off. The block is a thought, not a wall.

What you can do: the Six of Cups — two children in a village garden, one handing the other a cup full of white flowers, the whole scene in warm yellows. Water, number 6: taking time for yourself, childhood, harmony. And yellow, in my colour grid, is joy and the inner child.

Read across: Fire, then Air, then Water — noise, then a thought, then a feeling. Numbers 5, 8, 6: the conflict, the stuck point, the breathing space. The sentence out loud: “There’s a lot of noise around you and everyone pulls their own way; what keeps you stuck is a belief that you can’t move; this week, do something small and kind for yourself, the sort of thing you did as a child.”

Then ask whether it lands. If it doesn’t, say so and write it down — there’s no [fourth card to rescue it](/blog/the-clarifier-card/).

## Reading three cards without a spread {#without-a-spread}

You don’t need positions at all. Draw three cards, lay them in a row and read them as one sentence. Instead of asking what each slot means, ask what the three share and where they’re going.

Three questions do most of the work:

- Does one element lead? Three Water cards make it about feelings, whatever the question. [The four elements](/library/four-elements/) give you the rest — and the odd one out is often the point.
- Do the numbers rise or fall? Rising, something is building; falling, something is winding down.
- Which colour carries from card to card?

Say you draw the Ace, the Four and the Ten of Pentacles. All Earth, numbers climbing from 1 to 10: something material is being built, from the first coin to the whole household.

You’ve read the row without a single position. The [guide to card combinations](/blog/tarot-card-combinations/) goes further with pairs.

## Try it tonight {#try-tonight}

1. Write the three positions on a slip of paper: here, in the way, what I can do.
2. Shuffle, draw three, lay them left to right.
3. Before any meaning, name the element, number and main colour of each card.
4. Say one sentence for the whole row, out loud.
5. Write it in your [reading notebook](/blog/tarot-journal/) and reread it tomorrow evening.

When three cards start to feel natural, the [Celtic Cross](/blog/celtic-cross-spread/) is the big version of the same idea — and there’s a lighter way into it than laying all ten at once.
