A tarot reading ends well when the person leaves lighter and holding their own decision. In practice that’s three moves: let them speak, hand the decision back, close on one thing they can do. And there’s one thing to leave out: the next reading.

## Let the person speak first {#let-them-speak}

At Maison Tarot the person tells me nothing before a reading. The end is where that flips: now it’s their turn. Four closing questions work well:

1. What landed?
2. What didn’t?
3. What surprised you?
4. Is there something you expected to hear and didn’t?

Listen without defending. If something missed, say “that one’s a miss” and leave it there; I go through it in [what to do when a reading is wrong](/blog/tarot-reading-wrong/).

The fourth question matters more than it looks. Sometimes what the person came for never came up, and saying so out loud is a relief. They’re allowed to leave with their question still theirs.

## Hand the decision back {#hand-it-back}

Then give the reading back to its owner. A reading can show something; it can’t make a choice, and a reader who lets it decide is carrying a responsibility that isn’t theirs. At the end, I say some version of the same thing every time: don’t act on a card; act on what you feel.

You might put it like this: “That’s what I see. What you do with it is yours. If any of it doesn’t sit right with you, trust that over the cards.” I read; you decide.

**The last word of a reading should belong to the person, not to the cards.** I explain why in [tarot is not an authority](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/).

## Close on one thing they can do {#one-thing}

Don’t end with a recap of every card. Pick one sentence the person can take home, ideally something they can do this week.

If the last card is heavy, read it to the end instead of stopping at its worst word. Say the reading closes on the Nine of Swords: a figure sitting up in bed in the dark, face in hands, nine swords lined up on the wall behind.

“Anxiety” is the word everyone knows. But in my notes, the [Nine of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/nine-of-swords/) is also coming out of a nightmare: the end of a long troubled stretch, with protection above you. The swords are on the wall, not in the bed. So the closing sentence could be: “You’re coming out of something that has kept you up at night. One thing this week: sleep on it before you decide anything.”

That’s an ending the person can carry. A list of ten meanings isn’t.

## What to leave out at the end {#leave-out}

Endings are where readings go wrong, usually with good intentions:

- *A card “for the outcome”.* If the spread didn’t include one, don’t add it at the door. A last card rewrites everything before it; I explain why in [the clarifier card](/blog/the-clarifier-card/).
- *“One more quick question?”* Gently: “Let’s leave it there for today. What we have is enough to sit with.”
- *The next booking.* “Come back next week and we’ll see” turns a reading into a subscription. A reading ends when it ends.
- *A date to wait for.* “You’ll meet someone by spring” sends the person home to wait instead of to live. End on something they can do, not something that will happen to them.
- *Anything for sale.* No cleansing, no protection, no deeper session to fix what came up.

## Lighter, not dependent {#not-dependent}

The point of ending well is that the person needs you a little less afterwards, not more.

Some people arrive almost fidgety, wanting card after card. In my experience it isn’t a hunger for tarot; it’s a high dose of doubt running through them. When I see that, I usually decline to keep going, gently, because what they need is to breathe, make a decision, or go through the thing they’re afraid of. Another reading won’t do that for them.

Others come back with the same question again and again. Say so kindly: “We looked at this a fortnight ago. What’s changed since?” If nothing has, the cards won’t say anything new; there’s more on that in [asking tarot the same question twice](/blog/ask-tarot-the-same-question-twice/).

If someone asks when they should come back, my honest answer is: when something has changed, not when the worry comes back. A reading can be a check-up on your mood and mindset, taken with your feet on the ground. It isn’t meant to be a daily dose. The aim is for the person to trust their own decisions, mistakes included, and to need the cards less as time goes on.

## After they’ve gone {#after}

Write down what you said and what they confirmed while it’s fresh, in two columns. Then look after yourself. Interpreting takes energy, and feeling very tired after a reading is common; I sometimes stop after two hours of readings, out of caution. More on that in [feeling drained after a reading](/blog/energy-after-tarot-reading/).

Next time you read for someone, try the four closing questions and one handed-back sentence. And if it’s a friend across the table, the same care applies from the first card: [reading tarot for someone else](/blog/read-tarot-for-someone-else/).
