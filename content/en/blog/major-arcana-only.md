Reading with only the 22 major arcana is a real way to read, not a shortcut. You keep the named cards — the Fool to the World — and leave the 56 minors in the box. You gain big, clear readings and a smaller deck to learn. You lose the everyday, the people, and the sense of how big a question really is.

## What reading with only the majors means {#what-it-means}

You take the 22 major arcana out of the deck, shuffle them on their own, and read any spread with them: one card, three, a cross. Everything else stays the same.

It's especially common in France, where many readers first met the Tarot de Marseille and learned its 22 majors long before the rest. A small cross of five majors is a classic layout there — I've described [the French cross spread](/blog/cross-tarot-spread/) separately. There's a family echo too: in France, tarot is also a card game played at the kitchen table, and in it the majors are the trumps, with the Fool as the odd card out.

## What you gain {#what-you-gain}

A smaller deck to learn. Twenty-two scenes, each with a name and a story you can tell without a book. You can draw one a day and meet them all in about three weeks.

Readings that speak loudly. Each major describes something that takes months or years: a beginning with no plan, a long withdrawal, an ending that clears the ground. When the question is “what is this period of my life about?”, a majors-only spread answers in the right register.

Short, memorable answers. Three majors make a sentence people remember — useful when someone has five minutes, or when you want a reading you'll still recall in a year.

And the lenses still work. Every major has a number (reduce the ones above 10: the Tower, 16, plays a 7), an element, colours and an astrological attribution. [The 22 majors on one page](/blog/major-arcana/) lays them all out.

## What you lose {#what-you-lose}

The everyday. The minor arcana describe what happens on a Tuesday: an argument, a long day at the bench, a night without sleep. Take them away and every question gets a life-chapter answer. “Should I call her back?” draws the Tower, and suddenly a phone call sounds like a collapse. **With only majors, every question sounds like a turning point, and most questions aren't.**

The sense of scale. In a full deck, counting the majors before you read tells you how big the matter is: none means it's everyday and in the person's hands; three in a small spread means something larger is pressing on it. I've written about [what that count tells you](/blog/major-vs-minor-arcana/). With majors only, everything is big, so nothing stands out.

The people. The sixteen court cards are temperaments: the student, the one who charges ahead, the one who holds things from within, the one who decides. They're the colleague and the sister. The majors have figures too, but they're archetypes rather than people you'd meet — [the court cards](/blog/court-cards-explained/) do a job nothing else in the deck does.

The quick read of the elements. In a full spread, three Cups jump out before you read a single card. With majors, the elements are still there, but you have to know them. And in the attributions I use, seven of the 22 majors are Fire, against five for each of the other elements, so a majors-only reading leans a little towards drive and action.

Variety. With 22 cards, the same ones come back more often. Any given card turns up in a three-card draw about one time in seven, against roughly one time in twenty-six with the full deck. If you've ever wondered [why the same card keeps coming up](/blog/same-tarot-card-keeps-coming-up/), this is part of the answer.

## A majors-only reading, as an example {#example}

Say someone tells you nothing and draws three majors: the Hermit, the Wheel of Fortune and the Star.

The Hermit, a 9 in Earth: a grey figure alone on a peak with a lantern — the end of a cycle, introspection. The Wheel of Fortune, a 10 in Fire: a great wheel turning, and nobody's hand on it. In my notes, it's up to you to turn the wheel; you make your own luck. The Star, 17 reduced to 8, in Air: a woman pouring water under an open sky, giving freely after a hard time.

As a sentence: “You've spent a while withdrawn, finishing something on your own. A turn is coming, and nobody else is going to turn it for you. After it, something like hope.”

It's a strong reading. Notice what it doesn't say: where. Is this about work, a relationship, a move? A Pentacle beside the Hermit would have pointed to work or money; a Cup, to a person. The majors gave the shape of the year and left the ground blank.

## When to use it, and a middle way {#when-to-use-it}

Majors only suit questions about a whole period: the theme of the coming year, what a long chapter has been about, where someone stands after a big change. They make a good frame for [a year-ahead reading](/blog/year-ahead-tarot-reading/). They suit day-to-day questions badly.

There's a middle way I like to suggest to students. Shuffle the majors and the minors as two separate piles. Draw one major for the theme, then three minors for what it looks like day to day. You keep the loud voice and the ground under it.

## Try it for a week {#try-it}

For seven days, draw one card a day from the 22 majors alone, and write one sentence each time. The next week, draw from the full deck. Then reread both weeks side by side and see which sentences you could actually do something with. If a major leaves you stuck, ask it the three questions from [the cheat sheet](/blog/tarot-cheat-sheet/): colour, number, element.
