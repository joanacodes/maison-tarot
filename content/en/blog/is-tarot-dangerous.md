A deck of cards isn’t dangerous. A reading can be, in a few situations, and there are three where I put the cards down: when someone is in distress, when readings have become a dependency, and when a card is being treated as an authority. None of them is anyone’s fault, and each has something better to offer than a reading.

## Is tarot itself dangerous? {#the-deck}

Often the question comes from somewhere else: a family that kept cards out of the house, a faith that warns against them, a film. That fear deserves respect, and I don’t try to argue anyone out of it.

What I can say is what I’ve seen. A card is a picture; it’s a screen, and we mostly fear what we project onto it. I wrote about [whether tarot is evil](/blog/is-tarot-evil/), including the one time something seemed to come home with me. The danger I’ve actually watched, at the table, comes from how a reading is used. These are the three situations.

## 1. When someone is in distress {#distress}

People often reach for the cards when things are at their worst. That makes complete sense: they want something to hold, an answer, a sign that it will be all right.

It’s also when a reading does the most harm. A heavy card lands harder on someone who is already on the floor. A miss — and about one reading in ten misses, by my count — can feel like proof that nothing helps. And sometimes I feel that a thing shouldn’t be said at all, or not like that, because the person isn’t ready.

So I put the deck aside. I stay, I listen, and I ask what would help tonight: sleep, a friend, a walk, a phone call. If it’s more than a hard evening, the door I’d point to is a doctor’s, a therapist’s, or a crisis line’s. **When someone is in pain, the person matters more than the reading.** The cards will still be there next week.

Say a friend calls you late at night after a breakup and asks you to pull cards on whether he’ll come back. You can say yes to her and no to the cards: put the kettle on, let her talk, and offer to read together another day, once she has slept. That isn’t refusing her. It’s choosing what she needs.

I’ve written more about [where the door is in tarot and mental health](/blog/tarot-and-mental-health/), and, for your own deck, about [whether to read when you’re upset](/blog/reading-tarot-when-upset/).

## 2. When readings become a dependency {#dependency}

Some people arrive almost fidgety. It looks like an addiction, but what’s running through them is a high dose of doubt. Some come back with the same question every week.

The signs are worth knowing, for yourself as much as for anyone you read for:

- You’ve asked the cards the same question more than twice this month.
- You felt worse after the last reading, and wanted another one anyway.
- There’s a decision you could make today, and you’re waiting for a card to make it for you.
- You pull cards for yourself many times a day, looking for reassurance.

When I see this, I usually suggest we don’t read. Not as a judgement. What the person needs is a moment to breathe, then a decision, or the courage to go through the thing they’re afraid of. Another reading gives them ten more minutes of not deciding.

What helps: put the deck in a drawer for a month, and take the first small step of the decision without asking anything. I go into it in [why you should quit tarot reading for a while](/blog/why-you-should-quit-tarot-reading/). If the doubt doesn’t ease once the cards are away, that isn’t about tarot, and [a therapist’s door is the right one](/disclaimers/#therapy).

## 3. When a card is treated as an authority {#authority}

Say someone draws the Tower about their job and hands in their notice the next morning. Or draws Death about a relationship and ends it that night.

Death, as I read it, isn’t about death itself. It’s linked to the harvest, to starting again: making life fertile after a stretch without a goal. And the Tower is sudden change, not an instruction to cause one. Neither card tells you what to do on Monday.

Tarot is not an authority. A reading can show you a situation from an angle you hadn’t tried; the decision stays yours, and so does the responsibility for it. I read; you decide. A gentler question helps here too. Instead of “Should I quit?”, try “What do I need to look at before I decide?”, then decide with your own head. If you notice you can’t act without a card’s permission, that’s the muscle to train, and I explain how in [act on what you feel, not on a card](/blog/tarot-is-not-an-authority/).

## A word for readers {#for-readers}

The same three situations apply to you, plus one more thing to watch: your own tiredness. Interpreting takes energy. After a reading you can feel very tired, and that’s your body telling you to rest. I sometimes stop after two hours of readings, out of caution.

Empathy means feeling the other person’s emotions while keeping your own. If you start carrying theirs home, step back. And whatever happens, don’t let tarot replace therapy, or become a circle of dependency, for you or for the person in front of you. Always ask permission before reading anyone.

## When a reading is a good idea {#good-time}

Most of the time, a reading is a curious, light thing: a look at where you are, with your feet on the ground. If you’re wondering whether now is one of those times, I wrote about [when to get a tarot reading](/blog/when-to-get-a-tarot-reading/). If it isn’t, the cards can wait, and you come first.
