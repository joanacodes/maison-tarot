"Everything you said was right" is the sentence readers live for, and the one I trust least. Here is why a reading feels accurate, in descending order of how often it's the real reason.

## It fits everyone {#fits-everyone}

"You're going through a transition." "There's someone you haven't fully forgiven." "You give more than you receive." These land every time because they're true of every adult alive. Psychologists call the effect Barnum, after the showman; horoscopes are made of it, and so is most tarot on the internet.

The test is simple: would this sentence fit my neighbour? If yes, it doesn't count. I apply it to my own readings afterwards, in a notebook, and I'm ruthless about it. It's humbling how much of an average reading evaporates.

## You remember the hits {#remember-hits}

Say twenty things, get four specific and sixteen vague, and the person walks away remembering the four. So do you. A week later the reading has become "she knew everything". This isn't dishonesty; it's how memory works, and it's why every reader who doesn't count believes they're better than they are.

The only defence is to write everything down *before* the person reacts, then mark it afterwards. Hit, miss, or too vague to score. The ratio is the truth; the feeling is not.

## You told them without knowing {#you-told-them}

The person sits down and says "I'm asking about my relationship." Ten minutes of context follow. Then the reader, with cards, reflects that context back in slightly reorganised form, and the person is amazed at how much the cards knew. The cards knew nothing. The reader listened.

This is the one I've built my whole practice around. The person tells me nothing. No question, no situation. If the reading lands, it wasn't because I was handed the answer — because I wasn't handed anything. It's the single change that made my notebook worth reading.

## The reader believes it too {#reader-believes}

There's a fourth reason, and it's the reader's, not the sitter's. A confident reader is convincing. Say "you're carrying something you haven't put down" in a steady voice, hold the pause, and most people will nod — not because it's true of them in particular but because someone sure of themselves just said it. Confidence is contagious, and it inflates every reading it touches.

This is why the first thing I teach is to lower the expectation: tell yourself you're about to talk nonsense. It sounds like a trick to relax. It is also a trick against your own authority. A reader who expects to be wrong says the sentence more quietly, gives the sitter room to say no, and gets a cleaner signal back. The confident reader gets applause; the doubtful one gets data.

A worked example, from my notebook. Sentence one: "there's a decision you've been putting off" — would fit anyone, struck. Sentence two: "it's about a place, not a person" — narrower, half a point at best. Sentence three: "you've already told someone you're leaving, and you regret telling them" — specific, unprompted, confirmed with a very long silence. Three sentences, one hit. The reading *felt* like three hits. The notebook says one. The notebook is right, and one specific hit in three is worth more than three warm nods.

## And then there's the rest {#the-rest}

Switch off all three — apply the neighbour test, count, keep the silence — and something is left. Not "you're going through a transition" but "you turned down a job in April and you're still angry that you were right to". Specific, unprompted, confirmed. It doesn't happen every time; it happens at a rate I can state, with misses I name. I don't know why. [I've written about the theories](/blog/how-does-tarot-work/), and none of them fully covers it.

## How to test your own readings {#test-your-own}

1. Write down every sentence you say, as you say it.
2. Afterwards, strike every one that would fit anyone.
3. Of what's left, mark what the person confirmed and what they didn't.
4. Do this for ten readings before you form an opinion about yourself.

The first time you do it, the accuracy you *felt* and the accuracy you *scored* will be far apart. That gap is the most useful thing a beginner can see, and almost nobody looks at it.
