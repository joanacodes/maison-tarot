## Who he was {#who}

Aleister Crowley was born in Royal Leamington Spa, in England, in 1875, and died in Hastings in 1947. Britannica describes him as an occultist, writer and mountaineer. He grew up in a Plymouth Brethren family, turned against that upbringing early, studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, and left in 1898 without a degree.

That same year he joined the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, where he learned the system of correspondences that ties each tarot card to a Hebrew letter, a planet, a sign or an element. In 1904, in Cairo, he wrote *The Book of the Law*, which he presented as dictated to him, and which became the founding text of his own religious movement, Thelema.

He spent the following decades writing, travelling, founding groups and attracting scandal. The popular press of the 1920s called him “the wickedest man in the world”. Britannica sums up the arc in one line: denounced in his lifetime, he became a cult figure after his death. For this page, the question is narrower: what did he do for tarot?

## What he did for tarot {#tarot}

Two things: a deck and a book.

In June 1937 the writer Clifford Bax introduced him to the painter [Frieda Harris](/library/tarot-figures/frieda-harris/). The tarot they started was meant to take six months; it took from 1938 to 1943. Crowley supplied the design, the symbolism of every card worked out from the Golden Dawn system and his own revisions, and Harris painted it. By Crowley’s own account in his book, she painted some cards as many as eight times before he was satisfied. Her paintings are now held by the Warburg Institute in London.

The book is *The Book of Thoth*, published in 1944 in an edition of 200 signed copies. It explains each card and the system behind it, and most of what people now call “the Thoth meanings” comes from it.

He also changed the deck’s vocabulary. Four majors are renamed: Justice becomes Adjustment, Strength becomes Lust, Temperance becomes Art, Judgement becomes the Aeon. And unlike Waite, he kept the older numbering: Adjustment is VIII and Lust is XI. Here are those four cards as Pamela Colman Smith drew them, for comparison.

## What he did not do {#myths}

He didn’t paint the deck. Every card is Harris’s work. Deja Whitehouse’s recent study for Oxford University Press goes further and argues that her part went well beyond carrying out his instructions: she shaped the images, promoted them and exhibited them.

He didn’t see the cards printed. *The Book of Thoth* came out in 200 copies, and the deck itself was only printed as cards after both he and Harris had died; the first edition is usually dated 1969.

He didn’t invent the correspondences. The scheme he worked from, each major tied to a Hebrew letter and an astrological attribution, is the Golden Dawn’s, itself a reworking of what [Éliphas Lévi](/library/tarot-figures/eliphas-levi/) and the French occultists had proposed. Crowley revised parts of that system; he didn’t create it. [The Golden Dawn page](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/) shows where it came from.

And the deck isn’t dangerous to own or to read with. It is dense, and it carries the full weight of Crowley’s system, which is a different thing. **Cards aren’t magic, you are: that holds for the Thoth as much as for any other deck.**

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

The Thoth is the second deck many readers meet after the [Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/rider-waite-smith-tarot/), and switching between them is more than a change of style. The names change, two numbers change places, and the minors work differently: where Pamela Colman Smith drew scenes, Harris painted the suit symbols themselves, so colour and shape carry more of the meaning.

If you’re learning, I’d start with one deck and stay with it until the [numbers](/library/numerology/) and [elements](/library/four-elements/) come without thinking. Those skills carry over. Then open the Thoth with Crowley’s names in mind, and check where Strength and Justice sit before you trust a number. The full comparison is in [Thoth vs Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/), and the century that produced both decks is in [tarot in the 20th century](/library/tarot-history/tarot-in-the-20th-century/).

The man and the deck can be judged separately. You don’t have to admire Crowley to find the Thoth useful, and you don’t have to use the Thoth to understand why so many readers do.
