Oswald Wirth was a Swiss-born occultist and Freemason who, in 1889, drew the twenty-two trumps of the tarot with Éliphas Lévi’s Hebrew letters on them. His cards put the ideas of the Paris occultists onto something you could hold, and his 1927 book on them is still read.

## Who he was {#who}

Joseph Paul Oswald Wirth was born on 5 August 1860 in Brienz, in the Swiss canton of Bern, and spent his working life in France. He was initiated as a Freemason in 1884, in a lodge at Châlons-sur-Marne, and Masonry stayed at the centre of his life: after settling in Paris he moved from the Grand Orient to the Grande Loge de France, wrote a set of books on the first three Masonic degrees and founded the review *Le Symbolisme*. For decades he was one of the most read voices on symbolism in French Freemasonry.

Around 1887, while working as a magnetic healer, he met Stanislas de Guaita, a young occultist who was about to found the Ordre kabbalistique de la Rose-Croix. Wirth became his secretary and student, and stayed about ten years, until Guaita’s death in 1897. Wirth died in 1943.

## What he did for tarot {#tarot}

In 1889, under Guaita’s direction, Wirth drew *Les 22 Arcanes du Tarot kabbalistique*, “the 22 arcana of the kabbalistic tarot”: the twenty-two trumps only, in 350 copies. He kept close to the Tarot de Marseille and changed details to carry occult symbolism. Above all, he gave each card its Hebrew letter, taken from Lévi and Guaita, so his Magician carries Aleph. The same year, his cards illustrated *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* by Papus, another member of Guaita’s circle; later editions of that book swapped them for a different set.

He kept working on them. A redrawn version, with changes of detail, was published in 1926, and in 1927 came *Le Tarot des imagiers du Moyen Âge*, “the tarot of the image-makers of the Middle Ages”, his long commentary on the trumps, card by card. It appeared in English in 1985 as *The Tarot of the Magicians*.

**Wirth put Lévi’s ideas onto cards you could hold.** The theory behind his letters is on [Éliphas Lévi’s page](/library/tarot-figures/eliphas-levi/), and the period around him on [occult tarot in the nineteenth century](/library/tarot-history/occult-tarot-19th-century/).

## What he did not do {#myths}

He didn’t draw a full deck. His tarot is the twenty-two trumps; the minor cards packaged with some modern “Wirth” decks are not his work.

His tarot isn’t medieval, whatever the book’s title suggests. It is an 1889 redrawing of the Marseille pattern, which has its own, much later history ([the Tarot de Marseille](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/)), with nineteenth-century symbolism added. The title says what he believed about where the images came from, not when his cards were made.

He didn’t invent the letters on his cards, either. They are Lévi’s table, passed on through Guaita; Wirth’s part was to draw them onto the trumps and to defend them in print for decades.

The “Swiss” in this page’s title is the man, not the cards. His deck came out of a Paris occult circle, not from a Swiss card-making tradition.

And he wasn’t part of the Golden Dawn. His letters follow Lévi, so they don’t match the ones the [Golden Dawn](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/) worked out in London.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

If you learn from French books, you’ll meet Wirth’s images and Lévi’s letters sooner or later, often without a name attached. Knowing where they come from saves confusion. When a French author puts Aleph on the Magician, that’s Wirth’s and Lévi’s table, not an error, and [the table of the 22 letters](/library/kabbalah/hebrew-letters-major-arcana/) shows both orders side by side.

Try it with one card. In Wirth’s deck the Magician carries Aleph; on [the Magician’s page](/library/tarot-cards/the-magician/) here, he is Beth. Same figure, two tables, and neither is wrong.

The Rider-Waite-Smith I use was drawn twenty years after his first deck, on other choices. [Marseille vs Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/) puts the two families of cards face to face.

What I keep from Wirth is his patience with the picture. His book takes each trump slowly: what is drawn, and why it might be there. That’s close to how I teach — look before you interpret — and [the learning path](/learn-tarot/) starts exactly there.
