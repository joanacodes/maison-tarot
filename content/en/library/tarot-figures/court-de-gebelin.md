Antoine Court de Gébelin was a Protestant scholar who, in 1781, declared that the tarot was an ancient Egyptian book in disguise. He had no evidence, and he was wrong. He also changed how people looked at the cards for the next two and a half centuries — including the deck on my table.

## Who he was {#who}

He was born around 1725, in Nîmes by most accounts (some sources say Geneva), into a family of French Protestants at a time when their churches still met in hiding, in what was called the Désert. His father, Antoine Court, was the pastor who had reorganised those hidden churches. The son trained for the ministry at the seminary in Lausanne, taught there, and in 1763 settled in Paris to write.

In Paris he took on one enormous project: *Le Monde primitif*, a study that set out to find the single first civilisation behind every language, myth and calendar. Nine volumes came out between 1773 and 1782. He also served the French Reformed churches as their correspondent abroad, became a Freemason and founded a learned society. He died in Paris in 1784, with the work unfinished.

## What he did for tarot {#tarot}

Volume 8 of *Le Monde primitif*, published in 1781, contains his essay on the game of tarot. His argument: the twenty-two trumps aren’t random pictures but the pages of an Egyptian book of wisdom, saved because it was turned into a game nobody would think to destroy. He derived the word *tarot* from two supposed Egyptian words meaning “royal” and “road”, and he reread card after card in that light. The Hanged Man, he said, had been printed upside down: set upright, it showed Prudence.

The same volume prints a second essay, signed only with initials, by the Comte de Mellet — identified by Michael Dummett and his co-authors as Louis-Raphaël-Lucrèce de Fayolle. Mellet called the cards the Book of Thoth, after the Egyptian god of writing. He set the twenty-two trumps beside the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet, and he described a way to tell fortunes with them.

**From that volume on, tarot was no longer only a game: it was a book to be read.** Two years later, [Etteilla](/library/tarot-figures/etteilla/) began publishing a method for reading it. The whole period is told on the page [from card game to divination](/library/tarot-history/from-game-to-divination/).

## What he did not do {#myths}

He didn’t find Egypt in the cards; he brought it there. In 1781 nobody could read hieroglyphs. Jean-François Champollion announced the decipherment in 1822, in his *Lettre à M. Dacier*, and once Egyptian texts could be read, none of them turned out to mention tarot. The oldest tarot cards we have were painted in fifteenth-century Italy, for a game: that story is on [the Italian tarocchi](/library/tarot-history/italian-tarocchi/). The figures Court de Gébelin took for Egyptian priests and gods are Renaissance figures, printed in France on the pattern now called [the Tarot de Marseille](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/).

He didn’t invent tarot reading, either. The method for telling fortunes in his volume is Mellet’s, and turning it into a trade was Etteilla’s work.

And he wasn’t a crank in his own time. He was a respected scholar, and his method — tracing everything back to one lost first civilisation — was a serious project in the eighteenth century, when Egypt fascinated Paris and nobody could check. He was wrong the way a careful person can be wrong when the evidence doesn’t exist yet.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

Every book or video that calls tarot “ancient Egyptian wisdom” is repeating 1781. The idea travelled through Etteilla, then [Éliphas Lévi](/library/tarot-figures/eliphas-levi/) and the occultists of the nineteenth century, all the way to the decks we use now.

So what do you keep? His question was the right one: what does this picture say? Looking at a card as an image that means something, rather than a token in a game, is where reading starts. What you can let go of is the pedigree. A card doesn’t need to be ancient to be useful. The deck I read was drawn in 1909, and it works because of what is drawn on it and what you notice there, not because of its age. Cards aren’t magic, you are — and they aren’t Egyptian either.

If you want to start where he started, with the picture, [the learning path](/learn-tarot/) begins with colour, the first thing your eye sees.
