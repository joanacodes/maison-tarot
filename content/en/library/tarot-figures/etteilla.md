Etteilla is the pen name of Jean-Baptiste Alliette, a Paris tradesman who made card reading his business. He is often called the first professional tarot reader. More exactly, he is the earliest professional card reader we have records of — and tarot came to him halfway through his career.

## Who he was {#who}

Jean-Baptiste Alliette was born in Paris in 1738, the son of a master *rôtisseur*, a cook who sold roast meat. Little is known about his youth, and most of it comes from recent archive work rather than from what he said about himself, which is harder to check. Records show him as a seed merchant in 1763, then as a dealer in prints. He signed his books Etteilla: his own surname, read backwards. He died in 1791, two years into the Revolution.

## What he did for tarot {#tarot}

His first book came out in 1770: *Etteilla, ou manière de se récréer avec un jeu de cartes* — “Etteilla, or how to amuse oneself with a pack of cards”. The Encyclopædia Universalis calls it the oldest treatise on cartomancy in the West. It used a piquet pack — the 32-card deck French players knew — plus one extra card, named Etteilla, which stood for the person consulting. That extra card is the ancestor of what readers now call [the significator](/blog/significator-card/). The book laid the cards out in squares, half-circles and wheels: spreads, in other words.

Tarot came later, after [Court de Gébelin](/library/tarot-figures/court-de-gebelin/) declared it an Egyptian book in 1781. Etteilla took up the idea and ran with it. Between 1783 and 1785 he published *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots* — how to amuse oneself with the cards called tarots — in four parts. In 1788 he gathered his students into a society of “interpreters of the Book of Thoth”. Around 1788–1789 he issued his own tarot, usually described as the first deck made for divination rather than for play. He redrew the trumps, renamed and renumbered many of them, and printed a meaning at each end of every card: one to read upright, one reversed.

**Etteilla made tarot reading something you could learn from a book and buy in a box.** His students carried his meanings on, and they fed into the lists that later occultists gave for the cards — [Arthur Edward Waite](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/) among them. A generation later, [Mademoiselle Lenormand](/library/tarot-figures/marie-anne-lenormand/) would make the card reader a celebrity. The wider story is [on the history page for that period](/library/tarot-history/from-game-to-divination/).

## What he did not do {#myths}

He was not, strictly, the first professional *tarot* reader. He was a professional card reader first, with a piquet pack, and tarot joined his practice about thirteen years later. “The first professional tarot reader” is a fair shorthand, as long as you know that.

He can’t be shown to have read tarot before Court de Gébelin wrote about it. Later in life he said he had been practising since 1757, a claim no record has confirmed. What the dates do show is the order: Court de Gébelin’s volume in 1781, Etteilla’s tarot books from 1783.

And his deck is not an Egyptian book restored. It is a Paris design of the 1780s, built on a theory that was wrong from the start. The decks sold today as the Grand Etteilla descend from it through later versions; they are not his original printing.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

Three habits many readers share come from his table: reversed cards with their own meaning, a card that stands for the person being read, and cards laid out in a set pattern. You don’t have to use any of them. It helps to know they were one man’s choices in the 1770s and 1780s, not rules handed down from Egypt.

On reversals, I don’t learn a second list of 78 meanings. To me a reversal is the upright meaning gone sluggish or gone overboard; [my shortcut for reversed cards](/blog/reversed-tarot-cards/) shows how to read it that way.

And one difference I’ll name plainly. Etteilla sold readings; that was his living, and plenty of honest readers are paid for their time today. My own choice is different: I teach, and the readings I give are free, drawn at random at [Maison Tarot events](/events/).
