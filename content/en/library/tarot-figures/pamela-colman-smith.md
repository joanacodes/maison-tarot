## Who she was {#who}

Pamela Colman Smith was born in Pimlico, London, in 1878, to American parents: Charles Edward Smith, a merchant from a Brooklyn family, and Corinne Colman. She grew up between England, Jamaica and New York. The Kingston years stayed with her: in 1899 she published *Annancy Stories*, Jamaican tellings of the tales of Anansi the spider.

She studied art at the Pratt Institute in Brooklyn in the 1890s, then made her life in London. There she worked in the theatre circle of Ellen Terry and Henry Irving, designing costumes and sets. Her friends called her Pixie, a nickname Terry is said to have given her. She illustrated books, ran a small magazine, *The Green Sheaf*, with help from W. B. Yeats, and in 1907 Alfred Stieglitz showed her drawings at his gallery in New York.

Yeats also brought her into the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, around 1901. That is where she met Arthur Edward Waite. In 1911 she became a Catholic, and she later moved to Cornwall, first to the Lizard, then to Bude. She died in Bude in 1951, nearly penniless; her belongings were sold to pay her debts.

## What she did for tarot {#tarot}

In 1909 Waite asked her to draw a complete deck of 78 cards. William Rider & Son of London published it in December that year. According to the V&A, Waite gave her detailed direction for the 22 major arcana; the 56 minor cards were hers to design.

That second half changed how tarot is learned. In most older decks, the Five of Cups was five cups arranged on the card, and nothing else. Smith gave every minor a person and a situation: a cloaked figure bent over three spilled cups, with two still standing behind. **She turned the minors from patterns into scenes, and a scene is something a beginner can read.**

She didn’t work from nothing. Photographs of the Sola Busca, an Italian deck from the late fifteenth century whose minors are also illustrated, were at the British Museum by 1907, and the tarot scholar Mary K. Greer thinks Waite and Smith probably saw them there. Two of her cards echo it closely: the [Three of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/three-of-swords/) and the Ten of Wands.

She was paid a flat fee. In November 1909 she wrote to Stieglitz that she had just finished a big job for very little money. No share of the sales followed. What she did leave on the cards is her monogram, the letters P, C and S knotted together, in a lower corner of most of them. The [Two of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/two-of-swords/) shows it clearly at bottom right.

## What she did not do {#myths}

She didn’t set the programme of the majors. Waite did, and the order of the cards is his call too: Strength at 8 and Justice at 11 comes from the Golden Dawn, through him. [His page](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/) tells that part.

She didn’t copy an older deck. The Sola Busca borrowings are real, but they’re a handful of cards. The children of the Six of Cups, the bound woman of the Eight of Swords, the family of the Ten of Pentacles: as far as anyone has found, those are hers. The World of Playing Cards puts it fairly: she sometimes took a posture or a composition, but the scenes are her own.

She wasn’t an unknown amateur, either. When Waite hired her, she was a published illustrator and storyteller with a show at Stieglitz’s gallery behind her.

And “Rider” isn’t a person. It was the publisher. For decades the deck was sold as the Rider or Rider-Waite tarot, and her name came back slowly: a 2018 biography, *Pamela Colman Smith: The Untold Story*, built on Stuart Kaplan’s archive; an exhibition at Pratt in 2019; her cards on the walls of the Whitney Museum in 2022. The V&A’s catalogue now says plainly that the old name overlooked her.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

When you look at the Five of Cups and feel the loss before you know a single keyword, you’re reading Smith. Waite’s book gives each minor card a few lines. Her drawing gives you a body, a gesture, a sky, a set of colours.

That is why I start everyone on her deck. My method leans on what is drawn: the [colours](/library/tarot-colours/), the numbers, the elements. Those are her choices — the red of a cloak, the grey of a sky — and they hold together across 78 cards.

Try it with the three cards above. Look at her Three of Swords, then the Ten of Wands, and say what you see before you say what they “mean”. That is the first exercise on [the learning path](/learn-tarot/). The story of the deck itself, its publisher and how it became the standard, is in [the Rider-Waite-Smith article](/blog/rider-waite-smith-tarot/); the order she belonged to has its own page, [the Golden Dawn and its tarot](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/); and [tarot in the 20th century](/library/tarot-history/tarot-in-the-20th-century/) follows the century her cards conquered.
