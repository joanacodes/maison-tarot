Marie-Anne Lenormand was the most famous fortune-teller in Paris for some forty years, from the Revolution to the 1840s. The 36-card “Lenormand” deck carries her name, but she didn’t make it: it was published after her death, from a German parlour game.

## Who she was {#who}

Marie-Anne Adélaïde Lenormand was born in Alençon, in Normandy, in 1772. The Fondation Napoléon’s biography has her working as an apprentice seamstress and a shop cashier before Paris, where she learnt to read cards from another reader and opened an office on the rue de Tournon, a few steps from the Luxembourg gardens.

She read for people of every rank, and she wrote. Her name is on a long shelf of books about her predictions, her clients and her troubles. She died in Paris in 1843.

## What she did for card reading {#cards}

She made the card reader a public figure. She had a known address, clients who talked about her, and her own books in print. She came a generation after [Etteilla](/library/tarot-figures/etteilla/), the first professional card reader on record. How cards went from a game to a trade in those decades is told in [from card game to divination](/library/tarot-history/from-game-to-divination/).

Her most famous client was Joséphine de Beauharnais, the future empress. The best-known story, retold in the Fondation Napoléon’s biography, has Lenormand telling Joséphine, in the years of the Terror, that she would outlive her husband and marry again. Lenormand kept Joséphine as a client for more than twenty years and, the same biography notes, used her name as publicity. Napoleon was less pleased, though as a practical man he weighed the political use of a sibyl so many people listened to.

In December 1809, as Napoleon was preparing his divorce from Joséphine, Lenormand was arrested. She turned even that into a book: *Les Souvenirs prophétiques d’une sibylle sur les causes secrètes de son arrestation* (1814). In 1820 came her *Mémoires historiques et secrets de l’impératrice Joséphine*.

**What she left card reading is less a method than an image: the famous reader with famous clients.**

## What she did not do {#myths}

She didn’t create the Lenormand deck. The 36-card deck sold under her name appeared in Germany a few years after her death. Its pictures come from *Das Spiel der Hoffnung*, “the game of hope”, a game of chance designed by Johann Kaspar Hechtel of Nuremberg and published around 1799. Her name was a selling point.

The 54-card *Grand Jeu de Mlle Lenormand*, printed in Paris in 1845, also came after her death, and nothing shows that she designed or used it.

Her predictions for Napoleon are hard to check. Most of what we know about them comes from her own books, written after the events. The Encyclopædia Universalis is careful here: her fame rests largely on a card game of doubtful authenticity and on memoirs full of claimed encounters, and her biographers have drawn a figure quite far from the real woman.

That doesn’t make her a fraud, and it doesn’t make her a seer. She was a working reader who knew the value of a good story, and her stories outlived her. The cards she actually laid out are less documented than the legend.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

If you read Lenormand, you’re using a German game with a French name on the box, and that’s fine: the system works on its own terms. It isn’t tarot, and it isn’t hers. [Lenormand vs tarot](/blog/lenormand-vs-tarot/) explains how the two differ when you sit down to read.

The other lesson is about famous clients. A list of names proves publicity, not accuracy. I count my misses — about one reading in ten, by my own notebook — and I say so when it happens. A miss is a miss.

And it helps to remember why people went to her: revolution, war, prison, divorce. Most people who ask the cards are worried about someone, and that deserves care more than a show. It’s why I say plainly [what a reading at Maison Tarot can and can’t do](/disclaimers/#readings).
