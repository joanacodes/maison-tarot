## Who he was {#who}

Paul Foster Case lived from 1884 to 1954. He taught tarot, and he founded a school to do it: the Builders of the Adytum, usually shortened to B.O.T.A.

The name repays a second look. An adytum is the innermost room of an ancient temple, the part the public didn’t enter. I read the name as a programme: the school’s students were to build that inner room themselves, through study, rather than be shown into it.

He was born in Fairport, New York, in 1884. Around 1920 he was the chief instructor (the *praemonstrator*) of the American lodge of the Alpha et Omega, one of the orders that grew out of the Golden Dawn. In 1921, after a disagreement with Moina Mathers, who led that order, he stepped down. About a year later he started his own school, which became the Builders of the Adytum; it is based in Los Angeles today. He died in Mexico City in 1954.

## What he did for tarot {#tarot}

He taught it by correspondence. A correspondence course works like this: you enrol, lessons arrive by post one at a time, you study them at home, in order, and you move to the next when you’re ready. Applied to tarot, it means the cards are learned the way a language is learned: a little at a time, with exercises, over months. A book hands you everything at once; a course decides the order for you, and the order is a large part of the teaching.

That was a real change of setting. In the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, where [Arthur Edward Waite](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/) and Aleister Crowley learned their tarot, the teaching was handed out inside the order, grade by grade, to initiates. A correspondence course sends the lessons to a kitchen table.

**Case’s model turns tarot into a subject you study in order, at home, rather than a secret you wait to be let into.**

He also gave his students a deck, the B.O.T.A. tarot, drawn by the artist Jessie Burns Parke. A school that teaches by post needs every student to be looking at the same images; a shared deck is what makes a written lesson about a card possible at all. The deck follows Waite’s 1909 drawings closely, with changes Case saw as corrections, and it was printed in black and white on purpose: students coloured their cards by hand. Colouring a card is a slow way of looking at every detail of it.

His book, *The Tarot: A Key to the Wisdom of the Ages*, first published in 1947, is still reprinted.

## What he did not do {#myths}

He didn’t draw his deck. Jessie Burns Parke did. It is the same pattern you find in the two best-known decks of the century: Waite planned and [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/) drew; Crowley planned and [Frieda Harris](/library/tarot-figures/frieda-harris/) painted. The name on the system and the hand on the cards are rarely the same person.

“Correspondence” here doesn’t mean the occult tables of correspondences, the lists that tie each card to a letter, a planet or a sign. It means post: letters, lessons, envelopes. The two meanings meet in his courses, but the title of this page is about the second one.

And teaching by post didn’t make it a lesser tarot. A lesson read at home is not worth less than one heard in a lodge. The real limit of a course by correspondence is different, and I’d rather name it plainly: nobody is there to watch you read.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

Every tarot course you can take from home rests on the idea Case built a school on: that the cards can be learned, step by step, by anyone who does the work. That is my position too. There are no Chosen Ones; there are people who practise.

The limit he faced is still the one to watch. Lessons can give you the symbols, the [numbers](/library/numerology/) and the structure. They can’t tell you when you’ve read a card wrongly. That is why I suggest keeping a notebook, what you said on one side and what was confirmed on the other, and why I teach in [workshops](/workshops/), with people in the room.

If you want to start the way Case’s students did, with one subject at a time and in order, [the learning path](/learn-tarot/) is built like that. The orders and schools that shaped modern tarot are on [the Golden Dawn page](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/), and the century of decks and courses that followed is in [tarot in the 20th century](/library/tarot-history/tarot-in-the-20th-century/).
