## Who she was {#who}

Frieda Harris was born Marguerite Frieda Bloxam in London in 1877, the daughter of a surgeon, John Astley Bloxam. In 1901 she married Percy Harris, later a Liberal MP and his party’s Chief Whip. When he was made a baronet in 1932 she became Lady Harris, though she preferred to be called Lady Frieda Harris.

She painted, and she was drawn to spiritual ideas well before she met Crowley. She studied with followers of Rudolf Steiner, and researchers have traced the arcs and intersecting planes of her tarot to the projective geometry taught in that circle.

After her husband died in 1952 she moved to India. She died in Srinagar, in Kashmir, in 1962.

## What she did for tarot {#tarot}

In June 1937 the writer Clifford Bax brought her to meet [Aleister Crowley](/library/tarot-figures/aleister-crowley/), who was looking for an artist. The tarot they planned was meant to take six months. She painted it from 1938 to 1943.

Crowley gave the symbolism; she gave it a body. She reworked cards until they satisfied him: Crowley wrote that she painted some as many as eight times, and his biographer Richard Kaczynski counts seven versions of the Magus, the Thoth’s name for [the Magician](/library/tarot-cards/the-magician/), alone. During the work she also paid Crowley a small weekly stipend, two pounds by the surviving accounts. The two of them later disagreed about what it had been for.

She didn’t wait for a publisher. In June 1941 she showed the paintings in Oxford, in a hired room at the Randolph Hotel, after the gallery that had booked the show withdrew. In 1942 she exhibited them in London, at the Berkeley Galleries in July. Crowley later complained that he had heard of the first one only days after it opened. She also used her contacts to find backers for the catalogues and for printing the deck.

**The cards were printed only after both of them had died: Crowley in 1947, Harris in 1962, and the first card edition is usually dated 1969.** A better edition followed in 1977, made from new photographs of her paintings. She left the originals to Gerald Yorke, who placed them with the Warburg Institute in London. In 2025 the Warburg showed them in its exhibition *Tarot – Origins & Afterlives*.

## What she did not do {#myths}

She wasn’t a hired hand copying sketches. The label that stuck to her is Crowley’s “artist executant”. Deja Whitehouse’s study for Oxford University Press argues that her role went far beyond that: she shaped the images, promoted them, exhibited them and raised money for them.

She wasn’t Crowley’s creation as a seeker either. Research indicates that her interest in mysticism came before she met him, and her geometry came from elsewhere.

And the deck in the shop isn’t a perfect copy of her work. The first printing was poor, which is why the paintings were photographed again in 1977, and every edition since depends on how well the originals were reproduced.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

Two women drew the two decks most readers meet: [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/) for the Rider-Waite-Smith, Frieda Harris for the Thoth. Both were treated for decades as the hands of men with bigger names. When you hold either deck, you are reading a painter’s choices as much as an occultist’s system.

That matters for how you read. I teach with [colour](/library/tarot-colours/) as a first lens, and the Thoth leans on it even more than Smith’s deck: with fewer scenes on the minor cards, colour and shape carry the meaning. Harris’s palette does much of the talking.

If you’re choosing between the two decks, [Thoth vs Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/) lays out what changes. The bigger story, of how both decks moved from small circles to bookshop shelves, is told in [tarot in the 20th century](/library/tarot-history/tarot-in-the-20th-century/).
