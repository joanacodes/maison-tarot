This section tells the lives of the people who made tarot what we read today: the scholars, card readers, occultists and artists who, from 1781 onwards, turned a card game into a tool for reading. Each page says who the person was, what they really did for the cards, and what they didn’t do — because many of the stories told about them are wrong, and some of the most repeated are the least true.

A few examples. [Court de Gébelin](/library/tarot-figures/court-de-gebelin/) didn’t find Egypt in the tarot; he brought it there. [Mademoiselle Lenormand](/library/tarot-figures/marie-anne-lenormand/) didn’t create the deck that bears her name. [Éliphas Lévi](/library/tarot-figures/eliphas-levi/) didn’t invent the link with the Hebrew letters, though he made it a system. And [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/) drew all 78 cards of the deck most people learn with.

The figure pages tell a person; [the history pages](/library/tarot-history/) tell the periods, from the first Italian decks to the bookshop shelves of the twentieth century. Read them together: a date makes more sense with a face, and a face with its century.

None of this is required to read the cards. You can read the whole deck without knowing who Etteilla was. But when you know where an idea comes from — a table of letters, a reversed meaning, an Egyptian legend — you can decide for yourself whether to keep it.
