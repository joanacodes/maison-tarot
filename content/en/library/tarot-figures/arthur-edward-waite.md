## Who he was {#who}

Arthur Edward Waite was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1857. His father died at sea, and he was raised in England, a Catholic. He drifted from Catholicism to Spiritualism, then Theosophy, then the occult, and spent the rest of his life writing about it: Rosicrucians, Freemasonry, ceremonial magic, alchemy, the Kabbalah.

He was also a translator. In 1896 he published *Transcendental Magic*, his English version of [Éliphas Lévi’s](/library/tarot-figures/eliphas-levi/) *Dogme et rituel de la haute magie*, one of the books that carried the French occultists’ tarot into English.

He joined the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in January 1891, left in 1893, returned in 1896 and entered its inner Second Order in 1899. When the order split in 1903, he led a branch that turned towards Christian mysticism; it broke up in 1914. He died in 1942. His biographer, R. A. Gilbert, credits him with the first attempt at a systematic history of Western occultism, which is a fair summary of how he saw himself: a scholar first.

## What he did for tarot {#tarot}

In 1909 he asked Pamela Colman Smith, a fellow member of the Golden Dawn, to draw a complete deck. William Rider & Son published it in London in December 1909, with a small book by Waite, *The Key to the Tarot*. The fuller *Pictorial Key to the Tarot* followed in 1910 (it is often dated 1911), illustrated with all 78 of Smith’s cards and subtitled “being fragments of a secret tradition under the veil of divination”.

His part was the programme. The V&A notes that he gave Smith detailed direction for the majors, and several of them depart from the older Marseille pattern. The Lovers, a young man between two women in the Marseille deck, become a naked man and woman beneath an angel. The Sun, two children in the Marseille, becomes one child on a white horse.

He also swapped two cards. The Marseille order numbers Justice 8 and Strength 11; Waite put [Strength](/library/tarot-cards/strength/) at 8 and Justice at 11. This follows the Golden Dawn, which ties the eighth card to Leo and the eleventh to Libra: Leo’s lion belongs with Strength, Libra’s scales with Justice. In the *Pictorial Key* he declined to explain, saying the change “carries nothing with it which will signify to the reader”.

**The book matters as much as the deck: Waite set down a short divinatory meaning for every card, upright and reversed, and many of today’s keyword lists still echo his.**

## What he did not do {#myths}

He didn’t draw the cards. Every image is [Pamela Colman Smith’s](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/), and the 56 minor scenes were largely her design. “Rider” isn’t a co-author either: it is the publisher.

He didn’t publish the Golden Dawn’s system. The order tied every major to a Hebrew letter and to a sign, planet or element; Waite’s cards don’t print those attributions, and his subtitle calls the book “fragments”. Whether that was discretion or taste, the result is a deck you can read without learning the system first. The system itself is laid out on [the Golden Dawn page](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/).

He didn’t invent tarot, or reading the cards. Tarot decks were made in [fifteenth-century Italy](/library/tarot-history/italian-tarocchi/), and reading them was already a practice in [eighteenth-century France](/library/tarot-history/from-game-to-divination/), long before 1909.

And the swap of Strength and Justice wasn’t a whim. It came from the Golden Dawn’s astrology. The Thoth deck, built on the same system, still keeps the older numbering; [Crowley’s page](/library/tarot-figures/aleister-crowley/) explains how.

## Why it matters for a reader today {#today}

If you learned tarot from an English-language book, you probably learned Waite’s order and many of Waite’s meanings, through Smith’s drawings. Knowing that helps in two ways.

First, when two sources disagree about whether Strength is 8 or 11, neither is wrong: one follows Waite, the other the older order. When you bring a major back to 1–10 with the [numbers lens](/library/numerology/), use the number printed on the card in your hand. In this deck Justice is 11, which reduces to 2, and Strength is 8.

Second, his keyword lists are one man’s reading from 1910. They are a good place to check yourself, and a poor place to start. Look at the picture, say what you see, then open the book. The deck’s whole story, from Rider to the box on today’s shelves, is in [the Rider-Waite-Smith article](/blog/rider-waite-smith-tarot/).
