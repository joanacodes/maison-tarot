## What’s on the card {#the-card}

An old man with a long white beard sits on a massive stone throne and faces you without moving. Rams’ heads are carved into the stone. He wears a gold crown and a red robe — and under the robe, at his legs, armour. In his right hand he holds a sceptre shaped like an ankh, the Egyptian cross with a loop; in his left, a golden orb.

Behind him rise steep, bare mountains under an orange sky. Nothing grows there.

What’s missing: softness of any kind. No plants, no other people, no gesture of welcome. He doesn’t move, and neither does the throne. **The drawing is about order held in place by someone ready to defend it.**

## By number: 4 {#number}

The [number 4](/library/numerology/number-4/) is 2 exalted: the alliance has become a structure, the fruit of your work, something that holds. Look at the throne: a block of stone, square and level. The Emperor is a 4 sitting on a 4.

In the story of Instagram on my numerology pages, 4 is the big success that came fast: a shape, a team, habits — and the moment a company starts believing it will never have to change again. Hence the advice of 4: stay flexible, stay open to what you hadn’t planned. Its minus is carrying on when you should stop, out of pure stubbornness.

## By element: Fire {#element}

The Emperor is a [Fire](/library/four-elements/fire-element/) card: creation, destruction, impulse. In my notes he is the father, and Fire explains his kind of authority — he forges, he protects, he lights the way. He gives orders; he doesn’t receive them.

A 4 of Fire is fire in a hearth: contained, useful, keeping the house warm. Under his king’s robe he is ready for war, and that is the minus I note for this card: like fire, he can destroy everything, fast, when nobody is watching the flames.

## By colours {#colours}

Red and orange dominate: the robe, the sky, the mountains. In [my colour grid](/library/tarot-colours/red/), red is passion, action, strength, impulse — masculine energy, a body doing something. The Emperor’s is a deep, mature red; in my grid that shade leans towards purple, the colour of the one a group puts in charge. Grey on the throne: stability, matter that doesn’t move. Gold on the crown and the orb: power that has been earned, or taken.

## By astrology: Aries {#astrology}

In the Golden Dawn tradition, the Emperor goes with [Aries](/library/astrology/aries/), the first sign of the zodiac: “I am”, ruled by Mars, cardinal — the sign that charges. The rams’ heads on the throne are its signature. Aries adds what the stone hides: impatience, the urge to start and to command. Aries is the bricklayer who lays the first stone without reading the plan, and the Emperor is that bricklayer once he has a kingdom.

## In a reading {#reading}

Upright, the Emperor says structure: rules, a frame, a plan, someone who decides. Sometimes it’s an authority in the person’s life — a boss, a parent, an institution — and sometimes it’s their own discipline. I read it for the person in front of me first, not for someone who isn’t in the room ([why I read you, not your boss or your father](/blog/reading-tarot-about-someone-else/)). At work it’s leadership, and the question of leading well, which has its own section below.

Reversed, the fire leaves the hearth or goes out. Either the rules harden and authority turns into control — the manager who can’t delegate, the parent who decides everything — or there’s no structure at all, and nothing holds.

Next to [the Empress](/library/tarot-cards/the-empress/), it’s 3 then 4: what grows, then what protects it. Next to the [Two of Wands](/library/tarot-cards/two-of-wands/) — another man with a globe in his hand, looking out from a castle wall, and an Aries card too — the kingdom is starting to feel small. Next to [the Tower](/library/tarot-cards/the-tower/), the structure is about to be tested by fire. **The Emperor protects what he rules, and the reading asks whether the walls still serve the people inside.**

## At work {#at-work}

At work the Emperor wears several crowns: a boss or an institution above you; your own authority — a first management role, a company you found, your first hire; and structure — processes, a plan, a budget, the dull things that keep a team safe.

His plus is protection: clear rules, decisions taken, people who know where they stand. His minus is that he takes no orders — no feedback, no doubt — and the fire that destroys: the meeting where someone is humiliated, the decision made in anger. In my notes the [Chariot](/library/tarot-cards/the-chariot/) is the warrior who receives orders; next to the Emperor, who gives them, the pair can be a boss and the one who carries out, or the moment you move from one role to the other.

Say someone has just been promoted to lead the team they used to belong to. The card doesn’t say whether they’ll be a good boss; it asks what kind of structure they want to build. **Leading well starts with knowing who is allowed to tell you you’re wrong.**

Reversed at work, the rules harden into control — checking every email, confusing respect with fear — or the throne sits empty and nobody decides anything.

“What does my boss think of me?” is a natural question with this card: that person holds part of your working life. But your boss isn’t in the room. The Emperor gives orders; a reading shouldn’t. A kinder question: “What do I need from the people above me, and how can I ask for it clearly?” If the worry is about your contract or how you’re being treated, HR or a staff representative can tell you where you stand.

This week, ask one person for feedback and act on one piece of it — the one thing the Emperor never does.

## A reading, as an example {#example}

An invented two-card draw: the Emperor, then the [Eight of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/eight-of-swords/) — a blindfolded woman, her arms tied, standing inside a loose fence of eight swords stuck in the ground, with a castle on the hill behind her.

4 then 8: a structure, then the 4 exalted, control pushed to its limit. Fire then Air: a will, then thoughts going round in circles. The Emperor’s red and orange say will and heat; beside a figure who can’t see, that heat has nowhere to go.

The sentence I’d say out loud: “You’ve built rules to protect yourself, and now they’re walls you can’t see past.”

Then I’d check: is there a rule of yours — at work, at home — that you’d drop tomorrow if nobody were watching? My note for the Eight of Swords is that you’re a prisoner of yourself while holding every tool: the blindfold can come off. If the answer is no, that’s a miss, and I write it down.
