## What’s on the card {#the-card}

Five young men, each holding a wand, swing them in every direction on rough, uneven ground under a pale blue sky. The wands cross in a tangle above their heads. Every one of them is dressed differently — a yellow tunic, a red tunic and cap, a dark chequered jerkin, green with red stockings, white with blue spots — so you can’t tell who is on which side. There may not be sides at all.

Look closer: no one is hurt, no one is down, no blow is really landing. Some hold their wand high, others push against a neighbour’s. It could be a fight, a game or a rehearsal. Arthur Edward Waite, who wrote the deck’s first guide, called it mimic warfare.

**Five people, five directions, one jumble: the card is about energy that fights itself because nobody agreed on a plan.**

## By number: five {#number}

**five — conflict, trial, making do with what’s left.** [Five](/library/numerology/number-5/) is the trial: conflict, trying things, not being afraid to fail or to be disliked, doing several things at once, accepting mistakes. In my own notes, the Five of Wands is my example of a 5 where people seem to fight but aren’t coordinated — and that’s the whole drawing.

In the Instagram story, 5 is Facebook arriving to buy, or IGTV flopping: a threat from outside or a bet lost inside, and either way you find out what holds. My advice for a 5 is to avoid useless conflicts and to be honest with yourself and with the situation. Its minus is denial — telling yourself there’s no problem while five wands knock together over your head.

## By element: Fire {#element}

[Fire](/library/four-elements/fire-element/) is impulse, and here it’s impulse times five. Each person has drive, ideas and energy, and each pushes in their own direction. Fire spreads fast when nobody masters it; this is that moment on a small scale — noise, heat, rivalry, no real damage yet.

That’s why I don’t read this card as a war. It’s a group with too much energy and no coordination: a team project where everyone talks at once, a family planning a party with five different ideas, a brainstorm that turns into a shouting match. The teenager who thinks they can do anything, five times over, in the same room.

## By colours {#colours}

The sky is a clear, pale [blue](/library/tarot-colours/blue/): nothing threatening overhead. Whatever is going on is down here, between them.

On the ground, a patchwork — yellow, red, green, white, blue spots, a dark check — five outfits that never match, like five people who never agree. [Red](/library/tarot-colours/red/) comes back most often, in tunics and stockings: action, impulse, strength. Under their feet, the earth is brown and green, lumpy and uneven: they’re standing on something that isn’t settled either.

## By astrology: Saturn in Leo {#astrology}

In the Golden Dawn tradition, the Five of Wands is Saturn in Leo, the first decan of the sign, titled Lord of Strife. [Leo](/library/astrology/leo/) is “I will”: play, challenge, pride, wanting to be seen. [Saturn](/library/astrology/saturn/) is the hardest lesson — limits, structure, the work that takes years. Saturn in Leo is pride meeting a limit: everyone wants to shine, and there isn’t room for five stars on one stage.

It’s an optional lens, and here a useful one, because it turns the brawl into a lesson. The trial isn’t who wins. It’s learning to share the stage.

## In a reading {#reading}

Upright, the Five of Wands is friction: competition at work, a team where everyone pulls their own way, a family discussion going round in circles, a sport, a contest, a rivalry that keeps you sharp. Nobody is necessarily wrong. There’s too much energy and no shared plan. **In the Five of Wands, the problem is rarely the other people; it’s that nobody has said out loud what you’re all trying to do.**

Reversed, the conflict goes underground or gets out of hand. Either everyone avoids the argument — the meeting where nobody speaks and everyone complains in the corridor afterwards — or the game stops being a game, and the jostling turns personal. Both are the minus of 5: acting as if it’s fine, or as if it’s war.

Next to the [Five of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/five-of-swords/), the conflict moves from energy to ideas: an Air conflict, about decisions, where people don’t share the same point of view and someone wants to win the argument. Next to [Temperance](/library/tarot-cards/temperance/), the way out is drawn: mixing the energies instead of choosing one. Next to the [Six of Wands](/library/tarot-cards/six-of-wands/), the trial is over, and someone rides out of the jumble.

## A reading, as an example {#example}

Say someone sits down, tells you nothing, and draws the Five of Wands next to the Ace of Swords.

Lenses first. Number: five, then one — the trial, then a fresh start. Element: Fire, then Air — energy, then decision. Of the four elements, Air is the one that decides best: Fire is too impulsive for it. Colours: the patchwork of the Five, then the plain grey of the Ace of Swords, one white hand, one straight blade, a crown at its tip.

The sentence I’d say out loud: “There’s a lot of noise around you right now — a team, a group, a family — and everyone is pushing their own idea. What’s missing isn’t energy. It’s one clear decision, said out loud, and you may be the one placed to make it.”

Then I’d ask whether it lands and, if it does, which group it is. If it doesn’t, it’s a miss and I write it down. Reading two cards like this, lens by lens, is where [learning tarot](/learn-tarot/) starts at Maison Tarot.
