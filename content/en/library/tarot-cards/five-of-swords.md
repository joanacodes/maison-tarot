## What’s on the card {#the-card}

In the foreground, a young man with wild red hair looks back over his shoulder with a half-smile. He wears a green tunic over red sleeves and red hose, and brown boots. Two swords rest against his left shoulder, held in the crook of his arm; his right hand leans on the pommel of a third, point in the ground. Two more swords lie at his feet, dropped.

Behind him, two figures walk away towards the water, backs turned. The nearer one wears yellow, an orange cloak over one shoulder; the smaller one stands at the water’s edge, head bowed. Beyond them, a blue sea and a far shore. The sky is pale blue, torn by long jagged grey clouds all blowing the same way: the wind is strong.

The drawing is about **a fight that’s over, where the winner holds all the swords and has nobody left to share them with.**

## By number: five {#number}

**five — conflict, trial, making do with what’s left.** Five is the number of the battle: trying, not being afraid to fail or to be disliked, and making do with what’s left afterwards. In my notes, the Five of Swords is an Air conflict — a conflict about decisions, between people who don’t share the same point of view. Not a brawl but a disagreement, and somebody “won” it. The advice of [number 5](/library/numerology/number-5/) is to avoid useless conflicts and to be honest with yourself; its minus is denial. Both are on the card: the man gathering swords isn’t asking whether this fight was worth having. Instagram’s fifth stage was Facebook arriving to buy it, the test from outside where you find out what holds.

## By element: Air {#element}

[Air](/library/four-elements/air-element/) is the mind, decisions and communication, good or bad. That’s the battleground here: arguments, words, being right. Nobody is bleeding on this card; the wounds are in what was said. It’s the viper’s-tongue side of Air, and its tyrant side too — the leader who decides for everybody and then wonders why they’ve all walked off. A Five of Swords conflict is often one where both sides are sure they’re the one thinking clearly.

## By colours {#colours}

The sky is the first thing you see: pale blue cut by ragged clouds, the grey of neutrality torn up, as if the air itself had been through the fight. The winner wears green, growth, and [red](/library/tarot-colours/red/): action, impulse, strength. He’s the one with life in him, the one who acted. The two leaving wear [yellow](/library/tarot-colours/yellow/) and orange, warm colours seen from behind — joy and creativity walking off the field. The blue water they head towards: emotions, to be dealt with somewhere else.

## By astrology: Venus in Aquarius {#astrology}

In the Golden Dawn tradition, the Five of Swords is Venus in the first decan of Aquarius, under the title Lord of Defeat. [Venus](/library/astrology/venus/) is what you love and how you bond; Aquarius is the sign of ideas, “I know” — which I hear as “I think I know”. Together: bonds sacrificed to an idea, caring more about being right than about the people you’re right with. It’s an optional lens, but on this card it lands well.

## In a reading {#reading}

Upright, the Five of Swords is a conflict won at a cost: an argument you came out of right and alone, a decision imposed, a victory nobody congratulates you for. It can also describe the other side — the people walking away, who chose to leave the field rather than keep fighting. Ask where the person in front of you stands: holding the swords, or at the water.

Reversed, the conflict either drags on in the head, every reply rehearsed long after it’s over, or tips into excess: winning at any price, humiliation as a strategy. Sometimes the reversed card is the moment someone puts the swords down and goes to talk.

Next to the [Five of Wands](/library/tarot-cards/five-of-wands/), it’s the Fire version alongside: everyone fighting, nobody coordinated. Next to the [Two of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/two-of-cups/), the way out is visible — a reconciliation, one person holding out a hand. Next to the [Six of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/six-of-swords/), someone leaves the battlefield by boat, and it’s the right call.

## A reading, as an example {#example}

Say someone sits down, tells you nothing, and draws the Five of Swords next to the [Three of Pentacles](/library/tarot-cards/three-of-pentacles/). Lenses first. Five then three: a conflict, then a rhythm that works. Air then Earth: an argument about decisions, then something built together. A torn grey sky, then a craftsman at work in a church, two others standing by with a plan.

You might say: “You won an argument recently, maybe at work, and you’re about to need the very people you argued with.”

Then check: “Has there been a disagreement lately where you got your way?” If yes, ask how things stand with the others now. If no, it’s a miss — note it, and don’t go hunting for another conflict to make it fit. For more on reading two cards side by side, see [how card pairs work](/blog/tarot-card-combinations/).
