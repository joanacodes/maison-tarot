## What’s on the card {#the-card}

There are no people on this card. A big red heart floats in the middle, pierced by three swords: one straight down through the centre, the other two slanting in from either side and crossing inside it. The three points come out underneath. Heavy grey clouds fill the top of the card, and slanting lines of rain fall across the whole background.

Pamela Colman Smith doesn’t show who hurt whom, or how. No faces, no landscape, no ground to stand on: only the wound and the weather.

The drawing is about **a pain with a precise shape — something said, decided or understood that went straight to the heart.**

## By number: three {#number}

Three is the good rhythm: the machine running, the contract that works, siblings on good terms. So why is this three the painful one? Because the minus of [number 3](/library/numerology/number-3/) is betrayal and solitude, and in Air three can be one person too many in a thought. The classic reading is the triangle: two people and a third, a word overheard, a decision taken over someone’s head.

My notes add another reading, and I’m attached to it: the Three of Swords is also a blood pact. Three blades meeting in one heart, like the old gesture of cutting a palm to seal a promise. Sometimes the pain is what binds — two people who went through the same loss, a loyalty sworn after something hard. Ask which one it is before assuming the worst.

## By element: Air {#element}

It surprises people: the most emotional image in the deck belongs to [Air](/library/four-elements/air-element/), not Water. That’s the key to it. The Swords are the mind and communication, so this is the hurt that arrives through words — a sentence that cut, a message read too late at night, the viper’s tongue of gossip. And because Air can obsess, it’s the hurt that keeps replaying in the head long after the event.

## By colours {#colours}

Two colours, almost nothing else. The [red](/library/tarot-colours/red/) of the heart: passion, life, strength. This is a heart that’s alive, still full. Around it, grey everywhere — clouds, rain, background — the colour of no particular will, the flat weather of the days after. Read like that, before any meaning, the card says: a heart that still beats, in weather that will change, because weather does.

## By astrology: Saturn in Libra {#astrology}

In the Golden Dawn tradition, the Three of Swords is Saturn in the second decan of Libra. [Saturn](/library/astrology/saturn/) is the hardest lesson, the place where you become solid precisely because it was hard; in traditional astrology it is exalted in Libra, the sign of balance and of relationships. A painful lesson about a bond, then, that leaves you better built afterwards. Keep the lens if it helps; the pierced heart speaks for itself.

## In a reading {#reading}

Upright, the Three of Swords is heartbreak, grief, disappointment — a real hurt, named plainly. I don’t dress it up, and I don’t read it as a sentence either: the card shows the moment of the wound, not the rest of the story. Often the person already knows what it’s about, and the card puts it on the table so it can be talked about.

A pierced heart on a card doesn’t announce a heart problem or anyone’s death. If your body worries you, that’s a question for a doctor, and [health is one of the subjects I don’t read on](/disclaimers/#refusals); the cards can sit with the worry, not with the diagnosis.

Reversed, the same pain is held in: the wound nobody mentions, the grief swallowed to keep going. Or in excess — the scene replayed again and again, the swords pulled out and pushed back in. Either way, the question is who you could say this to.

Next to the [Three of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/three-of-cups/), the triangle shows: three people, and one of them hurting. Next to the [Six of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/six-of-swords/), the crossing has started and the hurt is being left behind, slowly. Next to [the Star](/library/tarot-cards/the-star/), the rain has stopped and someone is pouring water out again: repair is possible, at its own pace. I’ve written more on [the Three of Swords in love](/blog/three-of-swords-tarot-love/).

## At work {#at-work}

At work, the hurt of this card usually arrives in words: feedback harsher than it needed to be, a remark in a meeting, a colleague repeating something you said in confidence. Sometimes it’s your own decision: leaving a job you loved because it had stopped loving you back. A 3 at work is the team that clicks; its minus, in my notes, includes starting new projects before the others are finished, which is how a lot of teams come apart. And the blood pact has its work version: colleagues who went through a crisis together and came out tighter.

Reversed at work, the hurt is held in — “I’m fine, it’s only work”, said through a tight jaw for months — or the email is reread until you know it by heart.

People ask: “Is my boss against me? Did they do it on purpose?” It comes from feeling unsafe at work, which is exhausting, and I understand wanting to know. I don’t read on that: the colleague isn’t in the room and [hasn’t agreed to be read](/blog/reading-tarot-about-someone-else/), and a card asked “are they against me?” answers with your fear, so you go back on Monday more on guard than before. A gentler question: “What did that sentence touch in me, and what do I need now — to say it, to rest, or to get help?” Whether you speak up, stay or leave is yours to weigh.

If the hurt keeps repeating — humiliation, pressure, being singled out — it’s no longer a card’s business. Talk to HR, a staff representative or your occupational health service, and to a lawyer for the legal side; an ongoing case is something I don’t read on. If it follows you home every night, a doctor or a therapist is the right door.

This week, before you answer anyone, write down exactly what was said, then what you heard. They’re often different.

## A reading, as an example {#example}

Work through this pair as an exercise: someone draws the Three of Swords, then the Four of Swords. Three then four: the rhythm broken, then a structure that holds. Air and Air: it’s happening in words and in the head. Red and grey, then a knight lying still on a tomb, hands joined.

You might say, gently: “Something hurt you recently, probably something that was said, and what’s asked of you now isn’t to fix it but to stop for a while.”

Then check, with care: “Does that mean anything to you?” — and leave room. If the person wants to say more, listen; if not, that’s fine. If it doesn’t fit, it’s a miss, and you say so rather than looking for a pain that isn’t there.
