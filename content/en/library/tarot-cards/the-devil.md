## What’s on the card {#the-card}

A horned creature perches on a squat black block: goat’s horns, bat’s wings, hairy legs ending in claws, an upside-down star between his horns. His right hand is raised, palm out; Arthur Edward Waite, who devised the deck, called it the reverse of the Hierophant’s blessing. His left hand points a lit torch at the ground.

Two chains run from a ring on the block to the necks of a naked man and a naked woman, who have small horns and tails of their own: hers ends in grapes, his in a flame. They look like the couple from the Lovers, and nobody holds the other end. The chains hang loose on their shoulders. **They could lift them off over their heads, and nothing in the drawing stops them.**

No lock, no guard, no door; black from edge to edge, with no sky. The drawing is about an attachment that holds because no one has tried to take it off.

## By number: 15 {#number}

Fifteen comes back to [6](/library/numerology/number-6/), the number of the Lovers — hence the familiar couple. Six is success running fast, and its advice is to stay calm and humble while things go well; the Devil is the 6 that forgot it. In the Instagram story, 6 is the moment Facebook invests: everything speeds up, and nobody reads the conditions any more. That’s the Devil in one line — a deal that pays, and small print you stopped reading.

## By element: Earth {#element}

The Devil is an [Earth](/library/four-elements/earth-element/) card: matter, and whatever has value for you, money or sentimental. Earth is tenacity and endurance — and stubbornness. It’s what holds, and here holding is the whole subject: the job you keep because it pays, the habit you keep because it soothes, the relationship you keep because it’s familiar.

What chains you is usually something you value, which is exactly why it’s hard to see it as a chain.

## By colours {#colours}

[Black](/library/tarot-colours/black/) from edge to edge: in my colour grid, absence, void, heavy thoughts — and also reality, the bare thing without varnish. Then orange: the creature’s body, the couple’s skin, the flames of the torch and of the man’s tail — in my grid, creativity and libido. The creature and the two people are painted in the same warm tone, as if what holds them were made of the same stuff they are. Read the colours before the card and you already have: something real and heavy, a desire that keeps burning, and a hold that comes from inside more than from outside.

## By astrology: Capricorn {#astrology}

In the Golden Dawn tradition, the Devil belongs to [Capricorn](/library/astrology/capricorn/): its verb is “I use”, its animal the goat — the horns this creature wears. Capricorn is ambition, structure and work; well placed, it’s responsibility taken, badly placed, the tool that became the master. A good way into a card that is rarely about evil, and far more often about something useful that took over.

## In a reading {#reading}

Upright, the Devil is an attachment: to a job, a person, a habit, a picture of yourself. Every card has a plus and a minus, and the Devil’s plus is real — desire, ambition, the energy to want things. Its minus is when the wanting runs you. So the question isn’t “what’s wrong with you?” but “what are you holding that you could put down?” Sometimes it’s the deck itself: pulling cards every day about the same worry is a chain too.

Reversed, it’s often the chain starting to slip: you notice the loop and lift a hand to it. Or the same attachment in excess — doubling down, hiding it better, calling it a choice.

Next to [the Lovers](/library/tarot-cards/the-lovers/), the Devil asks whether a choice is still a choice. Next to the [Eight of Swords](/library/tarot-cards/eight-of-swords/) — a prisoner of yourself while you hold every tool — it says twice that the blindfold comes off. Next to the [Four of Pentacles](/library/tarot-cards/four-of-pentacles/), it’s holding on so tightly you can’t use what you have.

**The Devil shows you what holds you, and that the chain was never locked.** For questions of love, I’ve written about [the Devil in a relationship](/blog/devil-tarot-love/); a job that holds you has its own section below.

## At work {#at-work}

At work, the Devil is a job that holds you more than you hold it: golden handcuffs, a salary or bonus that makes leaving feel impossible; a job title that became the answer to “who are you?”; emails answered at eleven at night because stopping feels dangerous. Among my notes on Capricorn, the Devil’s sign, is Miranda Priestly from *The Devil Wears Prada*: ambition, structure, and a job that can swallow the person doing it.

Say someone has stayed eight years in a job they call “fine” and draws the Devil. The card doesn’t say the job is bad; it asks what, exactly, keeps them there. Next to the Eight of Cups — in my notes, moving on from what you’re already doing — it still doesn’t say leave. It shows that leaving is possible.

Reversed at work, the chain often starts to slip: the first evening you leave at six and find the building still standing the next morning. In excess, it’s working more so you never have to look.

“Should I quit?” People usually ask it tired, hoping for permission to go or a good reason to stay. I understand. But whether you can afford a change is a money decision, which I don’t read on ([what I don’t read, and why](/disclaimers/#refusals)), and a card that said “leave” would become one more chain: a voice outside you, deciding, when on this card nobody holds the other end. A kinder question: “What is this job giving me that I’m afraid of losing, and where else could I find it?” A budget and an honest adviser will help with the practical side; if the hours cost you sleep or health, see a doctor first.

**You don’t have to walk out to lift the chain.** This week, name yours in one word — money, status, fear — and loosen one link: one evening without email.

## A reading, as an example {#example}

Now imagine you’re the reader: the Devil comes out beside the [Six of Cups](/library/tarot-cards/six-of-cups/).

Two sixes: 15 comes back to 6, like the Six of Cups — one harmony comfortable, one chained. Earth and Water: what holds and what you feel. The Devil’s black beside the warm yellow of the Six of Cups: something heavy next to something sweet and old, from childhood.

The sentence you’d say out loud: “Something you started long ago because it kept you safe — a habit, a role in your family — still holds you, and it’s looser than you think.”

Then check it. Ask whether that speaks to something they do without deciding to, and let them name it. If they say no, note a miss; there’s no shame in it.
