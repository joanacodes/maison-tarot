In the 20th century, tarot left the secret societies. In 1909 a London publisher put the Golden Dawn’s ideas into a deck anyone could buy; the Order’s papers reached print in the 1910s and 1930s; the Tarot de Marseille got its standard French edition in 1930; and artists took the cards up as images. By the end of the century, tarot was something you could learn alone, from a shop.

## 1909: a deck for everyone, from a secret society {#rws}

In 1909, the occultist [Arthur Edward Waite](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/) (1857–1942) commissioned the artist [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/) (1878–1951) to design a pack of 78 tarot cards. The London publisher William Rider & Son brought it out in December 1909 with Waite’s small companion book, *The Key to the Tarot*; the illustrated *Pictorial Key to the Tarot* came in 1910. Both had been members of the Golden Dawn, and its correspondences run through the deck: [Strength is VIII, Justice XI](/library/tarot-history/golden-dawn-tarot/).

The Victoria and Albert Museum, which holds cards from the deck, describes the division of work. Waite gave Smith strict instructions for the major arcana. The minor arcana were entirely her design.

**The scenes on the minor cards, which let you read the deck without memorising it, are Pamela Colman Smith’s.** In the Tarot de Marseille, the minors are pips: cups, coins, swords or batons arranged by number. Hers show people: a figure walking away from eight cups, three women raising theirs, a man carrying ten heavy staves. I teach with this deck because of those pictures.

The deck still goes by several names. Many readers now say Rider-Waite-Smith, or Smith-Waite, to give the artist her due; “Rider” is the publisher. [Why it became the standard](/blog/rider-waite-smith-tarot/) has its own article.

## The secrets go into print {#print}

The Golden Dawn’s tarot teaching didn’t stay in the lodge for long. Aleister Crowley put a version of it in his journal, *The Equinox*, in 1912, and Israel Regardie’s four volumes of the Order’s papers came out in Chicago between 1937 and 1940.

[Aleister Crowley](/library/tarot-figures/aleister-crowley/) went on to design his own deck, the Thoth tarot, painted by Frieda Harris. It keeps the Golden Dawn’s astrology and decans and changes the look of everything else. [Thoth or Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/thoth-vs-rider-waite/) compares the two for a reader.

The Thoth deck itself waited: Harris painted it between 1938 and 1943, and it was first printed as cards only after both of them had died, in an edition usually dated 1969.

## The 1960s and 70s: tarot reaches the bookshop {#boom}

In New York, Eden Gray ran a bookshop and small publishing house, Inspiration House, which brought out her first book, *The Tarot Revealed*, in 1960. Her *Complete Guide to the Tarot* followed in 1970, and its closing chapter gave teachers a phrase they still use: the Fool’s Journey, the 22 major arcana read as the stages of one trip. Her books were built on the Rider-Waite-Smith images, so a whole generation of new readers learned on that deck.

**The deck everyone reads today owes its shelf space to a 1960s bookseller and a 1970s card publisher as much as to 1909.**

Around the same time, U.S. Games Systems, founded by Stuart Kaplan in 1968, acquired the rights to reprint the Rider-Waite deck (sources give 1970 or 1971), and its editions have kept it in shops ever since.

## France: the Marseille gets a standard edition {#france}

In France, the century opened with Papus and Wirth’s occult tarot of 1889 already in circulation. The standard edition came in 1930, when Paul Marteau of the Paris card maker Grimaud brought out *L’Ancien Tarot de Marseille*, drawn from 18th-century decks; its title fixed the pattern’s name. [The Tarot de Marseille page](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/) tells where that pattern came from.

## Tarot in the museum and the studio {#art}

The cards also became images for artists. This year, the Morgan Library & Museum in New York built the second half of its exhibition *Tarot! Renaissance Symbols, Modern Visions* around that story, taking the 1909 deck as its starting point. It followed tarot through the work of André Breton, Leonora Carrington, Remedios Varo, Niki de Saint Phalle, Betye Saar and Kerstin Brätsch, and commissioned a new work from Chris Ofili.

That says something about where tarot ended up. A century after Waite and Smith, the cards were being hung on museum walls as well as laid out on kitchen tables.

## What it means for you {#reading}

The deck in your hands carries a whole century of this history: a secret society’s correspondences, a publisher’s bet, and an artist who drew people where others drew pips. You don’t need any of it to read. But knowing it helps you see that the meanings in your little booklet were written by people, in the open, and can be tested.

If you’re starting out, start where Smith’s pictures make it possible: look at what the people on the card are doing before you look up a meaning. [The complete path](/learn-tarot/) begins there.
