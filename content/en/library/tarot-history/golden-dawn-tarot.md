Most of the correspondences in modern tarot books — the Fool on Aleph, Strength as card VIII, a sign of the zodiac for twelve of the trumps, a decan for each numbered minor card — come from one small London society founded in 1888. Knowing that helps you tell which “rules” are history and which are choices someone made.

## A small society in London, 1888 {#order}

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn opened its first temple, Isis-Urania, in London in 1888. Its three founders, William Wynn Westcott, Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers and William Robert Woodman, were Freemasons and members of an English Rosicrucian society. By the end of that year there were temples in London, Weston-super-Mare and Bradford.

The Order said its teaching came from a set of cipher manuscripts and from a charter granted by a German adept, Anna Sprengel. Historians doubt the Sprengel letters: Westcott most likely invented that story to give the Order a respectable pedigree. The rituals, however, were real, and so was the work done on the cards.

Several people who matter to readers today passed through it: [Arthur Edward Waite](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/), [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/) and [Aleister Crowley](/library/tarot-figures/aleister-crowley/).

## Book T, a secret that got printed {#book-t}

The Order’s tarot teaching was meant for members only. It was gathered in a document known as Book T: what each card shows, what it is attributed to, how to lay a reading. Members were bound by oaths of secrecy.

Secrets of that kind rarely last. In 1912, Crowley printed a version as “A Description of the Cards of the Tarot” in his journal, *The Equinox*, with the decans, the qualities of the court cards and a method of divination. Between 1937 and 1940, Israel Regardie published the Order’s rituals and teachings in four volumes in Chicago, breaking his own oath. **From then on, anyone with a library card could read what had been a secret teaching.**

## The correspondences {#correspondences}

The Golden Dawn’s scheme rests on one rule: put the Fool first, as card 0, give it Aleph, and let every trump take the next Hebrew letter. Each letter already carried an element, a planet or a sign in the Jewish tradition of *Sefer Yetzirah*; the card inherits it. The minors are placed on the [Tree of Life](/library/kabbalah/tree-of-life/) by number.

| Cards | What the Golden Dawn gave them |
| --- | --- |
| The 22 trumps | The 22 Hebrew letters, starting with the Fool on Aleph |
| 3 trumps (the “mother” letters) | An element; the Fool, for instance, is Air |
| 7 trumps (the “double” letters) | A planet: the Magician is Mercury, the High Priestess the Moon |
| 12 trumps (the “simple” letters) | A sign: the Emperor is Aries, Strength is Leo |
| Aces to Tens | The ten spheres of the Tree of Life, Ace to Keter, Ten to Malkhut |
| Twos to Tens | The 36 decans of the zodiac, ten degrees each |
| The four suits | The four elements: Wands Fire, Cups Water, Swords Air, Pentacles Earth |
| The court cards | Places on the Tree, and elemental sub-qualities |

The famous swap comes out of that rule. Counting from the Fool, card VIII landed on Teth, the letter of Leo, and card XI on Lamed, the letter of Libra. But in the older order, card VIII was Justice and card XI Strength. The lion belongs with Leo and the scales with Libra, so the Order exchanged them. The cipher manuscripts already suggested it.

## What the deck makers did with it {#decks}

The Rider-Waite-Smith of 1909 follows the Golden Dawn: [Strength](/library/tarot-cards/strength/) is VIII, Justice XI, and the astrology on this site’s card pages comes from the same scheme. Waite and Smith had been members; when the Order split, Smith followed Waite into the branch he led. [The 20th-century page](/library/tarot-history/tarot-in-the-20th-century/) tells what happened to their deck.

Crowley’s Thoth tarot, painted by [Frieda Harris](/library/tarot-figures/frieda-harris/), keeps the Order’s zodiac attributions and its decans, and the Golden Dawn’s names for the court cards. Only three modern planets on this site’s card pages — Uranus, Neptune and Pluto, for the Fool, the Hanged Man and Judgement — are later additions; the Order gave those three cards elements.

## How I use it {#reading}

I treat the Golden Dawn’s correspondences as one more lens, not as the truth of the cards. They’re useful when you read older books, when you read the Thoth deck, or when [astrology](/library/astrology/) speaks to you. They are optional when you start.

Say someone draws the Emperor next to the Four of Wands. Without any system, you already see red, a throne, a structure, a celebration. The Golden Dawn adds Aries to the Emperor and Venus in Aries to the Four of Wands: two fiery signs, a start that wants to last. Useful, if it helps. Leave it aside if it doesn’t.
