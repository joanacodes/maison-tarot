Tarot became a fortune-telling tool in France in the 1780s. People had read ordinary playing cards before that, and an Italian sheet suggests a few had read tarot too, but that decade gave tarot an invented ancient past, a published method and a deck designed for reading. Every reader today inherits all three.

## Before 1781: fortunes from ordinary cards {#before}

Fortune-telling with ordinary cards came before fortune-telling with tarot. One pack people read was the 32-card pack used for piquet: the man who would sign himself Etteilla published a book on reading it in 1770. Michael Dummett, the historian of the game, concluded that tarot was only taken up for cartomancy in the 1780s, once fortune-telling with regular cards had been around for a couple of decades.

There is one older trace. The Italian researcher Franco Pratesi found a single sheet from Bologna that gives short divinatory keywords for 35 cards of the local tarot pack. It is usually dated to around the middle of the 18th century, and its exact date is debated. It shows people in Bologna attaching meanings to some cards. It doesn’t show a system, a theory or a book.

## 1781: Court de Gébelin and the Egyptian book {#gebelin}

[Antoine Court de Gébelin](/library/tarot-figures/court-de-gebelin/) was a Paris scholar engaged in a vast work, *Le Monde primitif, analysé et comparé avec le monde moderne* — the primitive world, compared with the modern one. In volume 8, published in 1781, he turned to tarot. His claim: it wasn’t merely a card game but a relic of ancient Egypt, its pictures carrying Egyptian theology. He read the Popess, our [High Priestess](/library/tarot-cards/the-high-priestess/), as Isis.

The same volume carries a second essay, signed “M. le C. de M.***”, now identified as Louis-Raphaël-Lucrèce de Fayolle, Comte de Mellet. He linked the trumps to the letters of the Hebrew alphabet and described a way of telling fortunes with the cards. Historians of tarot judge his essay the more influential of the two.

**There was no evidence for the Egyptian story in 1781, and none has turned up since.** What there is, in archives and museums, is a trail of Italian card games starting in the 1440s. Court de Gébelin’s idea is still worth knowing, because much of the “ancient mystery” you’ll meet in tarot books flows from those pages.

## Etteilla: the first to make a living from it {#etteilla}

Jean-Baptiste Alliette (1738–1791) wrote under his surname read backwards: [Etteilla](/library/tarot-figures/etteilla/). After his piquet book of 1770, he took up Court de Gébelin’s theory and ran with it. Between 1783 and 1785 he published his method for tarot in four booklets, *Manière de se récréer avec le jeu de cartes nommées tarots*. In 1788 he founded a society of “interpreters of the Book of Thoth”.

Then he did something new: he designed a tarot deck for reading, not for play. The engravings were commissioned in 1788 and the deck appeared around 1789. You will sometimes see it dated 1785; that is the year of his last booklet, and the cards came a few years after.

His figure page tells his life. For this period, the point is that tarot now had a professional reader, a school of sorts, and a deck that only made sense on a reading table.

## What changed, and what didn’t {#what-changed}

The game didn’t disappear; it went on being played. What changed was that a second life opened beside the first, in a small Parisian circle. Tarot’s association with the occult only became widespread in the 19th century, when [the occultists of the next generation](/library/tarot-history/occult-tarot-19th-century/) took Court de Gébelin’s ideas much further.

Two myths take root in this decade or shortly after it. The Egyptian one starts here. The Romani one comes later, in the 19th century; [the first page of this history](/library/tarot-history/playing-cards-arrive-in-europe/) shows why the dates rule it out.

## What I keep from it {#reading}

Every meaning in a little guidebook has an author and a date. Etteilla’s are from the 1780s, Waite’s from 1910, and the ones in my notebook from the last few years. That’s no reason to throw any of them away. It’s a reason to treat them as proposals, not as revelations.

*Don’t take my words as ultimate truth. Test it and experience it for yourself.* That applies to Court de Gébelin as much as to me. [The method](/method/) explains how I count what held and what missed, so the meanings you keep are the ones that earned their place.
