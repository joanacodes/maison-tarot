Tarot began as a game. In the courts of northern Italy, in the first half of the 15th century, someone added a fifth row of picture cards to the four-suit pack, cards that beat all the others. The game was called *trionfi*, triumphs. The name *tarocchi* arrives around 1500, and fortune-telling nearly three centuries after that.

## Trionfi: a game with trumps {#trionfi}

The earliest references to the game date from the 1440s and 1450s. On 16 September 1440, a Florentine named Giusto Giusti noted in his diary that he had given Sigismondo Malatesta a pair of *naibi a trionfi*, made in Florence and painted with his coat of arms, for four and a half ducats. In February 1442, the account books of the Este court in Ferrara record *carte da trionfi*. The first records come from the courts and cities of northern and central Italy: Florence, Ferrara, Milan.

The game was a trick-taking game, like whist or bridge: each player lays a card, the strongest takes the trick. A triumph beats any card of the four suits, and our English word “trump” comes from the Italian *trionfo*. Michael Dummett, whose 1980 book *The Game of Tarot* founded the serious history of the subject, argued that tarot was invented precisely to play with that new idea.

**The major arcana people now read for their meaning were, first, the trumps of a card game.**

There is a curious forerunner. Early in the century, Filippo Maria Visconti, Duke of Milan, had his secretary and astrologer, Marziano da Tortona, devise a game with sixteen figures of ancient gods and heroes and four suits of birds; the painter Michelino da Besozzo made the cards. The deck is lost, and strictly speaking it wasn’t a tarot. But it already added allegorical cards to a pack, which is the move tarot made.

## The painted decks of Milan {#milan}

Three luxury decks survive from the middle of the 15th century, all attributed to the workshop of Bonifacio Bembo, a painter at the Milanese court. They were painted by hand, with a great deal of gold.

The deck called Visconti di Modrone, or Cary-Yale, is at the Beinecke Library at Yale. The collector Melbert B. Cary acquired it in 1947; Yale dates it to about 1440–45, and 67 cards survive. Recent research at Yale suggests it may be the work of Bonifacio’s older brother, Andrea.

The Visconti-Sforza deck is the most complete of all, and it is linked to Francesco Sforza and his wife, Bianca Maria Visconti. Its cards are now in three places: 35 at the Morgan Library & Museum in New York, bought by J. Pierpont Morgan in 1911; 26 at the Accademia Carrara in Bergamo; and 13 with the Colleoni family, also in Bergamo. That makes 74 of the original 78. The missing four are the Three of Swords, the Knight of Coins, [the Devil](/library/tarot-cards/the-devil/) and [the Tower](/library/tarot-cards/the-tower/). The trumps and the court cards are painted on gold; the numbered cards sit on a white ground. A few cards were redone later by another hand.

The Morgan catalogues two of its trumps as *Love* and *Time*; you know them as the Lovers and the Hermit. The figures were there; the meanings were not yet fixed.

This year the Morgan devoted the first half of its tarot exhibition, which closed on 4 October 2026, to these three Milanese decks.

## Not every “tarocchi” is a tarot {#mantegna}

One correction a museum makes for us. The fifty engravings known as the “Mantegna tarocchi”, or E-series, are not a tarot pack. The Metropolitan Museum explains that they illustrate the breadth of Renaissance knowledge — Geometry and Poetry are among them — and that the name comes from an early mistake. If you see one in a book about tarot origins, it is a cousin at most.

## From trionfi to tarocchi {#tarocchi}

For the whole 15th century, the records say *trionfi*. The new name appears in the first years of the 16th: a ledger of the Ferrara court in 1505 records packs called *tarochi*, and a document from Avignon the same year mentions *taraux*, which shows the game had already crossed into France. Where the word itself comes from is still debated.

The game kept spreading, in Italy and beyond the Alps, printed from woodblocks for ordinary players rather than painted for dukes. That is where [the Tarot de Marseille](/library/tarot-history/tarot-de-marseille-history/) begins.

## What it changes for a reader {#reading}

I read with the 1909 Rider-Waite-Smith, drawn more than four and a half centuries after these cards. The figures travelled a long way to reach it, and their meanings were added along the road.

So when a book says a card has meant something “since the Middle Ages”, remember that its first owners used it to win tricks. That doesn’t make reading less useful. It makes it yours: the meaning is built by readers, in the open, and you are allowed to test it. The [complete path](/learn-tarot/) shows how I read a card from its colour, number and element instead of an inherited list. If the game itself tempts you, [the first page of this history](/library/tarot-history/playing-cards-arrive-in-europe/) tells where the four suits came from.
