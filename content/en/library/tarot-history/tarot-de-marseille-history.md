The Tarot de Marseille is a pattern: a way of drawing the 78 cards that French card makers copied from one another and printed from woodblocks in the 17th and 18th centuries. The oldest example we know comes from Paris. Marseille gave the pattern its name, and that happened much later.

## From Milan to France {#origins}

Tarot crossed the Alps early. In 1505, a document in Avignon already mentions *taraux*. Michael Dummett, the historian of the game, traced the French pattern back to models from Milan, the city of the painted decks described in [the page on the Italian tarocchi](/library/tarot-history/italian-tarocchi/).

What changed on the way was the object. The Milanese decks were painted by hand for dukes. The French cards were woodcuts, coloured through stencils, made in batches by professional card makers for people who played. The figures stayed recognisable; the gold went.

## Jean Noblet, Paris, around 1650 {#noblet}

The oldest known Tarot de Marseille was made by Jean Noblet, a card maker in the Faubourg Saint-Germain in Paris. The Bibliothèque nationale de France, which holds the only known copy, dates it to about 1659; you will often see “around 1650”. It is a woodcut coloured by stencil, and 73 of its 78 cards survive: the Six to the Ten of Swords are missing. Noblet signed it. His name and address are on the Two of Cups, and his initials, I. N., on the shield of the Chariot.

**The oldest Tarot de Marseille we know was printed in Paris, not in Marseille.** The BnF notes, too, that Noblet made it at a time when the game was declining in the capital. The catalogue of the BnF’s 1984 tarot exhibition adds a detail: the back of Noblet’s cards matches one used by Viéville, another Paris card maker of those years.

## Dodal in Lyon, Conver in Marseille {#dodal-conver}

In the early 18th century, Jean Dodal printed the pattern in Lyon; his deck is usually dated to around 1701. Around 1760, Nicolas Conver printed it in Marseille, and his version became a model for later editions. Card historians distinguish two slightly different variants of the pattern, which Thierry Depaulis calls Type I and Type II.

What they share is what you see when you lay a Marseille deck next to your Rider-Waite-Smith. The 22 trumps carry Roman numerals and French titles: the Magician is *le Bateleur*, the High Priestess *la Papesse*. Card VIII is Justice and card XI is Strength, the older order that the Golden Dawn later swapped (see [Strength](/library/tarot-cards/strength/) and its story). In most Marseille decks, card XIII carries no title, and French readers call it *l’Arcane sans nom*, and [the nameless thirteenth card](/blog/death-card-no-name/) gets an article to itself.

The minor cards have no scenes. A Five of Cups is five cups arranged in a pattern. You read the number and the suit, and that is all the card gives you.

## Who named it {#name}

The name is a 19th-century label: the International Playing-Card Society notes that scholars and occultists coined it in that century. The occultists took the pattern as their model: the 22 major arcana that [Oswald Wirth](/library/tarot-figures/oswald-wirth/) drew in 1889, published with Papus’s book on tarot, follow it closely. [The page on occult tarot](/library/tarot-history/occult-tarot-19th-century/) tells that story.

The 20th century made the name stick. In 1930, Paul Marteau, who ran the Paris card maker Grimaud, published *L’Ancien Tarot de Marseille*. Despite the word *ancien*, it is a 20th-century edition based on 18th-century models.

A calm correction, then. The Tarot de Marseille is not the “original” tarot, nor a medieval secret book: it is an early modern French pattern, descended from an Italian game, whose oldest surviving example is Parisian.

## What it changes if you read the Rider-Waite-Smith {#reading}

Quite a lot, in a good way. With a Marseille deck, the minor cards teach you to read through numbers, because there is nothing else on them. That is close to how I teach the minors: before any scene, the [number from one to ten](/library/numerology/) tells you the step, and the suit tells you the field.

So if you ever pick one up, don’t look for the Rider-Waite-Smith scenes. Count, name the suit, and see what you already know. [Marseille or Rider-Waite-Smith](/blog/marseille-vs-rider-waite/) compares the two decks card by card, for readers who hesitate.
