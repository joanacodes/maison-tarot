Playing cards enter European records in the late 1370s, and they enter as a novelty. Everything points to the Islamic world, and more precisely to Mamluk Egypt, as the place they came from. They brought four suits with them. They didn’t bring tarot: the trump cards are an Italian addition, some sixty years later.

## The first records, 1377 {#first-records}

In March 1377, the government of Florence passed a ban on a card game it called *naibbe*. Franco Pratesi, who has spent decades in the Florentine archives, went through the city’s police records for the following summer: alongside about a hundred arrests for a dice game, *zara*, he found a dozen or so people caught playing *naibi*. The game was already being played, and already being fined.

The same year, a Dominican friar wrote the oldest detailed description of cards in Europe. He is known as Johannes of Rheinfelden, though his treatise says he was born in Freiburg. His Latin text, the *Ludus cartularum moralisatus*, uses the game as a mirror of society, the way an older friar, Jacobus de Cessolis, had used chess. By the friar’s own account, the game had reached his country that very year. He describes packs with kings and queens, others with officers called marshals, and packs with four, five or even six suits: the Europeans were still experimenting.

Then the mentions multiply. The International Playing-Card Society (IPCS) counts cards reliably reported by 1380 in places as far apart as Florence, Basel, Regensburg, Brabant, Paris and Barcelona. A ban in Bern dated 1367 is sometimes quoted as earlier, but its text survives in a copy made around 1398, and historians such as Hellmut Rosenfeld have argued the line on cards was added then.

**The shape of the evidence is the point: nothing, then a burst of records within a few years, several of them describing the game as new.** A chronicle of Viterbo, for instance, says a game called *nayb* was brought there in 1379 by a “Saracen”.

## Where they came from: the Mamluk cards {#mamluk}

The best clue is in Istanbul. In the Topkapı Palace collection, the scholar Leo Aryeh Mayer identified an almost complete pack of hand-painted cards from Mamluk Egypt. This pack is younger than the first European records — the IPCS places it in the 15th century, not before 1400 — but it shows the kind of game that had been travelling.

Its structure will look familiar. Four suits — coins, cups, swords and polo sticks — each with ten numbered cards and three court cards: a king and two deputies. The courts carry written labels, not figures. The Arabic word for deputy, *nā’ib*, is the most likely source of the Italian *naibi* and the Spanish *naipes*, still the ordinary Spanish word for playing cards.

Polo meant nothing to most Europeans, so the polo sticks became staves and cudgels. Look at your [Ace of Wands](/library/tarot-cards/ace-of-wands/): that suit began as a polo stick. Cups and swords kept their objects; the coins are the Pentacles of the Rider-Waite-Smith.

An honest limit: the case rests on the suits, the name and the timing, and historians still argue over details, including the date of Mayer’s pack. The broad route — from the Islamic Mediterranean to the ports of southern Europe, in the 14th century — is what most of them accept.

## What the first European cards were not {#not-tarot}

These packs had no trumps. No Fool, no Magician, no Tower: four suits, numbered cards and courts, roughly the structure of the minor arcana and of ordinary playing cards today. The picture cards that became the major arcana were added in northern Italy in the 15th century; that is the next chapter, [the Italian tarocchi](/library/tarot-history/italian-tarocchi/).

Nor were they used to tell fortunes. Every early record is about play and money: bans, arrests, a friar’s moral lesson. Reading the cards comes much later, and [the 18th-century turn](/library/tarot-history/from-game-to-divination/) tells how.

## Two myths the dates settle {#myths}

The first says tarot came from ancient Egypt. The Mamluk connection doesn’t rescue it: the Mamluk sultanate was a medieval state, its cards carried no trumps, and the Egyptian theory was put forward in Paris in 1781 by [Antoine Court de Gébelin](/library/tarot-figures/court-de-gebelin/), about four centuries after the cards arrived.

The second says the Romani people brought tarot to Europe. The dates rule it out. Cards are in Florence and on the Rhine in the 1370s; the first Romani groups appear in the records of western European cities in the 1410s and 1420s, and in Paris in 1427. The idea belongs to [19th-century French occultists](/library/tarot-history/occult-tarot-19th-century/) — Papus’s 1889 book is even called *Le Tarot des Bohémiens* — and it has followed Romani people ever since. It’s worth setting down gently, and for good.

## What it changes when you read {#reading}

I find this history useful at the table. The four suits are older than every meaning written on them: before they were Fire, Water, Air and Earth, they were coins, cups, swords and polo sticks. Some historians even read them as the emblems of four officers at a sultan’s court, though that remains a proposal.

So when you learn [the four elements](/library/four-elements/), you’re laying a later lens over a very old structure. It works, and I teach it, but it isn’t the cards’ first language. You can try it with the Wands: ask what the suit says as Fire, then what it said as a polo stick in a game of skill. Both tell you something about drive.
