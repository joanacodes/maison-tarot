## What a symbol is in tarot {#what}

A symbol is a drawn detail that carries more than itself. A dog is a dog, but on a card it’s also instinct. A river is water, but it’s also time moving on. A mountain is rock, and also the effort ahead. **On a tarot card, nothing is drawn by accident: every detail is a word you can read.**

Take the Fool. A young man, a cliff, a white sun, a small dog at his heels. You don’t need to know what the card “means” to see someone stepping into the unknown in full daylight, while the only one looking at the ground is [the dog](/library/tarot-symbols/dog/).

## How to read one {#how}

Look before you interpret. Name what you see first: where the detail sits, what colour it is, what it’s doing. Then ask what it does on this card, here, today — the same moon reads differently above a blindfolded woman and above a man walking away.

Then cross it with the other lenses. A symbol tells you what is present; the [colour](/library/tarot-colours/) gives the tone, the number the step in the cycle, the element the part of life. One symbol never carries a whole reading on its own, and it doesn’t have to.

## Why the 1909 deck is full of them {#why}

Most older decks, the Tarot de Marseille among them, showed the minor cards as patterns of suit signs: five cups, seven swords, no scene. Then a London deck changed that in 1909: [Pamela Colman Smith](/library/tarot-figures/pamela-colman-smith/), working with [Arthur Edward Waite](/library/tarot-figures/arthur-edward-waite/), gave a scene to all 78 cards. Suddenly the Five of Cups had a river and a bridge, the Ten of Pentacles had two dogs, the Two of Swords had a moon.

That’s why this deck is so readable for beginners: you can read what you see.
